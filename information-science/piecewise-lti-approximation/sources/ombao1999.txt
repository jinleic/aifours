   Abstract. We propose a new method, the \Auto-SLEX" method,
for analyzing bivariate non-stationary processes. The Auto-SLEX method
is a procedure that automatically segments the time series into approxi-
mately stationary blocks and automatically estimates the time-varying spec-
tra and coherence. It is based on the SLEX (Smooth Localized Com-
plex EXponential) transform which forms a library of orthogonal trans-
forms. The SLEX transform is built on the SLEX basis functions which are
complex-valued and are simultaneously localized in time and frequency. We
show that the SLEX periodograms are consistent, report simulation results
and apply the method to an electroencephalogram (EEG) data set recorded
during an epileptic seizure.

1 Introduction
Electroencephalograms (EEGs) or brain waves are brain electrical potentials recorded by
electrodes placed on the scalp of subjects. Examples of EEGs recorded at the left temporal
lobe and the left parietal lobe during an epileptic seizure are given in Figure 1. Epileptic
seizure EEGs can be modeled as non-stationary processes. As a patient progresses from
pre-ictal to post-ictal state, changes in the electrical activity of the brain are manifested
in changes in the estimated spectral power. In addition to estimating the power spectra,
neurologists are interested in the estimate of coherence between EEGs recorded at di erent
channels. An estimate of coherence is important because it gives an indication on how a
seizure spreads from a local area to neighboring regions.
    Most methods of analyzing EEGs are built on the premise that EEGs can be modeled
approximately by piecewise stationary processes. Hence, the time-varying spectrum can be
approximated by the piecewise constant spectra of the stationary processes. The standard
approach of estimating the time-varying spectrum is by the windowed Fourier transform
(Cohen, 1989; Williams, 1997). This method rst divides the time series into blocks of equal
sizes and then computes the windowed Fourier transform at each of the blocks. A drawback
to this methodology is that the length of the blocks is chosen subjectively. Furthermore, in
many applications, it may not be reasonable to require that all blocks or windows should
be of equal lengths.

                                             1
    Adak (1998) proposed a methodology that allows a data-dependent choice of the window
lengths. The lengths are adapted to the rate of change of the spectrum, i.e., bigger windows
are generated for longer periods of stationarity. Adak's methodology divides the time series
in a dyadic manner, up to some pre-speci ed level J , into possibly overlapping blocks and
then computes the windowed Fourier transform at each of the blocks. At a particular level
j = 0; 1;    ; J , the time series is divided into 2j blocks. Adjacent blocks at every level
are combined together to form one stationary block if their estimated spectra are close
according to some distance measure. Otherwise, they are left as distinct blocks.
    Donoho, Mallat and von Sachs [DMvS] (1998) introduced a model of a locally stationary
process and then developed a procedure for obtaining an adaptively-smoothed and consis-
tent estimator of the time-varying covariance under this model. Intuitively, a process is
said to be locally stationary if one can form an interval around each time point so that
the process is approximately stationary at that interval. Dahlhaus (1997) gives a slightly
di erent de nition of this local stationarity that we will use in our approach as a model to
validate the properties of our estimation procedure from the asymptotic point of view of
consistency.
    The DMvS method uses the cosine packet transform (CPT) that forms a library (col-
lection) of orthonormal transforms. Cosine packets are like windowed cosine vectors. They
are localized in time and frequency. The coecients are rst computed by taking the inner
product between the time series and the cosine packets. These coecients are arranged
in a cosine packet table and then squared to form the cosine packet periodograms. The
DMvS method automatically segments the time series by applying the Best Basis Algo-
rithm (BBA) of Coifman and Wickerhauser (1991) on the raw periodograms. At each of
the blocks chosen by BBA, wavelet-denoised estimators for the log-spectrum are obtained
and then exponentiated to form the spectral estimate. The inverse CPT is then applied on
the spectral estimate to obtain the estimate of the time-varying covariance.
    The primary limitation of the Adak method is that it is based on a non-orthogonal
transform. As a consequence, the theory of consistency of the method is not tractable
(Adak, personal communication, 1997). The Adak method, similar to the DMvS method
and our proposed method, employs a dyadic segmentation of the time series data set.
Adak (1998), however, failed to demonstrate that the e ect of the dyadic segmentation
is asymptotically negligible. In addition, the method is for univariate processes only and
hence cannot give estimates of the time lag and coherence between between components of
a multivariate time series.
    DMvS (1998) provide a consistent estimator of the time-varying covariance under the
DMvS model of a locally stationary process. A frequency domain approach giving an
estimate of the time-dependent spectrum, however, is more directly applicable to the study
of epileptic seizure EEGs. The primary limitation of the DMvS method is that it uses the
CPT, which is a real-valued transform. Hence, it does not have a phase and is not able
to model lag between pairs of components in a multivariate time series. Moreover, it is
developed only for univariate processes and hence cannot give estimates of coherence.
    In this paper, we propose a methodology, which we call the Auto-SLEX method, that
overcomes the limitations of the existing methods. The Auto-SLEX method is a statistical
procedure that (i.) automatically segments the bivariate time series into approximately
                                              2
stationary blocks and (ii.) automatically chooses the smoothing parameter for the esti-
mation of time-varying spectra and coherence. The Auto-SLEX method is based on the
SLEX (Smooth Localized Complex EXponential) transform. The SLEX basis vectors are
related to the Fourier basis vectors. They are obtained by applying two special windows
to the Fourier vectors. They are simultaneously localized in time and frequency and hence
are able to represent non-stationary time series. The SLEX transform also forms a li-
brary of orthonormal transforms. Orthonormal transforms are mathematically elegant and
they preserve the energy of the time series. Furthermore, they allow for the theory to be
tractable (DMvS, 1998). In addition, the SLEX transform is complex-valued. It has a
phase which can model the lag between the components of the bivariate time series. The
Auto-SLEX method can give estimates of coherence because it is designed for multivariate
non-stationary time series. Although the Auto-SLEX procedure presented in this paper is
for bivariate processes only, its extension to the multivariate setting is natural and straight-
forward. Finally, the Auto-SLEX method yields results that are easy to interpret because
they parallel existing spectral methods for analyzing stationary time series.
    The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section 2, we state the Dahlhaus
model for locally stationary processes and then discuss apparent intuitive approximations
by piecewise stationary and blended stationary processes. The SLEX transform is described
in Section 3 and the Auto-SLEX method is discussed in Section 4. In Section 5, theoretical
results are presented. In Sections 6 and 7 we show simulation results and apply the method
to a bivariate EEG data set before we nally conclude. Proofs are deferred to an appendix.

3 The SLEX Transform
3.1 The SLEX basis vectors
Fourier vectors are ideal at representing stationary time series because they are perfectly
localized in frequency and the spectral properties of stationary processes do not change
with time. Fourier vectors, however, do not adequately represent processes whose spectral
properties evolve with time. In order to alleviate the time localization problem, smooth
compactly supported windows have been applied to the Fourier vectors (Daubechies, 1992).
Windowed Fourier vectors, however, are generally no longer orthogonal. In fact, the Balian-
Low theorem says that there does not exist a smooth window such that the windowed
Fourier basis vectors are both (i.) orthogonal and (ii.) localized in time and frequency
(Wickerhauser, 1994). The SLEX basis vectors, on the other hand, are simultaneously
orthogonal and localized in time and frequency. They evade the Balian-Low obstruction
because they are constructed by applying two, rather than just one, special smooth windows
to the complex exponentials.

4 The Auto-SLEX Method
The Auto-SLEX Method is an automatic statistical procedure that simultaneously segments
the time series and gives the kernel-smoothed time-varying estimate of the spectra and
coherence. Given a non-stationary bivariate time series (Xt; Yt ), we aim to estimate the
time-varying spectra fXX and fY Y of thep univariate components, the cross-spectrum fXY =
fY X , and the coherence RXY = jfXY j= fXX fY Y .
    The method consists of two general steps: (1.) automatic smoothing and (2.) automatic
segmentation and estimation. The periodograms are rst smoothed over frequency using
a kernel smoother with bandwidth automatically selected by Generalized Cross Validation
(GCV). Then the BBA is applied to the smooth periodograms to obtain the segmentation
and the estimates of the time-varying spectra and coherence.

Remarks:
(1.) In order to make meaningful coherence estimates, it is necessary that only one seg-
mentation should be made for the multivariate time series. In the procedure, we combined
the cost each of the components of the bivariate time series to obtain a single segmentation.
(2.) The cost function (Equation 14) includes the term pMj . This penalty term safe-
guards the procedure from obtaining the best segmentation with too many or too few blocks.
A small value of leads to a procedure that tends to select a segmentation with too many
blocks. Although this is positive from the point of view that there will be less bias due
to non-stationarity, this implies having less observations within each block and hence vari-
ances of the estimates will be in ated. A large value of leads to a procedure that will
tend to select a segmentaion with very few blocks. Although variance of the estimates is
reduced, having too few blocks may lead to bias due to non-stationarity (i.e, error due to
not splitting a non-stationary block). The penalty parameter can either be approximated
or computed via a data-driven procedure. In this paper, we set = 1, which is motivated
by the thresholds set by Gao (1997). Excellent empirical results are obtained using = 1.
Relationship of the Auto-SLEX method to the Adak and DMvS methods
    A common thread that ties the three methods is the use of transforms that divide the
time series in a dyadic manner. The transforms used, however, are di erent. Adak uses the
non-orthogonal windowed Fourier transform and DMvS uses the real-valued cosine packet
transform. The Auto-SLEX method uses the SLEX transform that is complex-valued and
forms a library of orthonormal transforms.
    Adak (1998), suggested using two types of cost functions: (1.) a distance measure
between spectral estimates; (2.) a distance measure plus a complexity penalty term. Adak's
method (2) is supposed to safeguard against choosing a segmentation that divides the time
series into many blocks. It incorporates a data-driven procedure for selecting the best
penalty parameter. The Auto-SLEX method also uses a cost function that includes a
complexity penalty for too many blocks. Simulation results in Section 6 showed that the
present implementation of the Auto-SLEX method, where the complexity penalty parameter
is set to = 1, worked very well and performed better than the Adak method. There is also
a di erence in the underlying principles for segmentation between the Adak and the Auto-
SLEX methods. The Adak method searches for the best segmentation based on some well
de ned criterion, i.e., the cost computed for a parent block is a distance measure between
the spectra of children blocks. The Auto-SLEX method, on the other hand, uses the BBA
which searches for the basis that best represents the time series based on some well de ned
cost function. The best segmentation is implied in the best basis selected.
    DMvS showed that consistent estimates for the time-varying covariances can be obtained
by applying BBA using the squared cost function (the cost for a block is the sum of the
squared periodograms in that block). DMvS also suggested using the log-energy cost with
complexity penalty and provided theoretical justi cation. In addition to the theoretical
motivation in DMvS, we implemented the log-energy cost because empirical results indicate
that it is able to give a higher rate of correct segmentation than the squared cost.
    Both the Adak method and the DMvS method (with log-energy as cost function) apply
a segmentation procedure using raw periodograms. The Auto-SLEX procedure is more
                                             13
general because it includes raw periodograms (smoothing parameter set to zero) as a possible
output of the automatic smoothing step. There is also empirical evidence that suggests BBA
is able to give the correct segmentation at a higher rate when applied to smoothed SLEX
rather than raw SLEX periodograms.
    Finally, DMvS uses wavelet-denoising while the Auto-SLEX uses kernel-smoothing. Lin-
ear smoothers ensure that the estimate of the spectral density matrix is non-negative de -
nite.

7 Analysis of a bivariate EEG data set
We present an application of the Auto-SLEX method to EEG recordings at two channels,
namely the left temporal (T 3) and left parietal (P 3), during an epileptic seizure. The EEG
recordings are shown in Figure 1. Each EEG time series has length T = 32768 and was
observed at the sampling rate of 100 Hertz. Our neurologist states that seizure, for this
particular episode, started at the left temporal lobe at about time t = 185 seconds.
    The Auto-SLEX estimates of the time-varying log spectra, plotted in Figure 11, showed
a change in the power at about t = 185 seconds { a result consistent with our neurologist's
observation. One can note that prior to seizure, power was concentrated at the lower
frequencies. During seizure, however, power spread to all frequencies, consistent with the
recruitment of large populations of neurons with diverse ring frequencies. As seizure drew
to an end, the concentration of power was slowly restored to lower frequencies. Another
interesting observation is that dramatic changes in the estimate of the log-spectrum at P 3
came later than t = 185 seconds. This is also consistent with the fact that the patient was
diagnosed with left temporal lobe epilepsy. Hence, seizure for this patient is expected to
start at T 3 and then eventually spread to other regions.
    The Auto-SLEX estimates of the time-varying coherence (cf. Equation (15)), plotted
in Figure 12, showed that the EEGs were strongly coherent at the alpha frequency band of
about 9 to 12 Hertz prior to the onset of seizure. Furthermore, there was strong coherence
at a narrow band of about 40 Hertz. One can observe too that the EEGs were strongly
coherent at higher frequencies at the start of seizure. This is consistent with known clinical
facts that the seizure, which started at T 3 has not spread to P 3 until a few seconds later.
Thus, the EEGs were not in synchrony at the beginning of seizure. While dramatic changes
in the electrical activity at the T 3 channel were taking place at t = 185 seconds, there was
\normal" electrical activity at the P 3 channel at that time. An important observation is

8 Conclusion
The existing methods for analyzing non-stationary time series are built on the assumption
that these non-stationary time series can be modeled by processes that are stationary within
a small time interval. We have shown that the Auto-SLEX method is attractive both from
practical and theoretical points of view. It is a procedure that is developed for bivariate
time series and hence can give estimates of the spectra and coherence. It uses the SLEX
transform which is localized in both time and frequency and hence is ideal for representing
non-stationary processes. The method is also computationally ecient as it takes advantage
of existing powerful algorithms: the fast Fourier transform and BBA. We have also given
strong theoretical justi cations for the Auto-SLEX method. We have rigorously shown that
the smoothed SLEX periodograms are consistent estimators for the time-varying spectrum
of the Dahlhaus locally stationary process. This important theoretical result tells us that
the e ect of the dyadic structure as well as the inclusion of another window, , vanish
asymptotically. Finally, the Auto-SLEX method yields results that are easy to interpret
because they parallel existing methods for stationary processes.
    The Auto-SLEX method is also being further developed in both practical and theoret-
ical directions. In practice, EEGs can be recorded at 128 channels. The method (both on
the segmentation and estimation) has a natural extension from the bivariate to the mul-
tivariate setting. We are working on implementing SLEX (frequency) domain principal
components analysis to extract the important information from data sets with high dimen-
sionality. Furthermore, we are working on a SLEX model of locally stationary processes
where the spectral representation is not in terms of the Fourier complex exponentials, as in
Equation (1), but in terms of the SLEX vectors themselves. We have begun theoretical work
on the open questions of consistency of the Auto-SLEX estimator when the segmentation
is selected by the BBA and when our multivariate estimator is considered. We expect that
our new SLEX-based model of locally stationary processes will facilitate our theoretical
development.
