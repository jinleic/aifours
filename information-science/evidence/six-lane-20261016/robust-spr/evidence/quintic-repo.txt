                                                Abstract
           Every Hurwitz-stable interval family of monic real polynomials of degree ﬁve admits a
       single real numerator of degree ﬁve that makes all the associated transfer functions strictly
       positive real. We prove this existence theorem. The complete proof has also been formalized
       in Lean 4.

Keywords: robust strictly positive real synthesis; interval polynomial; Hurwitz stability;
Kharitonov polynomial; root interlacing; convex separation; Lean 4


1     Introduction
Strictly positive real transfer functions arise in passivity, absolute stability, adaptive control,
and system identiﬁcation. Dasgupta and Bhagwat [1] gave early design conditions for adaptive
output-error identiﬁcation; Yu and Wang [2] survey robust SPR analysis and synthesis. Under
coeﬃcient uncertainty, synthesis requires a single numerator for an entire family of denomina-
tors. The numerator may depend on the family, but it must remain ﬁxed as the denominator
coeﬃcients and the frequency vary.
    For a real interval polynomial family, Kharitonov’s theorem [3] reduces Hurwitz stability
to four distinguished vertex polynomials. For any prescribed numerator, the SPR inequalities
also reduce to these four vertices. The existence problem is harder: four separate numerators,
each suitable for one vertex, do not supply one numerator suitable for all four. The issue is
therefore the simultaneous feasibility of the frequency inequalities, rather than the number of
denominators at which a proposed solution must be tested.
    The fourth-order literature illustrates this distinction. Anderson et al. [4] used a linear-
programming formulation to claim SPR synthesis for every stable fourth-order interval family.
Betser and Zeheb [5] exhibited a synthesizable family for which that linear program was infea-
sible, showing that the proposed condition was not necessary. Their example did not disprove
the existence claim, which Yu and Wang [6] subsequently established. Related low-order re-
sults [7] treated stable segments and interval families of order at most four and developed the
four-vertex reduction.
    For polynomial segments, synthesis was established at order ﬁve [8] and order six [9], and
then at arbitrary order by Yu, Wang, and Ackermann [10], with a further treatment in [11].
These results concern a single scalar interpolation parameter. An interval family allows all
independent coeﬃcient combinations in a box, and numerators obtained for its individual
segments need not coincide. The arbitrary-order segment theorem therefore leaves the interval
existence problem unresolved.
    We prove that every Hurwitz-stable interval family K of monic real quintic polynomials
has a common real numerator b of actual degree ﬁve for which Re(b(iω)/a(iω)) > 0 for every

                                                    1
a ∈ K and every ω ∈ R (Theorem 2.1). The argument exploits both the independent coeﬃcient
intervals and the quadratic degrees of the even and odd components.
    The proof begins with the four-vertex reduction and a compact convex set in the six-
dimensional numerator-coeﬃcient space. A strict separating vector is a common quintic nu-
merator. If synthesis fails, continuous expansion from a singleton family yields a critical family
with a nonzero limiting vector and nonnegative pairings. The constraint vectors at which the
limiting pairing vanishes lie in a hyperplane, so their positive weighted dependence uses at most
six support points (Proposition 3.3). This ﬁnite representation is accompanied by inequalities
that remain valid for every denominator and every frequency.
    Independent coeﬃcient variations ﬁrst force the numerator components to be coprime.
The weighted identities then become an interpolation identity whose degree bounds allow
only a constant proportionality factor (Proposition 4.6). The resulting alternating signs, the
common order of the denominator root intervals, and contact multiplicities reduce the possible
conﬁgurations to ﬁve (Proposition 5.5). This reduction includes an explicit ﬁnite enumeration,
recorded in full in Appendix A. Three comparisons of value ratios at vertices with a shared
component exclude all ﬁve conﬁgurations. The ﬁnal proof combines these propositions to obtain
strict positivity.
    Sections 2–6 develop this argument in the order just described. The proof and its complete
mathematical dependency chain have also been formalized in Lean 4; Section 8 describes the
separate development and gives its source location. The mathematical proof presented here
is self-contained. Its support and degree bounds are speciﬁc to the quintic problem, and no
higher-order interval result is asserted.

A separating vector gives a common numerator. At the ﬁrst parameter where separation fails,
a nonzero limiting vector conﬁnes every support point to one hyperplane; this improves the
usual seven-point convex-hull bound to six.

4     Algebraic consequences of nonnegativity and interpolation
Retain the critical data ﬁxed above. Nonnegativity for the entire box permits independent
coeﬃcient perturbations at a contact. These perturbations exclude common roots of Eb , Ob ;
the resulting coprimality is the essential input to the interpolation argument.

4.1   Exclusion of common roots
Lemma 4.1. Let a be a monic Hurwitz-stable real quintic polynomial, and let c, d ∈ R. If
cEa (t) + tdOa (t) ≥ 0 for every t ≥ 0, then c = d = 0.

Proof. At the roots α1 , α2 of Lemma 2.3, the signs of Oa are positive and negative, respectively.
Thus d ≥ 0 and d ≤ 0, giving d = 0. At t = 0, the inequality Ea (0) = a5 > 0 gives c ≥ 0, while
Ea (β1 ) < 0 gives c ≤ 0.

Lemma 4.2. The polynomials Eb and Ob have no common positive root.

Proof. Suppose Eb (r) = Ob (r) = 0 for some r > 0. Then Pa,b (r) = 0 for every a ∈ K. Since
Pa,b is nonnegative on [0, ∞), it has a local minimum at r, so Pa,b′ (r) = 0. The derivative

equation simpliﬁes to
                               Eb′ (r)Ea (r) + rOb′ (r)Oa (r) = 0.
Changing one coeﬃcient independently leaves the denominator in K, so nonnegativity on the
whole half-line is preserved. Keep the other coeﬃcients ﬁxed and change a5 from its lower
to its upper endpoint. Subtracting the two equations gives ∆5 Eb′ (r) = 0, hence Eb′ (r) = 0.
Independently changing a4 gives r∆4 Ob′ (r) = 0, hence Ob′ (r) = 0. Since Eb , Ob have degree at
most two, simultaneous vanishing of their values and derivatives implies

                              Eb (t) = b1 (t − r)2 ,       Ob (t) = b0 (t − r)2 .

Therefore,
                            Pa,b (t) = (t − r)2 {b1 Ea (t) + tb0 Oa (t)} ≥ 0.

is nonnegative on the half-line. Lemma 4.1 gives b1 = b0 = 0, and the two factorizations then
force the other four coeﬃcients to vanish, contradicting b ̸= 0.

has no such numerator. Proposition 3.3 then supplies a critical family, a nonzero polynomial b
with nonnegative pairings throughout that family, and a ﬁnite weighted representation. Propo-
sitions 4.4 and 4.6 give coprime numerator components and the constant interpolation relation;
Proposition 4.7 gives the two possible support sizes. Proposition 5.5 leaves only Q1–Q5.
    Proposition 6.2 excludes Q4 and Q5, Proposition 6.3 excludes Q1 and Q2, and Proposi-
tion 6.4 excludes Q3. This contradiction proves that a single b ∈ R[s] of actual degree ﬁve
satisﬁes (2.2) for every a ∈ K and every ω ∈ R.

    The quintic assumption enters at two related steps. First, the numerator has six coeﬃ-
cients, and the critical vector determines a ﬁve-dimensional hyperplane, so at most six support
points are needed. Second, both components have degree at most two. The moment identities
and coprimality therefore reduce the interpolation identity to a constant proportionality rela-
tion. These restrictions turn the constraints over inﬁnitely many denominators and frequencies
into the exclusion of ﬁnitely many conﬁgurations. For higher-order interval families, the corre-
sponding degree comparisons do not directly yield the same restrictions, and no higher-order
extension is asserted here.

A     Complete classiﬁcation of contact conﬁgurations
We give the ﬁnite classiﬁcation used in Proposition 5.5 and its recurrence rule. The classiﬁcation
records only two kinds of information: the numerator interval containing each node, and the
denominator component signs at that node. The ﬁrst is represented by the interval index qi
from Section 5; the second is encoded below by an integer. This encoding serves only to list
a ﬁnite table and introduces no new contact conditions. Encode ++, −+, −−, +− by 0, 1, 2, 3,
respectively. The allowed ordered regions for each nonzero denominator sign pair are

          Z(0) = {0, 1, 7, 8},   Z(1) = {1, 2, 3},    Z(2) = {3, 4, 5},   Z(3) = {5, 6, 7}.

Given m, the numerator degree, and the sign of c, put ν = 1{c<0} and ε = 1{t1 =0} . Set h = 5
in the quintic case and h = 4 in the quartic case. Here ε records only whether a zero node
is included in the support; b5 = 0 does not imply ε = 1. Thus branches without a zero node
must initially allow e = 0 as well. The interval indices of every actual positive-contact sequence
satisfy

We enumerate these nondecreasing integer sequences using only necessary conditions. In par-
ticular, when ε = 0, we do not yet remove sequences containing index 0 because of a possible
e = 0; these empty-interval cases are excluded separately at the end of Proposition 5.5. In

                      σi ≡ qi + 3 + 2(m − i + ν) (mod 4),         0 ≤ σi ≤ 3.                 (A.1)

                                 gi = min{z ∈ Z(σi ) : z ≥ gi−1 }.                            (A.2)

Discard the sequence if the set is empty at any step. This test is equivalent to the existence of
nondecreasing region indices ζi ∈ Z(σi ). If the recurrence succeeds, take ζi = gi . Conversely, if
such indices ζi exist, induction gives gi ≤ ζi , so every step of the recurrence is possible. This
proves that the recurrence is both necessary and suﬃcient for region compatibility.
