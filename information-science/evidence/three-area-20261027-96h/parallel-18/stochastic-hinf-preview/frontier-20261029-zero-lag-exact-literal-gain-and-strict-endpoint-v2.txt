0. Exact theorem and scope

Let T>0. On the canonical Brownian filtration consider the SAME actual scalar law
 dx_t=w_t dW_t, z_t=x_t, x_0=0, 0<=t<=T,
with progressive inputs. Write E1 for E||w||_L2[0,T]<infinity and E2 for E||w||_L2[0,T]^2<infinity. A nonzero input means E||w||>0.

Define
 g(y)=exp(y^2/2)-y integral_0^y exp(s^2/2)ds
     =1-sum_(n>=1) y^(2n)/(2^n n!(2n-1)),
and let r be the unique positive zero of g. The candidate theorem is:

 (A) Both literal expected-path-norm gains are exactly r sqrt(T).
 (B) For EVERY nonzero progressive E1 input, hence also every E2 input,
     E||x|| <= r E sqrt(integral_0^T (T-t)w_t^2 dt)
            < r sqrt(T) E||w||.                        (0.1)
     No E1 or E2 input attains the literal gain.
 (C) The original pointwise strict criterion, for this FIXED LAW and either input class,
     E||x||/E||w|| < gamma for every nonzero admissible w,
     holds if and only if gamma>=r sqrt(T). The strict criterion has a CLOSED feasible threshold despite an unattained supremum. A common uniform strict gain margin instead requires gamma>r sqrt(T).
 (D) The same law has RMS gain sqrt(T), not r sqrt(T).
 (E) Exact rational root enclosure: 32673/25000<r<130693/100000, i.e. 1.30692<r<1.30693.

This is a scalar fixed-controller/value subclass, NOT an existential-controller synthesis theorem. For example the law embeds in the registered plant by choosing B0=1, u=w, output x and all drift/state-noise/feedthrough matrices zero except the output coefficient. 1. Scaling and the lower critical number

For u in [0,1] let B_u=W_(Tu)/sqrt(T), v_u=sqrt(T)w_(Tu), X_u=x_(Tu). Then dX=v dB, ||v||=||w||, and ||x||=sqrt(T)||X||. Progressive measurability and both domains are preserved. Work on [0,1] until section 5.

The function g is entire and even, g(0)=1, g'(y)=-integral_0^y exp(s^2/2)ds<0 for y>0, and g''(y)=-exp(y^2/2). It has a unique positive zero because g(y)<=1-y^2/2. Moreover
 g(1)=exp(1/2)-integral_0^1 exp(s^2/2)ds>0,
so r>1. Direct differentiation gives
 g''-y g'+g=0.                                        (1.1)

2. A self-contained finite-vector terminal inequality

Put alpha=integral_0^r exp(s^2/2)ds>0, so g'(r)=-alpha and exp(r^2/2)=r alpha. Define for rho>=0
 f(rho)=-g(rho)/alpha for 0<=rho<=r,
 f(rho)=rho-r for rho>=r.
The two branches have equal value 0 and equal derivative 1 at r. At 0, f'(0)=0. On the inner branch,
 f'=alpha^(-1) integral_0^rho exp(s^2/2)ds,
 f''=alpha^(-1)exp(rho^2/2),
 f-rho f'=-alpha^(-1)exp(rho^2/2).                    (2.1)
Thus f is convex and its tangent line at r is rho-r; consequently
 f(rho)>=rho-r for every rho>=0.                     (2.2)
Also 0<=f'<=1 everywhere.

For a>0 and m in R^n set F(m,a)=sqrt(a) f(||m||/sqrt(a)), and write rho=||m||/sqrt(a). This is C1 and piecewise C2. At m=0 it is smooth because the even inner branch has f'(0)=0. At rho=r the a-derivatives also match: the inner branch has 2sqrt(a)F_a=-exp(r^2/2)/alpha=-r, equal to the outer branch. First derivatives are continuous across the joining sphere, so the generalized Ito formula has no local-time term.

The radial and tangential eigenvalues of D_m^2 F are f''/sqrt(a) and f'/(rho sqrt(a)), respectively. On the inner branch,
 f'/rho<=alpha^(-1)exp(rho^2/2)=f'',
by the average bound on the increasing integrand exp(s^2/2). The eigenvalues are nonnegative; at rho=0 take the common limit. Therefore
 2F_a+lambda_max(D_m^2F)=0 for rho<r.                 (2.3)

On the outer branch, f''=0 and f'=1, so for n>=2 the largest Hessian eigenvalue is 1/(rho sqrt(a)); for n=1 it is 0, which is no greater. Since rho>=r>1,
 2F_a+lambda_max(D_m^2F)
 <=(-r+1/rho)/sqrt(a)<0 for rho>r.                   (2.4)
The Hessian remains positive semidefinite on both branches. The one-sided inequality suffices at the joins. Finally ||grad_m F||=f'<=1.

Now let M_t=integral_0^t q_u v_u dB_u be any R^n-valued continuous martingale with deterministic bounded vector q_u and progressive E2 v. Set
 A^q_t=integral_0^t ||q_u||^2 v_u^2 du.
For epsilon>0 apply finite-dimensional generalized Ito to F(M_t,A^q_t+epsilon):
 drift = [F_a ||q||^2+(1/2)q^T D_m^2F q]v^2 dt
       <=(1/2)||q||^2[2F_a+lambda_max(D_m^2F)]v^2 dt<=0.
The martingale integrand is bounded in magnitude by ||q|| |v|, hence is L2. On compacta the piecewise second derivatives are bounded after epsilon-regularization; localization followed by the usual expectation/Fatou argument justifies the formula and inequality if needed. F has linear growth in ||M|| and sqrt(A^q+epsilon), so its terminal value is integrable. At time 0,
 F(0,epsilon)=-sqrt(epsilon)/alpha.
Taking expectations, using (2.2), and then letting epsilon down to zero gives

 E||M_1|| <= r E sqrt(A^q_1).                         (2.5)

This derivation is given in full to avoid importing a remembered sharp martingale theorem. There is no claim that the inequality or its constant is novel.

3. The new representation: finite vectors of output samples

For n>=1 let t_j=j/n (j=1,...,n), and define
 M^n_t=(X_(t wedge t_1),...,X_(t wedge t_n))/sqrt(n).
This is an actual R^n-valued martingale for E2 v, with
 dM^n_t=q^n(t)v_t dB_t,
 q^n_j(t)=1(t<t_j)/sqrt(n),
 ||q^n(t)||^2=(1/n)#{j:t<t_j}.
No Hilbert-space Ito or stochastic Fubini theorem is needed. At terminal time,
 ||M^n_1||^2=(1/n)sum_(j=1)^n X_(j/n)^2.
Almost surely, these Riemann sums converge to integral_0^1 X_t^2 dt because X has continuous paths.

The associated clock is
 A^n_1=integral_0^1 ||q^n(t)||^2 v_t^2 dt.
For almost every t, ||q^n(t)||^2->1-t, and it is at most 1. Dominated convergence on each finite-energy path gives
 A^n_1->A^out:=integral_0^1(1-t)v_t^2 dt,
and dominated convergence in expectation gives E sqrt(A^n_1)->E sqrt(A^out), since sqrt(A^n_1)<=||v|| is integrable. Apply (2.5) for each n and then Fatou on the output norms:

 E||X|| <= r E sqrt(integral_0^1(1-t)v_t^2 dt).        (3.1)

This is the sharp weighted upper inequality. In particular it is <=r E||v||, but the weight will also prove the endpoint.

3.1 E1 domain identification. For any actual E1 input v let v_n=v 1(|v|<=n). These are bounded E2 inputs and E||v-v_n||->0 by dominated convergence. Apply (3.1) to v_n-v_m: their outputs are Cauchy in L1(Omega;L2[0,1]). The input brackets integral|v-v_n|^2 tend to zero almost surely, so stochastic-integral localization identifies the uniform-in-probability output limit with the actual X(v). Completeness of the Banach space gives the same limit in expected L2 path norm. Passing to the limit in (3.1), using E sqrt(integral(1-t)|v-v_n|^2)<=E||v-v_n||, proves (3.1) for E1. The E1 ratios are approximated by E2 ratios; the two gains coincide.

3.2 Strictness for every physical input. If a sample path has ||v||>0, then integral_0^1 t v_t^2 dt>0: a nonzero L2 function cannot be supported on the single point t=0. Thus on that event
 sqrt(integral_0^1(1-t)v_t^2 dt)<||v||.
A nonzero input has this event with positive probability. Both sides have finite expectation for E1, so their expectations are strictly ordered. Combining with (3.1) proves E||X||<r E||v|| for EVERY nonzero E1/E2 input, not merely for bounded witnesses. No maximizer exists.

4. Matching lower bound using bounded actual progressive inputs

The proof is repeated here so the earlier bracket packet is not needed.

For any 0<a<r, put U(x,t)=sqrt(t)g(x/sqrt(t)), t>0. By (1.1), U_t+(1/2)U_xx=0. For a Brownian beta let
 tau_a=inf{s>=0:|beta_s|=a sqrt(1+s)}.
Up to this boundary, g(a)<=g(beta_s/sqrt(1+s))<=1. Ito and bounded stopping at tau_a wedge N give
 g(a)E sqrt(1+tau_a wedge N)
 <=E U(beta_(tau_a wedge N),1+tau_a wedge N)=1.
Therefore monotone convergence proves E sqrt(1+tau_a)<=1/g(a)<infinity, so tau_a<infinity a.s. The boundary value is |beta_tau|=a sqrt(1+tau). The dominated martingale limit also gives g(a)E sqrt(1+tau)=1, though only finiteness is needed.

For delta in (0,1), integer N>=1 and R=N/delta, use the physical Brownian path on [0,delta] to define beta_s=sqrt(R)B_(s/R), 0<=s<=N. Stop at the boundary above or at N, whichever is first. The physical input is
 v_t=sqrt(R)1(t<delta)1(Rt<tau_a).
The event in the second indicator depends only on the Brownian path by time t. This input is bounded and progressive, hence E2, with ||v||^2=tau_a wedge N. Its actual state after delta is beta_(tau_a wedge N). Hence

 E||X||/E||v|| >= sqrt(1-delta)
    E|beta_(tau_a wedge N)|/E sqrt(tau_a wedge N).       (4.1)

For the limit, couple the stopped variables on one beta; this is a law comparison, not anticipative dependence in the physical input. The bound |beta_(tau_a wedge N)|<=a sqrt(1+tau_a) is integrable. Dominated and monotone convergence show that the ratio on the right tends, as N grows, to
 a E sqrt(1+tau_a)/E sqrt(tau_a)>a.
Then let delta down to zero. The literal gain is at least a for every a<r and therefore at least r. Together with (3.1) this proves the exact unit-time gain r and both domain assertions in (A).

This is a fully specified admissible family proving the supremum. No finite numerical N or delta is claimed to attain r or to meet a separately specified error. Infinite clock energy is never asserted to be an admissible E2 input: each actual witness uses finite N and bounded v.

5. Physical horizon, strict-threshold topology and RMS

Rescaling (3.1) gives exactly (0.1), because
 sqrt(T) sqrt(integral_0^1(1-u)v_u^2 du)
 =sqrt(integral_0^T(T-t)w_t^2 dt).
Strictness follows from integral t w_t^2>0 on every nonzero input path, as in 3.2. The lower witnesses rescale too, so the gain is r sqrt(T), not just an upper bound.

For gamma>=r sqrt(T), every input ratio is strictly below r sqrt(T)<=gamma. For gamma<r sqrt(T), the supremum property supplies a bounded E2 witness with ratio>gamma. This proves (C). It does not confuse pointwise strictness with a common strict margin.

For the SAME law, Ito isometry and Tonelli give
 E||x||^2=E integral_0^T(T-t)w_t^2 dt <=T E||w||^2.
The deterministic pulses w=epsilon^(-1/2)1_[0,epsilon] have squared RMS ratio T-epsilon/2. Therefore the RMS gain is sqrt(T). In particular, for this fixed law and any gamma in [sqrt(T),r sqrt(T)), pointwise RMS strictness holds but pointwise literal strictness fails. This is a comparison of the two genuinely different criteria, not a counterexample to existential-controller synthesis (another controller could behave differently).
