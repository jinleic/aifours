# Exact nonaliasing of finite arrays, and two invalid lattice shortcuts

Date: 2026-10-14 (America/Los_Angeles). Status: classical written proof and exact computational certification; **not Lean/kernel verified**. The isotropic criterion is reproduction of prior work, not a claimed discovery. See README for primary-source access limits and scope.

## 1. Precisely what is being answered

The book asks what conditions on an arbitrary array prevent spatial-spectrum aliasing; it identifies aliasing with grating lobes and motivates the question with nonuniform/conformal arrays. We answer the noiseless, narrow-band, single far-field plane-wave, calibrated isotropic-sensor reading, with unknown nonzero complex source amplitude (in particular, unknown common phase). We do not infer identifiability of several simultaneous sources, low sidelobes, robustness to noise, or arbitrary directional element patterns from this answer.

Let N>=1, d>=1, positions p_1,...,p_N in R^d in **half-wavelength units**, and any set U subset R^d of allowed direction coordinates. Duplicate sensors and rank-deficient geometries are permitted. Put

    a(u)_j = exp(i*pi*p_j dot u),
    D = [(p_2-p_1)^T; ...; (p_N-p_1)^T],
    G = {x in R^d : Dx in 2 Z^(N-1)}.

An alias is a pair u != v in U with a(u)=c a(v), c in C\{0}. This is equality of complex projective steering vectors, not literal vector equality when the phase origin is arbitrary. The source amplitude is not known: s a(u)=t a(v) for some nonzero s,t is exactly this condition.

For strictly positive real taper weights w_j, matched beamforming at v has normalized magnitude

    F_w(u;v) = |sum_j w_j exp(i*pi*p_j dot (u-v))| / sum_j w_j.

A full-height grating-lobe copy means u != v and F_w(u;v)=1. Smaller sidelobes are not being forbidden.

## 2. General isotropic theorem (all finite real geometries and direction sets)

**Theorem 1.** For u,v in U, the following are equivalent:

1. a(u) and a(v) are projectively equal.
2. D(u-v) lies in 2 Z^(N-1).
3. (p_j-p_k) dot (u-v) is an even integer for every j,k.
4. F_w(u;v)=1 for one strictly positive taper w.
5. F_w(u;v)=1 for every strictly positive taper w.

Consequently the array is alias-free on U if and only if

    G intersect (U-U) subset {0}.                         (T1)

Equivalently, the translates U+g, g in G, are pairwise disjoint. Empty/singleton U cause no difficulty; the subset notation in (T1) avoids falsely asserting that 0 belongs to U-U when U is empty.

**Proof.** Since every isotropic component has modulus one, a(u)=c a(v) forces |c|=1. Divide the jth component equality by the first: exp(i*pi*(p_j-p_1) dot (u-v))=1, equivalently an even integer phase difference. This proves 1 iff 2. Subtracting the reference equations gives 3, and taking k=1 reverses the implication. Equality in the triangle inequality for the sum of nonzero complex numbers w_j exp(i*pi*p_j dot (u-v)) occurs exactly when all summands have the same argument. Strict positivity makes this condition equivalent to 3 for every sensor, proving 4 and 5. The pairwise and difference-set formulations now follow. Finally, U+g and U+h intersect iff g-h belongs to U-U; G is an additive group. QED.

One can also prove the weight claim directly from the book's co-array identity:

    1-F_w(u;v)^2
      = [sum_(j,k) w_j w_k (1-cos(pi*(p_j-p_k) dot (u-v)))]/(sum_j w_j)^2.

Every summand is nonnegative, and every coefficient is positive. Vanishing is equivalent to all pair differences being even integers. Thus no minimum-distance statistic of the co-array alone is required. For nonnegative weights with zero entries the theorem applies only to the active subarray. The stronger phrase "no lobe under any nonzero weighting" is false: retain one sensor and the magnitude is constant in every direction. Arbitrary complex or unmatched weights are outside the equivalence.

## 3. The alias group need not be a full-rank lattice

Let H=ker D, W=H-perp and L=G intersect W. Orthogonal decomposition gives

    G = H direct-sum L.                                  (T2)

Indeed D(h+x)=Dx, so each component x in W of an element of G belongs to L. On W the map D is injective. Every nonzero x in L has ||Dx||>=2, hence ||x||>=2/||D|| when D is nonzero. L is therefore discrete; a discrete subgroup of the r-dimensional Euclidean space W is a lattice in its own real span, of rank q<=r=rank D. (The standard elementary discrete-subgroup theorem chooses successive shortest independent vectors; discreteness ensures the resulting fundamental cell has no accumulation of group points.) The r=0 case gives L={0}, G=R^d.

In particular, full column rank makes G discrete but does **not** make its rank d. Exact examples:

* d=1, D=[1;sqrt(2)] has G={0}: x=2m and sqrt(2)x=2n imply m=n=0.
* d=2, D=[(1,0);(sqrt(2),0);(0,1)] has G={0} x 2Z, rank one although rank D=2.
* Appending a zero third column gives G={0} x 2Z x R, a mixed discrete/continuous group.
* If D is rational and has full column rank, G does have full rank: choose a positive integer Q clearing every denominator; then 2Q Z^d subset G. This sufficient rationality fact must not be imposed on arbitrary real data.

Theorem 1 never requires a lattice basis or any commensurability hypothesis. The 2022 paper explicitly assumes full column rank and introduces its parallelepiped after assuming a full-rank ("nontrivial") solution lattice; its full-rank basis shortcuts cannot simply be exported to these other cases.

## 4. Full physical domains and boundary conventions

For a line array the usual identifiable direction coordinate is u=sin(theta) in the closed interval [-1,1], not every physical bearing on the full sphere. For a planar array, restricting to one closed hemisphere gives a unit disk of projected directions. Without that hemisphere convention, mirror directions cannot be resolved by a planar isotropic array. For a volumetric array the physical directions form S^2. We also include the in-plane unit circle S^1.

For any d>=1, the difference of the closed unit ball B_d with itself is the closed ball 2B_d. For d>=2 the same is true of S^(d-1)-S^(d-1): given nonzero x with ||x||<=2, choose h perpendicular to x with ||h||^2=1-||x||^2/4 and set u=h+x/2, v=h-x/2. Both are unit vectors and u-v=x. In the ball take h=0. The one-dimensional sphere S^0 is not covered by this statement; it is not the linear-array interval domain.

**Corollary 2.** On B_d (d>=1) or S^(d-1) (d>=2), alias-free is equivalent to

    there is no x in G with 0<||x||<=2.                  (T3)

If D is rank deficient, any sufficiently small nonzero vector in ker D violates (T3). If D has full column rank and G is nonzero, discreteness gives an attained shortest nonzero norm, and (T3) says that norm is strictly greater than 2. If G={0}, use shortest norm +infinity. This reproduces the correct content of Tucker et al., Proposition 3/Corollary 4, with the missing group/domain cases stated explicitly.

The threshold is **strict** for the closed domains. The two sensors p_1=0,p_2=1 are separated by exactly lambda/2. The endpoints u=1,v=-1 have a(u)=a(v)=(1,-1), so the closed interval is not alias-free. For a uniform line array with at least two sensors and spacing t in half-wavelength units, closed-interval alias-free means 0<t<1. At t=1 only the two endfire endpoints alias; open interval (-1,1) instead allows t=1. A beam steered to a fixed interior direction can also have no copy at t=1. The book's informal "not exceeding half a wavelength" statement must be read with that endpoint/steering qualification, not silently substituted into (T3).

## 5. A terminating exact finite decision procedure

This is an explicit bounded implementation of the classical integer-wrapping test, not a new uniqueness principle, not an unbounded shortest-vector restatement, and not quantifier elimination.

**Theorem 3.** Given finitely many rational or real algebraic coordinates in half-wavelength units, one can decide (T3) in finitely many exact operations as follows.

1. Compute D and its rank by exact row elimination. If rank D<d, extract a nonzero kernel vector q. Divide q by a positive integer M large enough that ||q/M||<=2; this is a certified alias difference and the answer is ALIASED.
2. Otherwise choose d independent rows of D, forming an invertible square matrix B. For row b_i let M_i=floor(sqrt(b_i dot b_i)).
3. Enumerate all k in the finite box product_i [-M_i,M_i] intersect Z^d, excluding 0. For each set x=2 B^(-1)k. Accept it as an alias iff x dot x<=4 and every component of Dx/2 is an integer. If none qualifies, the answer is ALIAS_FREE.

**Proof of bound and completeness.** Any physical alias has ||x||<=2. On every row d_j of D its wrapping integer satisfies

    |(d_j dot x)/2| <= ||d_j||.                          (T4)

In particular its k=Bx/2 is in the enumerated box. Since B is invertible and x!=0, k!=0. The algorithm reconstructs that exact x and checks every omitted sensor equation. Conversely every accepted x is nonzero, has norm at most 2 and lies in G, so Corollary 2 supplies a physical alias. The rank-deficient branch is correct for the same reason. There are at most product_i(2M_i+1)-1 candidates, independent of any assumed rank of G. Thus partially commensurate and trivial groups are handled without first trying to construct a full-rank lattice. QED.

Choosing d independent rows instead of enumerating all N-1 wrapping integers is only an implementation of the same linear-algebraic constraint; no originality claim is attached. The 2022 paper attributes the original all-direction N&S integer-image test to Godara–Cantoni (1981), Theorem 6. The 1981 full text was not obtained here, so exact historical constants and endpoint conventions are not asserted from its theorem. The independently derived (T4) uses **difference radius 2**, and is not copied from the potentially differently normalized one-unit-ball wording in the later paper.

### Executable algebraic representation and termination

`work/check_exact.py` accepts JSON. Rational coordinates are fraction strings (no floating inputs). Algebraic coordinates are polynomials in a common real primitive element alpha, specified by an irreducible polynomial over Q and a rational open isolating interval containing exactly one root. A coordinate list `[c0,c1,...]` denotes sum_i c_i alpha^i. Any finite tuple of real algebraic numbers admits such a representation by the primitive-element theorem; supplying it is the input encoding, not a numerical approximation. The executable verifies irreducibility and root isolation.

Arithmetic is exact in Q[t]/(f), with rational coefficients. Equality is equality of reduced polynomial residues. To order a nonzero residue, evaluate its polynomial by rational interval arithmetic on the isolating interval and bisect that interval, choosing the root-containing side using an exact root count, until its sign is separated from zero. This terminates: a nonzero residue of degree less than irreducible f cannot vanish at alpha, and interval widths tend to zero. Constants are compared directly. An element is an integer iff its reduced residue is a constant integer. Bounds M_i are obtained by exact sign comparisons to integer squares using exponential bracketing and binary search. All loops are finite; no tolerance or unknown-to-false conversion is present.

The supplied examples are small, not a complexity or performance claim for every input. The program processes the integer box lazily and is single-threaded. Its optional resource cap must raise an explicit error, never declare ALIAS_FREE from an incomplete search.

## 6. Separate refutations of equation (11) and Corollary 5

Source: Tucker–Zhao–Ahmad–Potter, *IEEE Signal Processing Letters* 29 (2022), 2457–2461, DOI 10.1109/LSP.2022.3224834, open author manuscript at PMC9757818. Columns of V are lattice basis vectors. The source's equation (11) tests only Vc for c in {0,1}^d\{0}, whereas Corollary 5 says all distinct parallelepiped vertices must be more than 2 apart, which also tests coefficients in {-1,0,1}^d\{0}. These are different tests. We do not conflate them.

### 6.1 Counterexample E: equation (11), not Corollary 5

Take

    V = [[3, 29/10], [0, 3/10]],
    D = 2 V^(-1) = [[2/3, -58/9], [0, 20/3]],
    p_1=(0,0), p_2=(2/3,-58/9), p_3=(0,20/3).

DV=2I, so the **entire** alias group is precisely V Z^2: Dx in 2Z^2 iff x in VZ^2. The nonzero binary-column sums have squared norms

    ||v_1||^2=9,  ||v_2||^2=17/2,  ||v_1+v_2||^2=349/10,

all greater than 4. Equation (11) therefore declares alias-free. But

    x=v_2-v_1=(-1/10,3/10),  ||x||^2=1/10,
    u=x/2=(-1/20,3/20), v=-x/2,
    Dx=(-2,2).

Both directions are in the unit disk. Directly from the original sensor positions, p_j dot u=(0,-1,1) and p_j dot v=(0,1,-1), hence

    a(u)=a(v)=(1,-1,-1).

This is a full-height alias for every positive matched taper. Geometrically, the two unit disks centred at 0 and x overlap, since ||x||<2. Corollary 5 correctly fails on this example because v_1 and v_2 are close; E alone does **not** refute Corollary 5.

### 6.2 Counterexample C: even all pairwise vertex distances are insufficient

Take

    V = [[3,30], [0,3/10]],
    D = 2 V^(-1) = [[2/3,-200/3], [0,20/3]],
    p_1=(0,0), p_2=(2/3,-200/3), p_3=(0,20/3).

Again DV=2I and G=VZ^2. Up to sign, differences of distinct parallelogram vertices are v_1,v_2,v_1+v_2,v_2-v_1, with squared norms

    9, 90009/100, 108909/100, 72909/100,

respectively. Every pair of vertices is separated by at least 3>2; Corollary 5 declares alias-free. Equation (11) passes as well. Nevertheless

    x=10v_1-v_2=(0,-3/10),  ||x||^2=9/100,
    u=(0,-3/20), v=(0,3/20), Dx=(20,-2).

Original-data phases are p_j dot u=(0,10,-1) and p_j dot v=(0,-10,1), so a(u)=a(v)=(1,1,-1). Geometrically G=3Z x (3/10)Z, because (m,n) maps to (3(m+10n),3n/10); the shortest nonzero lattice norm is exactly 3/10. Its coefficient vector (10,-1) is outside the union of origin-adjacent fundamental cells used in the source's sufficiency argument. That asserted coverage is false for an arbitrary skew basis.

Appending an orthogonal basis vector (0,0,3) to either example gives a three-dimensional counterexample to the corresponding arbitrary-basis shortcut. Every additional pairwise difference has squared norm at least 9; the old short vector remains. Corollary 2 converts it into two unit-sphere directions. No three-dimensional reduction heuristic is needed for the refutation.

These refutations do not refute Proposition 3 or Corollary 4. Correctness of the explicit counterexamples is certified below; whether an erratum or prior report already contains them is **NOVELTY-UNCLEAR**. An unsuccessful correction search is not evidence of priority.

## 7. What reduction can and cannot repair

In dimension two, a **Gauss-reduced** basis b_1,b_2 satisfies

    a=||b_1||^2 <= b=||b_2||^2,
    2|c| <= a, where c=b_1 dot b_2.

**Lemma 4.** Its shortest nonzero lattice norm is ||b_1||.

**Proof.** For x=m b_1+n b_2, n=0 gives ||x||^2=m^2 a>=a. For |n|=1 and m=0 use b>=a; for |n|=1, |m|>=1 use ||x||^2>=m^2a+b-|m|a>=a. If |n|>=2, complete the square:

    ||x||^2 = a(m+nc/a)^2+n^2(b-c^2/a)
             >=4(b-a/4)>=3a.

Thus no nonzero vector is shorter than b_1. QED.

Both the binary-sum test and all-vertex-pairs test include b_1 and inspect only nonzero lattice vectors; therefore **both shortcuts are valid after a verified Gauss reduction in 2D**, by this lemma, not for an arbitrary basis. Standard exact Gauss reduction swaps to put the shorter vector first and subtracts the nearest integer multiple until the two inequalities hold. Every nonterminal swap strictly reduces a positive lattice length, and discreteness gives termination. The checker verifies unimodularity, equality of the lattices, and the reduction inequalities before using the conclusion. In E a shortest vector is (-1/10,3/10); in C it is (0,3/10).

For 3D we do **not** claim that an unspecified "Minkowski reduction", pairwise size reduction, or LLL makes either printed shortcut correct. A definition of Minkowski reduction that already certifies its first vector as a shortest vector would trivially imply both tests, but obtaining that certification contains the missing shortest-vector task. We do not use that circular observation as a new algorithm.

Instead, for any invertible rational/algebraic V in any dimension, use the certified finite box

    |z_i| <= floor(2 ||row_i(V^(-1))||),                 (T5)

because ||Vz||<=2 implies z=V^(-1)(Vz). Enumerate its nonzero integer vectors and check ||Vz||^2<=4 exactly. This is a complete replacement for **both** shortcuts in 3D as well as 2D. It is equivalent to Theorem 3 for D=2V^(-1), not a claim that the original 2^d-1 test was correct.

## 8. Directional conformal elements, including zeros

For arbitrary calibrated complex patterns g_j:U->C define

    b_j(u)=g_j(u) exp(i*pi*p_j dot u),
    Z={u in U : b(u)=0}, A=U\Z.

Assume a single source of unknown nonzero complex amplitude. For u,v in A, equality of possible noiseless nonzero outputs is equivalent to b(u)=c b(v), c!=0. Equivalently all 2-by-2 minors vanish:

    b_j(u)b_k(v)-b_k(u)b_j(v)=0, for every j,k.          (T6)

Proof: proportionality makes the minors vanish. Conversely select k with b_k(v)!=0. Vanishing of minors forces b_j(u)=b_k(u)b_j(v)/b_k(v); because b(u)!=0, b_k(u)!=0, and the ratio is nonzero. This also proves that supports {j:g_j(u)!=0} and {j:g_j(v)!=0} must agree. On their common nonempty support it is equivalent to constancy over j of

    [g_j(u)/g_j(v)] exp(i*pi*p_j dot (u-v)).             (T7)

The normalized matched-correlation criterion

    |b(u)^* b(v)|/(||b(u)|| ||b(v)||)=1

is equivalent by equality in Cauchy–Schwarz. It is already the perfect-ambiguity criterion in Kastinen–Kero (2020), Section 2.1, equations (3)–(6), for arbitrary complex receiver gains on nonzero responses.

Zero vectors require separate treatment: a blind direction yields zero data for every nonzero source amplitude. Two blind directions are mutually indistinguishable; one blind and one visible direction are not indistinguishable under the nonzero-amplitude assumption. Thus direction identifiability for all nonzero sources (including a zero measurement) means projective injectivity on A **and |Z|<=1**. If absence of a source (zero amplitude) is also an allowed hypothesis, even a single blind direction is inseparable from no source; requiring detectability on all directions means Z is empty. The minors alone wrongly label every zero/nonzero pair as proportional, so they must not be used without this split.

If g_j(u)=c_j h(u) for fixed nonzero c_j and h(u)!=0 everywhere, invert the fixed diagonal c_j and absorb h into the source amplitude: exactly Theorem 1 applies. Fixed c_j=0 means discard those inactive sensors. Otherwise arbitrary directional gains can remove or create geometry-only aliases. Indeed choosing g_j(u)=exp(-i*pi*p_j dot u) makes every b(u)=(1,...,1), even for a geometry with trivial isotropic alias group. Conversely directional amplitudes can distinguish a geometrically aliased pair.

(T6)–(T7) are exact characterizations, **not a constructive universal solution for arbitrary gain functions**. Neither their statement nor a generic equation solver proves finite global injectivity for unspecified functions. The 2020 paper supplies case-by-case numerical gradient searches and noisy-ambiguity simulations, not the exact global finite decision of Theorem 3 for all such patterns. The book does not specify a representation/regularity class of conformal gains. Our full constructive result is therefore confined to isotropic/common-pattern arrays; a general directional-conformal synthesis/decision problem is not counted as solved.
