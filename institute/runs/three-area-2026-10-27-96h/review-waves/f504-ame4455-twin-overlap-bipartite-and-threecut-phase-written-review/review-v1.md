## 4. Reconstruction of the actual common-tensor interface

**Source: S1 lines 47–77.**

A modulus-one entry exhausts its row and column in each unitary flattening. The \(24\) such entries therefore occupy distinct rows and columns, leaving a \(12\times12\) unitary residual matrix with \(24\) nonzero entries.

A residual row or column cannot have only one nonzero entry, because its unit norm would make that entry modulus one. Every residual row and column thus has at least two nonzeros; the total count forces exactly two.

For each physical coordinate pair \(ij\), this proves that:

- \(R_{ij}=\pi_{ij}(E)\) has twelve elements;
- each element has exactly two residual extensions;
- the complementary maximal-pair projection is injective.

For block closure, two distinct residual rows sharing one nonzero column must share their second nonzero column. Otherwise their inner product contains exactly one nonzero summand. Since every column also has degree two, these rows and their two columns form an isolated full \(2\times2\) block.

This is actual support closure enforced by unitary orthogonality, not an assumption about arbitrary degree-two graph shadows.

## 5. Reconstruction of the twin-overlap invariant

**Source: S1 lines 133–175.**

Suppose residual words differ only at physical coordinate \(i\), using distinct symbols \(a,a'\). With \(v\) denoting the other three physical coordinates, let

\[
T=V_a\cap V_{a'}.
\]

The original pair supplies an element of \(T\), so \(T\neq\varnothing\).

### 5.1 Each other coordinate supplies a perfect matching

Fix one other coordinate \(j\), with complementary coordinates \(k,l\). For \(v\in T\), the rows

\[
(a,v_j),\qquad(a',v_j)
\]

in the actual \(ij\mid kl\) flattening share column \((v_k,v_l)\).

Each row has exactly two nonzero entries. Actual block closure forces their other columns to coincide. Hence the unique other tuple with that \(j\)-coordinate belongs to both slices and therefore to \(T\).

For each \(j\), this is a fixed-point-free pairing of \(T\). The three remaining coordinates consequently give three perfect matchings.

### 5.2 The matching union is simple and cubic

If distinct \(v,v'\in T\) shared two of their three coordinates, the corresponding physical pair would have the four distinct extensions

\[
(a,v),\quad(a',v),\quad(a,v'),\quad(a',v').
\]

This contradicts exact multiplicity two.

Therefore the three matching neighbors at each vertex are distinct. Their union is a simple cubic graph, not a multigraph obtained by counting repeated matching edges.

### 5.3 Complex orthogonality forces bipartiteness

Define

\[
z_v=U_{a,v}\overline{U_{a',v}}.
\]

Both factors are nonzero residual entries, so \(z_v\neq0\). Along any matching edge \(vv'\), orthogonality of the corresponding actual rows gives

\[
z_v+z_{v'}=0.
\]

Consequently \(z\) changes sign along every edge. An odd cycle would imply \(z_v=-z_v\), impossible for a nonzero complex number.

The graph is therefore bipartite. Nothing in this argument assumes that amplitudes are real, have equal moduli, or have individually chosen signs.

### 5.4 The two corollaries follow at their stated scope

A nonempty simple cubic bipartite graph has at least three vertices in each bipartition class, hence at least six vertices.

- A degree-two symbol has only four residual words. Its common slice cannot contain this graph. This proves the varying-low-symbol prohibition.
- Two degree-three symbols each have six residual words. A nonempty common slice must therefore exhaust both six-word slices. Their neighborhoods coincide, and the six-vertex cubic bipartite graph is \(K_{3,3}\).

These establish S1 lines 177–193 without importing no-distance-one as a hypothesis.

\[
\arg u-\arg v-\arg w+\arg x

## 9. Downstream synthesis and exact extent of the exclusion

**Source: S1 lines 304–407.**

I also checked the stated downstream consumption of these interfaces.

Both five-symbol parties must therefore have profile \((3,3,2,2,2)\). Each low symbol uses both opposite high symbols, exhausting all high degrees and forcing

\[
R_{34}=(L_3\times H_4)\sqcup(H_3\times L_4).
\]

For \(a\in A_1\), the manuscript’s counts \(x(a)\) and \(u(a)\) count the same residual words through their \(13\)- and \(14\)-pairs. Exact multiplicity two gives \(x(a)=u(a)\). Degree three and the two-element high sets give

\[
1\le x(a)=u(a)\le2,\qquad
\sum_{a\in A_1}x(a)=6.
\]

For a low party-3 slice:

- Equal party-1/party-4 partitions would create a forbidden varying-party-2 distance-one pair.
- Equal party-2/party-4 partitions would create a forbidden varying-party-1 pair.
- Thus only all-distinct type A or equal-party-1/party-2 type D remains.

Type D creates a distance-one pair between the two high party-4 symbols. Their degree-three twin neighborhoods coincide, leaving one of the four party-1 symbols adjacent to neither high symbol. This contradicts \(u(a)\ge1\).

Every low party-3 slice is therefore type A. Distinct balanced partitions imply that every party-1 neighbor of such a slice occurs with both high party-4 values. Every party-1 symbol has a low party-3 neighbor, so \(u(a)=x(a)=2\) for all four symbols. The resulting sum is eight, contradicting the required sum six.
