## 4. Full Latin left-kernel and parity proof

\[

\[

If \(a\) is constant, fix one column and vary the row. The Latin property makes \(L(r,j)\) run through all ten symbols, forcing \(c\) constant. The equation then forces \(b\) constant. The same reasoning applies if either other coordinate family is constant. Thus every solution outside \(T\) is nonconstant in all three families.

For such a solution, choose one row with \(a_r=0\) and another with \(a_r=1\). Each row sees every symbol once. The first gives

\[
\operatorname{wt}(b)=\operatorname{wt}(c),

\[
\operatorname{wt}(b)=10-\operatorname{wt}(c).
\]

Therefore both weights are five. Applying the same reasoning to columns gives \(\operatorname{wt}(a)=5\). Every nonconstant relation has total weight \(15\), and gives the asserted balanced quotient

## 5. Universal complex shared-ray lemma

Let \(U\) be the \(3\times3\) unitary overlap matrix of two disjoint nonparallel ONBs of \(\mathbb C^3\).

No entry has modulus one, since that would identify two rays. Therefore every row and column has at least two nonzero entries: a sole nonzero entry in a unit-norm row or column would have modulus one.

Two zeros in the same row or column violate this condition. If two zeros lie in different rows and columns, permute them to \(U_{11}=U_{22}=0\). Then

\[
U_{12},U_{13},U_{21},U_{23}\neq0.
\]

The inner product of the first two rows has exactly one nonzero summand,

\[
U_{13}\overline{U_{23}}\neq0,

Thus \(U\) has at most one zero.
