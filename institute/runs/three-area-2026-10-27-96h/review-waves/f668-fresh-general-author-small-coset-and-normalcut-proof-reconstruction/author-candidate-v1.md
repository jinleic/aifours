## 1. Object, standing lemmas, and imported source

A left coset is `gU={gu:u in U}`. For a group `X`, `B_7(X)<=r` means that every `r+1` pairwise-disjoint actual left cosets of finite-index subgroups have a pair of subgroup indices with gcd at least 8. `J_7(X)<=s` means this gcd conclusion for every `s+1` proper finite-index subgroup **labels**, with arbitrary repetitions and without any disjointness assumption. Thus `J_7<=s` implies `B_7<=s`; a proper index at most 7 makes every finite `J_7` bound false by repetition. A bad family below means pairwise-disjoint actual cosets with every pair of indices having gcd at most 7.

We use the following elementary facts, for arbitrary groups when indices are finite.

1. If subgroup indices `m,n` are coprime, every left coset of one meets every left coset of the other. For `A,B<=X`, the map `X/(A∩B) -> X/A × X/B` is injective. Its domain has size divisible by both coprime `m,n` and at most `mn`, hence size `mn`; the map is surjective. In particular, disjoint actual cosets have noncoprime indices.
2. Any finite disjoint family has `sum 1/[X:U_i] <= 1`. For infinite `X`, pass to the finite quotient by the intersection of the finitely many normal cores of the `U_i`; it preserves each index and disjointness. For finite `X`, this is just counting cardinalities.
3. If `xU` is contained in `yV`, then `y^-1 x` belongs to `V` and `U<=V`. Thus translating the containing coset back to `V` turns `xU` into an actual left `V`-coset of `U`.
4. A perfect group has no nontrivial solvable quotient. For every `q>=4`, `SL(2,q)` is generated by upper and lower unipotents (ordinary row elimination); a diagonal element `diag(t,t^-1)` with `t^2!=1` expresses each such unipotent as a commutator. Therefore both `SL(2,q)` and its projective quotient are perfect. In `A5`, double transpositions invert 3-cycles, and `[t,c]=c` for such an involution `t` and 3-cycle `c`; 3-cycles generate `A5`, so `A5` is perfect. Consequently neither a perfect group nor `A5` has a proper subgroup of index 2, 3 or 4: the coset action would give a nontrivial image in solvable `S4`.

## 3. An order-60 lemma: both q=4 and q=5

**Lemma.** If a perfect group `X` has order 60, then `B_7(X)<=7`.

A bad family containing an index-1 coset has only one member. The other possible indices are divisors of 60 excluding 2,3,4, namely `5,6,10,12,15,20,30,60`. We use an overinclusive list, so no subgroup-existence table is assumed.

If index 5 occurs, every other index is divisible by 5 by the coprime-index lemma. There are at most two labels with index greater than 5: one from `10,20,30,60`, all pairwise sharing 10, and at most one of index 15. At most five index-5 cosets fit; if five fit they fill the group, while otherwise there are at most four plus those two. In either case there are fewer than eight.

If index 5 does not occur, at most three labels have index greater than 6: at most one from `10,20,30,60`, at most one 12, and at most one 15. At most six index-6 cosets fit, with six filling the group. With at most four of them, there are at most seven labels. With five, an eight-family would require all three larger types. Pairwise low gcd with 15 rules out 30 and 60, leaving `12,15` and either 10 or 20. Their reciprocal sum is at least `1/12+1/15+1/20=1/5`, but the five index-6 cosets leave only `1/6`. Contradiction.

The projective order formula and perfectness apply to `q=4` and `q=5`. Thus both satisfy the actual-coset conclusion. In `q=4`, the projective-line point stabilizer has index 5; in `q=5`, it has index 6. Repeating either label shows the respective `J_7` is not finite.

## 4. The order-360 exceptional case: actual index-6 transporters

Let `X=PSL(2,9)`. Its order is 360 and it is perfect. The imported maximal indices are 6,10,15. Any index-6 subgroup `H` is maximal: an overgroup has index dividing 6, and the list allows no smaller proper index. Hence `H≅A5`.

First count all bad-family labels of index greater than 6, with repetitions included. At most one index is divisible by 10 and at most one by 15. Any remaining index is divisible by 6 but not 5, since a multiple of both 6 and 5 is divisible by both 10 and 15. Its subgroup lies in an index-6 `A5`; its index is `6m`, where `m>1` divides 60 and is not divisible by 5. The only possibilities are `m=2,3,4,6,12`; the first three are impossible by perfectness of `A5`. The remaining original indices 36 and 72 have gcd 36, so contribute at most one label in total. Therefore at most three labels have index greater than 6.

Next establish the actual incidence of index-6 cosets. The normalizer of a maximal `A5` subgroup `H` is `H`: otherwise it is all `X`, making `X/H` a nontrivial solvable group of order 6, impossible for perfect `X`. In the six-point action on `X/H`, the stabilizer `H` fixes only its own point. Every nontrivial orbit has size at least 5 because `A5` has no subgroups of index 2,3,4. Thus the remaining five points form one orbit: the action is 2-transitive.

For nonconjugate index-6 subgroups `H,K`, `H` has no fixed point on `X/K`, for a fixed point would make the two equal up to conjugacy. The same small-index exclusion makes its six-point action transitive. Thus `H` meets every left `K`-coset, and translating by an arbitrary element shows **every** left coset of `H` meets **every** left coset of `K`. This applies to any two different conjugacy classes; hence all index-6 subgroups underlying a pairwise-disjoint collection belong to one class.

Identify that class with the stabilizers `H_x` of the six points. Self-normalizing implies different points give different stabilizers. Every actual left coset of `H_x` is precisely a transporter

`D(x,y)={g in X : g(x)=y}`,

because if `g0(x)=y`, then `D(x,y)=g0 H_x`. For two different cells `(x,y),(x',y')`, their transporter sets are disjoint exactly when `x=x'` or `y=y'`: equality of only one coordinate contradicts a permutation, while 2-transitivity supplies an element when both coordinates differ. Consequently any pairwise-disjoint set of at least three such cells is contained in one row or one column. Five of them have as complement the sixth cell in that row or column, an actual left coset `zK` of an index-6 subgroup. Six fill `X`.

If two additional bad-family cosets `uU,vV` lie inside `zK`, the containment lemma gives `U,V<=K`; translating by `z^-1` gives two disjoint actual left cosets inside `K`. If `[K:U]=m`, `[K:V]=n`, the original indices are `6m,6n`, so `6 gcd(m,n)<=7` forces `gcd(m,n)=1`, contradicting the coprime-index lemma inside `K`. Thus five index-6 cosets admit at most one more member. With at most four index-6 cosets, the preceding large-index count gives at most seven total; with six, there is no room at all. This proves `B_7(PSL(2,9))<=7`. Repetition of its index-6 `A5` label makes every finite `J_7` bound false.

### 5.1 The q=7 seven-point action

The remaining small prime case has order 168. We derive a small incidence geometry from the imported `S4` classification; no identification with `GL(3,2)` or imported Fano-plane theorem is needed. Both index-7 `S4` classes are self-normalizing: a larger normalizer would be the whole group, giving a solvable quotient of order 7, contrary to perfectness.

**Degree-seven lemma.** Each action on the seven left cosets of either index-7 subgroup is 2-transitive. Indeed, a Sylow 7-subgroup `P` acts regularly: the point stabilizer has order 24. Its normalizer is the order-21 projective-line Borel. From `diag(t,t^-1) U(s) diag(t,t^-1)^-1=U(t^2s)` over `F7`, the normalizer's order-3 quotient acts on the six nonzero translations with two orbits `{1,2,4}` and `{3,5,6}`, which are negatives of each other. In the regular `P` coordinates, an orbit of the whole group on ordered pairs of distinct points is a union of these two normalizer-orbits; its nontrivial subdegree is therefore 3 or 6. If there were two subdegrees 3, one would orient each unordered pair in exactly one direction, giving an invariant tournament. An involution acting nontrivially on a tournament would swap some two vertices and reverse their directed edge, impossible. The image of this degree-seven action is nontrivial and perfect; if it had odd order, that order would divide 21 and the image would be solvable (its Sylow-7 subgroup is normal), contradiction. It therefore has an involution by Cauchy's theorem. Thus the tournament alternative is impossible and the action is 2-transitive.

Call the first class of seven stabilizers **points**, and choose a subgroup `K≅S4` in the other class. `K` has no fixed point in the first action, since such a point stabilizer has the same order and would be conjugate to `K`. Its only index-2 subgroup is `A4`. A `K`-orbit of size 2 would make that `A4` fix two points, but 2-transitivity gives a two-point stabilizer of order `168/(7·6)=4`, too small to contain it. The `K`-orbit sizes divide 24; with no sizes 1,2 or 7, the seven points split as 3+4. Let the three-point orbit be the **line** stabilized by `K`, and take all seven conjugates of `K` as lines.

Each line has three points. By point 2-transitivity every unordered point pair lies on the same number `lambda` of lines; counting gives `lambda·C(7,2)=7·C(3,2)`, hence `lambda=1`. In particular the seven line sets are distinct. Each point is on three lines by transitivity and incidence counting; every two lines meet in exactly one point, since a line meets the other six through its three points and each pair of points determines at most one line. The action on lines is also 2-transitive by the degree-seven lemma. Because `K` has point orbits of sizes 3 and 4, the group is transitive on incident point-line flags and on nonincident flags separately.

Consequently actual index-7 cosets have two types of transporter:

`P(x,y)={g:g(x)=y}` for points, and `L(a,b)={g:g(a)=b}` for lines.

Each is a left coset of the appropriate stabilizer. Within one type, two distinct cells are disjoint exactly when they share a source or share a target; this follows from 2-transitivity. A point cell `P(x,y)` and a line cell `L(a,b)` are disjoint exactly when the incidence truth values `x in a` and `y in b` differ. If the truth values agree, flag or antiflag transitivity supplies an element in both.

**Six-cell completion lemma.** Any six pairwise-disjoint index-7 cosets leave as complement one actual index-7 coset. To prove it, if all six are of one type, their cells form a single row or column and the missing seventh cell is the complement. If the types are mixed, exchange point/line roles if necessary so at least three are point cells. At least three pairwise-disjoint cells of one type share a row or a column; replacing every element by its inverse exchanges source and target and preserves transporter form, so take them to be `P(x,y)` for `y` in a set `Y`.

If `|Y|>=5`, no line cell can be disjoint from all of them: when `x` is off its source line, all `y` must be on its target line (only 3 points), and when `x` is on its source line, all `y` must be off its target line (only 4 points). Thus the mixed cases have `|Y|=4` or 3.

If `|Y|=4`, any disjoint line cell has source through `x` and target the unique line `b` whose four-point complement is `Y`. The two line cells have distinct source lines through `x`; the remaining third source line through `x`, mapped to `b`, is disjoint from all six and completes the partition.

If `|Y|=3` and `Y` is itself a line `b`, every disjoint line cell has target `b` and source not through `x`, because any two lines meet. There are four lines not through `x`; the one not used by the three line cells supplies the seventh cell. If instead `Y` is not a line, exactly one line `b` is disjoint from it: of the seven lines, `3·3-3=6` meet `Y`, using the three distinct pair-lines. Every disjoint line cell must have target `b` and source through `x`; the three source lines through `x` are all used. The unique point `y0` outside `Y∪b` gives the seventh cell `P(x,y0)`. In each case seven disjoint sets of density `1/7` fill the group, so the completion is exactly the complement. Inverting back, if used, retains an actual left-transporter coset.

Now count a bad family. Any proper index is a multiple of 7 or 8 by the imported maximal list. A subgroup whose index is not divisible by 7 contains an element of order 7; that element acts as a seven-cycle on both points and lines, so every one of its left cosets meets every index-7 transporter. Thus if an index-7 coset occurs, all other indices are multiples of 7. For an index `7m>7`, Lagrange gives `m>1` dividing 24. For two such labels the low-gcd condition says `gcd(m,n)=1`; since their only possible prime divisors are 2 and 3, there are at most two labels of this type, including repetitions.

If at most five index-7 cosets occur, the family has at most seven members. If exactly six occur, their complement is one index-7 coset. Two further cosets inside it would have relative subgroup indices `m,n` with `gcd(m,n)=1`, and after one common translation would be disjoint coprime-index left cosets of that stabilizer, impossible. Seven index-7 cosets already fill the group. Finally, if there is no index-7 coset, at most two indices are multiples of 7 and at most one of the remaining indices is a multiple of 8, so there are at most three labels. This proves `B_7(PSL(2,7))<=7` conditional on the stated small-q classification. Repeating an index-7 subgroup label makes every finite `J_7` bound false.

Combining §§2–5 proposes the stronger all-finite-field statement `B_7(PSL(2,q))<=7` for every prime power `q>=2`. The assigned `f>=2` statement relies only on §§2–4; the newly selected `q=7` proof and its incidence/normalizer steps need their own disjoint check before any broader adoption.

## 6. Actual normal cuts: theorem and exact necessary conditions

Let `G` be arbitrary, `N` normal, `Q=G/N`, and `pi:G->Q`. For a finite-index subgroup `U_i`, put

`a_i=[Q:pi(U_i)]`, `b_i=[N:N∩U_i]`, `n_i=[G:U_i]=a_i b_i`.

The index identity follows from `[G:U_i]=[G:NU_i][NU_i:U_i]` and the second isomorphism theorem. Each projected `pi(g_i U_i)` is an actual left coset `A_i` of `pi(U_i)`. For `q in A_i`, choose **one common** representative `t` of `q`, and choose `s_i in g_i U_i ∩ tN`. Since `g_iU_i=s_iU_i` and `tN=s_iN`,

`(g_iU_i ∩ tN)t^-1 = (s_i t^-1) · t(U_i∩N)t^-1`.

This is an actual left coset in `N` of the conjugated subgroup, of index `b_i`. Using the same `t` for every active label preserves all disjointness; the conjugation cannot in general be dropped. The finite-index coprime lemma and finite-core density lemma therefore apply simultaneously to the active slices. Also, prime-by-prime,

`gcd(a_i,a_j) gcd(b_i,b_j) | gcd(a_i b_i,a_j b_j)=gcd(n_i,n_j)`.

**Conditional lift.** If `B_7(N)<=5` and `J_7(Q)<=2`, then `B_7(G)<=7`. Otherwise an actual bad eight-family has at most two proper quotient labels, since three would have quotient-index gcd at least 8, hence original-index gcd at least 8. The other at least six image cosets are all `Q`. Slice them in one common fibre; their six disjoint actual `N`-cosets have pairwise index gcd at most 7, contrary to `B_7(N)<=5`. This uses neither splitness nor a quotient Helly theorem, and is valid for infinite groups by finite indices.

**Necessary 6+2 form at the original kernel bound.** Assume instead `B_7(N)<=7`, `J_7(Q)<=2`, and an actual bad eight-family. As before, at most two `a_i` exceed 1. If at most one does, all eight projected cosets share a quotient point, yielding eight disjoint bad slices in `N`, a contradiction. Hence, after relabeling,

`a_1=...=a_6=1`, `a_7,a_8>1`.

The two proper image cosets `A_7,A_8` must be disjoint; otherwise all eight have a common fibre. The coprime-index lemma then gives `d=gcd(a_7,a_8)>=2`. For `j<=6` and `k=7,8`, choose a point of `A_k`; the common-fibre slices of labels `j,k` are disjoint, so `2<=gcd(b_j,b_k)<=7`. Likewise `2<=gcd(b_i,b_j)<=7` for `1<=i<j<=6`. The actual original-index constraints remain

`gcd(b_i,b_j)<=7` for `i,j<=6`,
`gcd(b_j,a_k b_k)<=7` for `j<=6`, `k=7,8`, and
`gcd(a_7 b_7,a_8 b_8)<=7`.

Writing `e=gcd(b_7,b_8)`, the product-divisibility fact gives `d e<=7`. Equivalently either `e=1` and `2<=d<=7`, or `(d,e)` is one of `(2,2),(2,3),(3,2)`. These arithmetic restrictions are necessary in addition to actual projected cosets, actual original subgroups and compatible disjoint slices. The fibres used for `A_7` and `A_8` need not coincide. Nothing here constructs, glues, or excludes a 6+2 configuration; replacing the original kernel hypothesis by `B_7(N)<=5` is the separate conditional theorem above. For `q=4,9` and the other small prime exceptions with finite-index labels at most 7, `J_7(Q)<=2` fails, so the 6+2 conclusion cannot be invoked for them.
