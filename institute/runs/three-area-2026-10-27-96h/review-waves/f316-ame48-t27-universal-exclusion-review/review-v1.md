## 5. Independent reconstruction of F1–F7 and excess-three Lemma D

**Original selected evidence:** S2 LF 19–46 and S3 LF 53–90.  
**Status: SUPPORTED.**

Let \(V\) be an arbitrary finite complex unitary matrix whose support has minimum row and column degree two.

### F1: no unique overlap

If distinct rows \(r,s\) overlap in exactly one column \(c\), their Hermitian inner product is
\[
V_{rc}\overline{V_{sc}},
\]
a nonzero complex number. Orthogonality forbids this. The column statement is identical.

### F2: square components and nonnegative excess

For a connected support component with \(p\) rows and \(q\) columns, its \(p\) orthonormal rows are supported in \(q\) coordinates, so \(p\le q\). Its \(q\) orthonormal columns similarly give \(q\le p\). Thus \(p=q=m\), and the component restriction is itself unitary.

If it has \(L\) entries,
\[
r=L-2m=\sum_{\text{rows }\rho}(\deg\rho-2)\ge0.
\]
Component excesses add to the whole matrix’s excess.

### F3: excess-zero components

Excess zero forces every row degree to equal two. Since there are \(m\) columns, all of degree at least two, every column degree also equals two.

A finite connected simple 2-regular bipartite graph is a cycle. For \(m\ge3\), consecutive rows on the cycle overlap in exactly one column, contradicting F1. Thus \(m=2\): the component is a full \(2\times2\) block.

### F4: disjoint degree-two pairs in a larger component

Assume \(m\ge3\). Two degree-two rows cannot share exactly one column by F1.

If they share both columns, their \(2\times2\) restriction has orthonormal rows and is unitary. Its columns already have norm one, so no other ambient entries occur in those columns. The rows and columns form an isolated \(2\times2\) component, contrary to \(m\ge3\).

Therefore all degree-two row supports are disjoint column pairs.

### F5: at least two higher neighbours

Fix a degree-two row on \(C=\{c,c'\}\). By F1, any other row meeting either column contains both. By F4, those other rows have degree at least three.

At least one exists because the columns have minimum degree two. If there were exactly one, \(\sigma\), the two columns would be supported on the original row and \(\sigma\). Their square restriction would have orthonormal columns and hence orthonormal rows. The norm of \(\sigma\) would be exhausted on \(C\), making it degree two, a contradiction.

Thus every degree-two pair has at least two higher-row neighbours.

### F6: counting

Let \(s\) be the number of degree-two rows and \(b=m-s\) the number of higher rows. Their disjoint pairs occupy \(2s\) columns, so
\[
2s\le m=s+b,\qquad s\le b.
\]
Every higher row contributes at least one to the excess, so
\[
s\le b\le r.
\]

### F7: parity on low-row pairs

By F1, a higher row meets each degree-two pair in zero or two columns. If those pairs cover all columns, every higher-row degree is even.

These are necessary consequences of actual complex unitarity, not a realizability claim for graphs satisfying only these conditions.

### Excess one

Components of size at most two have no positive excess under the minimum-degree assumption. For \(m\ge3\), excess one would imply \(s\le b\le1\), hence \(m=s+b\le2\). Contradiction.

### Excess two

Now \(s\le b\le2\), so \(3\le m\le4\).

For \(m=4\), necessarily \(s=b=2\), with both higher rows of degree three. The disjoint degree-two pairs cover all columns, so F7 requires those higher degrees to be even. Contradiction.

Thus an excess-two component has \(m=3\) and eight entries.

### Excess three: exhaustive proof

Here \(m\ge3\) and \(s\le b\le3\), so \(m\le6\). All possibilities are as follows.

- **\(m=3\):** \(L=2m+3=9\), giving a full \(3\times3\) component.

- **\(m=4,\ (s,b)=(2,2)\):** the positive higher-row excesses sum to three, so the higher degrees are three and four. The low-row pairs cover all columns, forcing both higher degrees even by F7. Contradiction.

- **\(m=4,\ (s,b)=(1,3)\):** all higher degrees are three. Let the low-row pair be \(X\), with outside columns \(u,v\). A higher row cannot avoid \(X\), since only two outside columns exist. It therefore contains \(X\) and one outside column. Only three incidences reach \(u,v\), whereas their minimum degrees require at least four. Contradiction.

- **\(m=5\):** necessarily \((s,b)=(2,3)\), all higher degrees three. Two disjoint low-row pairs and one outside column account for all columns. F7 forces each higher row to use one pair and that outside column. There are only three higher-row incidences with the pairs, but F5 requires at least four. Contradiction.

- **\(m=6\):** necessarily \((s,b)=(3,3)\), all higher degrees three. The low-row pairs cover all columns, making every higher degree even by F7. Contradiction.

Therefore:

### 6.2 Common high marginals

**Source:** S1 LF 115–151.  
**Status: SUPPORTED.**

\[
s_i(a)=2d_i(a)+|\{b:(a,b)\in H_{ij}\}|.

\[
h_i(a):=s_i(a)-2d_i(a)
=|\{b:(a,b)\in H_{ij}\}| \tag{2}

**Source:** S1 LF 173–206.  
**Status: SUPPORTED.**

**Status: SUPPORTED.**
