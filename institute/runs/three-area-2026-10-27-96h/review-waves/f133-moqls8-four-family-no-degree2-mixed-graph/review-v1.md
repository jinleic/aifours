## 1. Definitions fixed for this review (from input 1, with input 2's framework)

- **D0 (ordinary QLS, orthogonality, family).** A QLS(n) is an n×n array of unit vectors ("rays") of Cⁿ whose every row and every column is an orthonormal basis (ONB). Members Q, R are *orthogonal* when the n² same-cell tensor products `Q_x ⊗ R_x` form an ONB of Cⁿ⊗Cⁿ; since the products are unit vectors this is exactly `⟨Q_x,Q_y⟩·⟨R_x,R_y⟩ = 0` for all distinct cells x ≠ y. A family of t members is *mutually orthogonal* when every pair is. Isotopy = per-cell phases, row/column permutations, one unitary per member; all of the above is invariant under isotopy, and the candidates' "independent normalization of each member on the chosen row with common column labels" is such an isotopy.
- **D1 (classical / nonclassical, registered sense).** A member is *classical* iff there is one global ONB B of Cⁿ such that every cell ray lies in B up to phase ("per-member global-ONB-ray sense", input 1). *Nonclassical* = not classical. Input 2 records that under the pinned source's definitions one nonclassical member makes the set nonclassical; the candidates' hypotheses are stated for one nonclassical member Q, consistent with this.
- **D2 (reference row, light/mixed, mixed graph).** Fix a row r₀ of Q (the candidates' "first row"); its cells form an ONB B₀ = (q₀,…,q_{n−1}) indexed by column; after the allowed unitary, q_j = e_j. A cell is *light* if its ray equals some e_j up to phase, *mixed* otherwise. The *mixed graph* G(r₀) is the bipartite graph whose vertices are the rows and columns containing a mixed cell and whose edges are the mixed cells.
- **F1 (nonclassical ⇔ a mixed cell exists, for every choice of r₀).** If Q ⊂ B for a global ONB B, then row r₀ ⊂ B and |row r₀| = n, so B = B₀ up to phases and every cell is light. Conversely all-light means Q ⊂ B₀, i.e. classical. Hence a nonclassical Q has at least one mixed cell relative to every reference row, and a classical Q has none. (This step is used implicitly by all four candidates; see M2.)
- **D3 (cycle component, parts, support).** For a 2-regular connected component C of G(r₀): it is a simple cycle C_{2k} (k ≥ 2) with k rows, k columns and 2k cells; its cells split into the two parts P, M of the cycle of cells (consecutive cells share a line). The *support* of a mixed ray is the set of axes j with nonzero component.
- **D4 (transition matrix, basis group).** For a mate R and a cycle component (or a basis group, below) with parts P, M, `U^R_{pm} = ⟨R_p, R_m⟩` (p ∈ P, m ∈ M). Two cycle components with the same support {a,b} are in the same *basis group* when their unordered ray pairs {v, v^⊥} ⊂ span{e_a,e_b} coincide up to phases.

## 2. Reconstruction: the lemma chain actually used by the four candidates (reviewer's own proofs)

- **L1 (degree ≥ 2).** A line with exactly one mixed cell u has n−1 light cells, which are pairwise orthogonal coordinate rays, hence distinct axes; u is orthogonal to all of them, so u lies on the remaining axis and is light — contradiction. So every vertex of G(r₀) has degree ≥ 2. (Stated and proved in candidate 4 §1; used tacitly by candidate 3's corollary, M1.)
- **L2 (degree-2 line).** A line with exactly two mixed cells u, w: its n−2 light cells are distinct axes; {u, w} is an ONB of the 2-dimensional coordinate plane span{e_a, e_b} of the two remaining axes; u, w are not axes, so each has support exactly {a,b}; w is the unique ray of that plane orthogonal to u.
- **L3 (cycle component structure).** Along a 2-regular component C = C_{2k}, L2 applied to each row and column propagates one common support {a,b} to all 2k cells (each cell lies in one row and one column of C). Consecutive cells are orthogonal, and in a 2-dimensional plane the orthogonal complement of a ray is a ray, so the rays alternate v, v^⊥, v, … around the cycle of cells; the cycle is even, so the alternation closes: all k rays of P equal v, all k rays of M equal v^⊥ (up to phase), and v ⊥ v^⊥.
- **L4 (collinear Q-rays force orthogonal mate rays).** For a mate R and distinct cells x ≠ y with ⟨Q_x, Q_y⟩ ≠ 0, orthogonality of Q⊗R forces ⟨R_x, R_y⟩ = 0. Hence R(P) is an orthonormal k-tuple, and so is R(M).
- **L5 (light e_a count and the k-dimensional span).** Let C be a cycle component with support {a,b}, and suppose every mixed cell outside C has zero a-component (automatic when C is the whole mixed graph; "a exclusive to C" in candidate 4). Rows of C contain no light e_a (their light cells avoid a, b). Every row outside C contains exactly one light e_a cell: if it has mixed cells they form a degree-2 pair of another component whose support excludes a, and by L2 the row's light cells are all axes outside that support, including a; otherwise the whole row is light. So there are exactly n−k light e_a cells, in distinct rows; by L4 their R-rays are orthonormal and (since every ray of C has nonzero a-component) orthogonal to every R-ray on C. Hence V_R := span R(C) has dimension ≤ k, and by L4 exactly k; R(P) and R(M) are both ONBs of V_R; the n−k rays at the light e_a cells form an ONB of V_R^⊥.
- **L6 (transition matrix).** U^R is k×k unitary (change between two ONBs of V_R). If p ∈ P and m ∈ M share a row or column of R (exactly the two cycle neighbours of p, distinct cells since 2k ≥ 4), R's line ONB gives U^R_{pm} = 0. So each row of U^R has at most k−2 nonzero positions, and at least one (unitarity).
- **L7 (support disjointness, row pigeonhole).** For mates R ≠ S and p ∈ P, m ∈ M (distinct cells), R⊗S orthogonality gives `U^R_{pm} U^S_{pm} = 0`. The supports of the t−1 matrices are pairwise disjoint; each needs a nonzero entry in row p, which has k−2 admissible positions: **t−1 ≤ k−2**.
- **L8 (complement pigeonhole).** Let b be the other support axis of C. The reference-row cell (r₀,b) has Q = e_b and, in R's own normalization with common column labels, R = e_b. Every ray of C has nonzero b-component and (r₀,b) ∉ C, so by L4 e_b ⊥ V_R, i.e. e_b ∈ V_R^⊥. The ONB of V_R^⊥ from L5 contains R_{(r₀,a)} = e_a, orthogonal to e_b, so e_b has a nonzero coefficient on at least one of the other n−k−1 vectors: B_R := {y light e_a, y ≠ (r₀,a) : ⟨R_y, e_b⟩ ≠ 0} is nonempty. For mates R ≠ S, a cell y ∈ B_R ∩ B_S would give ⟨R_y⊗S_y, R_{(r₀,b)}⊗S_{(r₀,b)}⟩ = ⟨R_y,e_b⟩⟨S_y,e_b⟩ ≠ 0, contradicting R ⊥ S. So the B's are disjoint nonempty subsets of an (n−k−1)-set: **t−1 ≤ n−k−1**. (b need not be exclusive; only a's exclusivity is used, in L5.)

**Candidate 4's case analysis, reconstructed (n = 8).** Under "every mixed line has exactly two mixed cells", G(r₀) is a finite simple 2-regular bipartite graph, so a disjoint union of cycles C_{2k_i}, k_i ≥ 2; K = Σk_i counts mixed rows, and r₀ is unmixed, so K ≤ 7 and there are at most three components. By F1 there is at least one.
- **E1 (exclusive axis).** If some axis a of component C_i occurs in no other component's support, L5–L8 apply to C_i: t ≤ min(k_i−1, n−k_i) ≤ ⌊(n−1)/2⌋ = 3.
- **E2 (all supports equal {a,b}).** Group components by basis pair (D4). Two distinct ONBs of a 2-plane have no zero cross-overlap (if ⟨v,w⟩ = 0 then w = v^⊥ and the bases coincide), so by L4 the R-spans W_g of different groups are mutually orthogonal; within group g the K_g v-cells give K_g orthonormal R-rays, so dim W_g ≥ K_g; the n−K light e_a cells of the unmixed rows give n−K orthonormal R-rays orthogonal to every mixed R-ray; dimension count forces dim W_g = K_g exactly, so both parts of each group are ONBs of W_g. Each group's transition matrix has the two neighbour zeros per row (neighbours lie in the same component, hence the same group) and disjoint supports across mates (L7): t ≤ K_g − 1 for every group. With t = 4 and two or more groups, K ≥ 10 > 7. With one group, L6–L8 apply to the common K-dimensional span (e_b ∈ V_R^⊥ because every mixed ray has nonzero b-component): t ≤ min(K−1, 8−K) ≤ 3.
- **E3 (support trichotomy).** One component: E1. Two components: supports equal (E2) or differ (then one has an axis absent from the other, E1). Three components with no exclusive axis: the three 2-sets, as edges on the axes, have minimum degree ≥ 2; if exactly two distinct sets occur, the set occurring once has an axis absent from the other, contradiction; if all three are equal, E2; otherwise three distinct edges with minimum degree ≥ 2 span at most 3 vertices, hence a triangle {a,b},{a,c},{b,c}.
- **E4 (triangle).** Take C_i, C_j sharing axis a (other axes b ≠ c): every ray of C_i has exactly one common support coordinate with every ray of C_j, with nonzero product, so by L4 W_i ⊥ W_j. The third component avoids a, so the remaining n−k_i−k_j rows (including the third component's rows) each carry exactly one light e_a cell; their R-rays are orthonormal and orthogonal to W_i and W_j; dimension count gives dim W_i = k_i, dim W_j = k_j, both parts of C_i are ONBs of W_i, and L6–L7 give t ≤ k_i − 1. Three components with Σk_i ≤ 7 force every k_i ≤ 3, so t ≤ 2.

Every step above was checked by the reviewer by hand and, where finite, by machine (§4, controls D and E). No step requires any mate to be classical, any phase restriction, or any premise from earlier candidates, the pinned witness or the public source.

If Q is a classical Latin square L with one 2-symbol row/column cycle (or union of cycles) rotated to v, v^⊥ ∈ span{e_{s0},e_{s1}}, then a classical mate R of Q is exactly an orthogonal mate of L (as Latin squares) whose symbol classes meet a rotated s0-cell iff they meet a rotated s1-cell: rotated cells with collinear Q-rays need distinct R-symbols (L4); unrotated cells with L ∈ {s0,s1} may not share an R-symbol with any rotated cell; counting the 16 cells with L ∈ {s0,s1} forces the rotated 0-cells and 1-cells to carry the same symbol set and the unrotated ones its complement, which together with distinctness elsewhere is orthogonality of L and R plus the cycle condition. Symbol relabeling of R is a unitary on R, so transversal decompositions enumerate all classical mates up to isotopy. Verified: all 832 cycle-condition decompositions are mates (addendum v1, B′) and all 4096 pairing-condition decompositions are mates (A′); sampled violators all fail.
