## 2. Elementary tools and prime fields other than 7

### 2.1 Cosets, perfectness, and index bounds

If finite-index subgroups `A,B≤X` have coprime indices `m,n`, every left `A`-coset meets every left `B`-coset. Indeed, `X/(A∩B)→X/A×X/B`, `x(A∩B)↦(xA,xB)`, is injective. Its finite domain has size divisible by both `m` and `n` and at most `mn`; coprimality forces size `mn`, hence surjectivity. Consequently two disjoint actual cosets have noncoprime indices.

For pairwise-disjoint actual cosets of finite-index subgroups, the sum of reciprocal indices is at most 1. For finite groups this is ordinary cardinality; in general pass to the quotient by the intersection of their finitely many normal cores, preserving indices and disjointness. If `xU⊆yV`, then `y⁻¹x∈V` and `U≤V`, so left translation by `y⁻¹` realizes `xU` as an actual left `U`-coset inside `V`.

For a field with at least four elements, the elementary upper and lower unipotent matrices generate `SL₂`: use row elimination, with the Weyl matrix a product of upper and lower unipotents. Choose `t` with `t²≠1`; conjugating `U(s)=[[1,s],[0,1]]` by `D=diag(t,t⁻¹)` gives `D U(s) D⁻¹=U(t²s)`, so `[D,U(s)]=U((t²−1)s)`. All upper unipotents are commutators; conjugation gives the lower ones. Thus `SL₂` and its projective quotient are perfect. A perfect group has no nontrivial solvable quotient; hence it has no proper subgroup of index 2, 3, or 4, as its nontrivial transitive coset action would have image in solvable `S₄`.

### 2.2 All primes p>7

Let `T=PSL₂(F_p)`. Its order is `p(p²−1)/2`, and its natural action on the `p+1` projective-line points is 2-transitive: the upper translations act transitively on the finite points while fixing infinity, and the group is transitive on all points. The Sylow `p`-subgroup `P∞` consists of the upper unipotents modulo scalars. Its unique common fixed point is infinity, so its normalizer is exactly the upper-triangular point stabilizer `B`, of index `p+1`. The conjugates of `P∞` are the root groups at the projective points; any two distinct ones are conjugate as an ordered pair to the upper and lower unipotent groups, which generate `T` by elementary matrix elimination.

Let `H<T`. If `p` divides `[T:H]` there is nothing further to prove. Otherwise `H` contains a full Sylow `p`-subgroup. It cannot contain two distinct Sylow `p`-subgroups, because those generate `T`; consequently its unique Sylow `p`-subgroup is normal in `H`, giving `H≤N_T(P)` and `[T:H]` divisible by `p+1`. Thus **each** proper subgroup index is divisible by `p` or by `p+1`. Both exceed 7. Given any three proper labels, repetitions included, choose one of these two divisors for each; two choices agree, so the corresponding gcd is at least 8. This proves `J₇(T)≤2`, and hence `B₇(T)≤2≤7`. No maximal-subgroup classification is used in this paragraph.

### 2.3 p=2,3

`|PSL₂(F₂)|=6`, so eight nonempty disjoint subsets cannot exist. At `p=3`, `|PSL₂(F₃)|=12`. All subgroup indices divide 12. At most one label in a bad family can have index exceeding 7, since the only such divisor is 12 and `gcd(12,12)>7`. Any hypothetical eight-family would therefore contain at least seven cosets of index at most 6, whose reciprocal-index sum is at least `7/6>1`, impossible. The natural projective-line stabilizers have indices 3 and 4 respectively, so repetition makes every finite `J₇` bound false for both groups.

### 2.4 p=5: an order-60 density lemma

We prove that every perfect group `X` of order 60 has `B₇(X)≤7`. By the preceding index argument, its possible proper indices form a subset of `5,6,10,12,15,20,30,60`; this is an overlist, not an assertion that all occur. Index 1 cannot coexist with another disjoint coset.

If a bad family contains index 5, every other index is divisible by 5 by the coprime-coset lemma. There is at most one label from `10,20,30,60`, since any two have gcd at least 10, and at most one label of index 15. There are at most five index-5 cosets; if five occur they fill `X`, while at most four together with the at most two other labels give at most six.

If index 5 is absent, at most three labels have indices other than 6: at most one from `10,20,30,60`, at most one of index 12, and at most one of index 15. At most six index-6 cosets fit. Six fill `X`; at most four plus the three other labels give at most seven. If exactly five occur, an eight-family would require all three types. The index-15 label forbids 30 and 60 in the first type, leaving 10 or 20. Those three additional reciprocal indices sum to at least `1/12+1/15+1/20=1/5`, more than the `1/6` density remaining after five index-6 cosets. Thus no bad eight-family exists.

`PSL₂(F₅)` has order 60 and is perfect, so the lemma applies. Its projective-line stabilizer has index 6; repeated copies disprove any finite `J₇` bound.

## 3. q=7: exact classification premise and actual transporter geometry

Conversely any index-7 subgroup equals an index-7 maximal subgroup, and is therefore one of the stated `S₄` subgroups.

Every such `H≅S₄` is self-normalizing. Its normalizer contains `H` and has index dividing 7, hence is `H` or `T`; in the latter case `T/H` is a nontrivial group of order 7, impossible as a quotient of perfect `T`. Each of the two conjugacy classes therefore has exactly seven distinct subgroups. Let `Ω` be the seven cosets of a representative `H`, with `T` acting by left multiplication. We now prove this action 2-transitive, for either class, without importing a Fano-plane identification.

Let `P` be a Sylow-7 subgroup. Its intersection with every conjugate of `H` is trivial, so `P` acts freely and hence regularly on the seven points. On the projective line the normalizer of the upper translation group `U(s)` is the Borel of order 21: a normalizer preserves its unique common fixed point. Its quotient by `P` acts on the nonzero translation parameters by multiplication by the nonzero squares `{1,2,4}` in `F₇`. In regular `P`-coordinates on `Ω`, an element of this normalizer acts affinely and acts on a difference by such a square. Consequently the normalizer has exactly two orbits on ordered pairs of distinct points, according as their difference is in `{1,2,4}` or in its negative `{3,5,6}`. These are interchanged by reversing an ordered pair.

Any `T`-orbit on ordered distinct pairs is a union of those two normalizer orbits. If there are two `T`-orbits, either one directs every unordered pair exactly once, yielding a `T`-invariant tournament. A nontrivial involution cannot act on a tournament: it swaps some two vertices and would reverse their directed edge. But the image of `T` in its seven-point action is transitive and perfect. If that image had odd order, its order would be 7 or 21, the odd divisors of 168 divisible by 7. Both are solvable: in order 21 the Sylow-7 subgroup is normal and the quotient has order 3. A nontrivial perfect solvable image is impossible. Thus the image has even order and contains a nontrivial involution, a contradiction. There is only one orbit on ordered distinct pairs: the action is 2-transitive.

### 3.2 Seven points, seven lines, and the intersection criterion

Call the seven objects of one conjugacy class **points**, with stabilizers `H_x`. Take `K≅S₄` in the other class. It fixes no point: otherwise it lies in a point stabilizer of the same order and hence equals a conjugate of `H`, contrary to the two classes. Its only subgroup of index 2 is `A₄` (transpositions generate `S₄` and are conjugate, so the only nontrivial map to `C₂` is sign). A `K`-orbit of size 2 would make this `A₄` fix two points. Yet a two-point stabilizer in the 2-transitive action has order `168/(7·6)=4`, too small to contain `A₄`. Orbit lengths divide 24; no lengths 1, 2, or 7 are possible, so the seven points form a 3-orbit and a 4-orbit.

Associate to each of the seven conjugates of `K` its 3-orbit, called a **line**. Counting pairs of points among these seven indexed 3-sets gives `λ·C(7,2)=7·C(3,2)`, where point 2-transitivity makes `λ` constant; therefore `λ=1`. In particular no two indexed lines coincide, since each contains a pair. Each point lies on three lines by transitivity and counting 21 incidences. Two lines meet in at most one point, and each line meets the other six through its three points, two other lines through each; hence every pair of lines meets in exactly one point. The line action is also 2-transitive by §3.1. The line stabilizer has point orbits of sizes 3 and 4, so the group is transitive separately on incident point-line flags and on nonincident flags.

Write

`P(x,y)={g∈T:g(x)=y}` and `L(a,b)={g∈T:g(a)=b}`,

where `x,y` are points and `a,b` are lines. Each is an **actual left coset**: if `g₀(x)=y`, then `P(x,y)=g₀H_x`, with the analogous formula for lines. Every index-7 coset is one of these cells because every index-7 subgroup is a point or line stabilizer.

Two distinct point cells intersect exactly when their source coordinates differ **and** their target coordinates differ: one direction follows because a group element is a permutation, the other from 2-transitivity. The same statement holds for line cells. A point cell `P(x,y)` and a line cell `L(a,b)` intersect exactly when `x∈a` and `y∈b` have the same truth value. Necessity follows from incidence preservation; sufficiency follows from transitivity on flags and on antiflags. These are assertions about intersections of the actual subsets of `T`, not about their index labels.

### 3.3 Complete six-cell lemma

**Lemma.** Any six pairwise-disjoint index-7 left cosets have as complement one actual index-7 left coset.

First, any set of at least three pairwise-disjoint cells of one type is a single row or a single column. Indeed two disjoint cells share a source or target; if they share a source but have different targets, a third cell disjoint from both must share that source. The target version is symmetric. If all six cells have one type, they are six cells of a row or column; the missing seventh cell of that row or column is the complement.

Otherwise at least three of the six cells are of one type. Interchange the point/line terminology if needed; the incidence properties above are self-dual. By inversion, which preserves disjointness and sends `P(x,y)` to `P(y,x)` and `L(a,b)` to `L(b,a)`, assume all point cells are `P(x,y)` for `y` in a set `Y`, with `|Y|≥3`. A line cell `L(a,b)` disjoint from all of them must satisfy either `x∈a` and `Y` is contained in the four points off `b`, or `x∉a` and `Y` is contained in the three points of `b`. Thus `|Y|≥5` is impossible in the mixed case. We have exactly the following cases.

* If `|Y|=4`, every line cell has `x∈a` and the same target line `b` whose complement is `Y`. The two used source lines through `x` are distinct. The third source line through `x`, paired with target `b`, gives a seventh cell disjoint from all six.
* If `|Y|=3` and `Y` itself is a line `b`, a target line cannot be disjoint from `Y` because any two lines meet. Thus each of the three line cells has target `b` and source not through `x`. There are four such sources; the unused fourth, paired with `b`, is a disjoint seventh cell.
* If `|Y|=3` and `Y` is not a line, exactly one line `b` is disjoint from `Y`: the three points lie on nine point-line incidences, and the three distinct pair-lines account for the only double counts, so six lines meet `Y`. Each line cell must have target `b` and source through `x`. The three source lines through `x` are all used. The unique point `y₀` outside `Y∪b` gives a disjoint seventh cell `P(x,y₀)`.

In every case the seven actual index-7 cosets are disjoint and each has cardinality `168/7`; they fill `T`. Therefore the seventh is exactly the complement. If inversion or dual terminology was used, undoing it still gives an actual left-transporter coset. This proves the lemma.

### 3.4 Complete bad-family count, including cross-class cosets

Let a bad family contain an index-7 cell. An index not divisible by 7 corresponds to a subgroup `U` containing an element `u` of order 7: its order is divisible by 7. Such a `u` is a 7-cycle in either seven-object action because the stabilizers have order 24. For any `gU` and any point cell `P(x,y)`, the seven elements `g u^j` send `x` through all points, so one belongs to `P(x,y)`; the same holds for every line cell. Thus no index not divisible by 7 can occur in this bad family.

Any other index is `7m`, where `m>1` divides 24. For two such indices `7m,7n`, the bad condition says `7 gcd(m,n)≤7`, hence `gcd(m,n)=1`. Since every `m>1` has a prime divisor 2 or 3, at most two such larger-index labels can occur, even with repetitions. If the family has at most five index-7 cells it therefore has at most seven members. Seven index-7 cells fill the group. If it has exactly six, their complement is an actual index-7 coset `zK` by the lemma. Two further cells `gU,hV` would be contained in `zK`; after the common left translation by `z⁻¹`, they are disjoint actual cosets inside `K`, and their relative indices `m=[K:U]`, `n=[K:V]` satisfy `gcd(m,n)=1`. This contradicts the coprime-coset lemma inside `K`.

Finally, if the bad family has **no** index-7 cell, at most two labels have index divisible by 7 by the same relative-divisor argument, and every remaining proper index is divisible by 8 by the imported maximal list, so at most one of those remains. Thus the family has at most three members. The whole-group coset, if present, is already the entire family. This proves the q=7 prime-field candidate `B₇(T)≤7`, conditional on §3.1's explicit source premise. An `S₄` subgroup has index 7, so repeating its label disproves every finite `J₇(T)` bound.

## 4. Actual normal cuts and the unpaid 6+2 obstruction

The identity follows from `[G:U_i]=[G:NU_i][NU_i:U_i]` and the second isomorphism theorem. Each image `A_i=π(C_i)` is an actual left coset in `Q`.

For a quotient point `q∈A_i`, choose **one common** `t∈G` with `π(t)=q`. Choose `s_i∈C_i∩tN`. Then

`(C_i∩tN)t⁻¹ = (s_i t⁻¹) · t(U_i∩N)t⁻¹`,

an actual left coset in `N` of a conjugate of `U_i∩N`, with index `b_i`. For different active labels the same `t` preserves disjointness; the conjugation in this right-translated form cannot be dropped. `gcd(a_i,a_j) gcd(b_i,b_j) | gcd(a_i b_i,a_j b_j)`.

Indeed for each prime, `min(v(a_i),v(a_j))+min(v(b_i),v(b_j))≤min(v(a_i)+v(b_i),v(a_j)+v(b_j))`.
