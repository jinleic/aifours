# Independent author candidate: exact support 48 and 28 maximal entries

**Status: UNADOPTED AUTHOR PROOF CANDIDATE.** The claim below is that no single normalized complex AME(4,6) tensor can have exactly 48 nonzero coefficients of which exactly 28 have modulus 1/6. It is not an exclusion of all support 48, a minimum-support bound, a construction, a source-classification or novelty claim, or an acceptance by a parent or reviewer. The proof is written from the unitarity equations of the same tensor in all three balanced cuts. Its component classification is proved here, not taken from an earlier support theorem. All arguments below require independent checking and a separate parent disposition.

## 1. Actual tensor and residual matrices

Let `psi` be a normalized tensor on four 6-symbol parties and suppose all its two-party reduced states are `I/36`. Set `U=6 psi`. Each of the three 36-by-36 matrices obtained by placing two parties on each side is unitary. Consequently every entry of `U` has modulus at most one. Let `P` be the 28 entries of modulus one and `E` the other 20 nonzero entries. An entry of modulus one exhausts both its row and its column. Thus no two `P` words agree on any physical pair, no `E` word agrees on a physical pair with a `P` word, and deleting the 28 singleton rows and columns in any cut leaves one actual 8-by-8 unitary with precisely 20 nonzero entries, all of modulus strictly between zero and one. Each residual line has at least two entries. We use the actual complex entries, not independently chosen support matrices, in all cuts.

Two distinct rows of a unitary cannot have exactly one common nonzero column, and the same holds with rows and columns exchanged: their inner product would consist of one nonzero complex term. This observation does not say that three- or four-term inner products are two-term equations.

## 2. Component theorem, proved from unitarity

For an arbitrary connected component of a residual support graph, let its row and column orders be `r,c`. The supported `r` rows are orthonormal in `C^c` and the supported `c` columns are orthonormal in `C^r`; hence `r=c=m`. Define the excess `e` to be the number of entries in the component minus `2m`. Equivalently, `e` is the sum of `degree-2` on either side. It is nonnegative, and the total over components is `20-16=4`.

A connected component of excess zero has all degrees two. If a row meets columns `a,b`, every row meeting either must meet both, by the one-overlap prohibition. Each column has only two neighbors; consequently these two columns and their two common rows form a separate full 2-by-2 component. Thus every excess-zero component is exactly a full 2-by-2 block. A component of order two has excess zero because its minimum degree is two.

Now consider a connected component of order greater than two. Call a degree-two row low. Its columns `a,b` have identical row neighborhoods, since a row meeting exactly one would overlap the low row once. Two low rows cannot use the same pair: as orthonormal vectors supported on those two columns they span that column plane, forcing every other row to vanish there and isolating a 2-by-2 block. Therefore different low rows use disjoint column pairs. A twin column pair belonging to a low row has common degree at least three: degree two would give a square unitary 2-by-2 subblock and isolate it, contrary to connectedness. Each pair therefore consumes at least two units of column excess. There are at most `floor(e/2)` low rows. Every other row consumes at least one unit of row excess, so there are at most `e` such rows. Hence

`m <= e + floor(e/2)` for every connected component of order greater than two.                         (2.1)

There is no excess-one component. An excess-two component has order at most three and cannot have order two, so it is 3-by-3 with eight entries: the full 3-by-3 support minus one position. Its row and column degrees are both `(2,3,3)`.

Consider an excess-four component. By (2.1), `m<=6`. If `m=6`, equality throughout the preceding counts gives two low rows and four degree-three rows. The two disjoint twin pairs of columns must have degree exactly three and account for all column excess; the two remaining columns have degree two. Each twin pair meets its own low row and two high rows. A high row cannot meet both twin pairs because its degree is three, so the two pairs have disjoint high-row neighborhoods and together use all four high rows. Each high row has one additional entry in one remaining column. If such a column meets one of the two high rows attached to a twin pair, it must meet the other as well, otherwise its overlap with a twin column is exactly one. Its degree is now exhausted. The two twin-pair groups therefore use different additional columns and form two separate 3-by-3 components, contradicting connectedness.

If an excess-four component appears in the whole 8-by-8 residual unitary, every other component has excess zero and order two. Hence `8-m` is even. Since `m` is neither two nor six, `m=4`. Its row degree multiset sums to 12, and with degrees between two and four the possibilities are `(3,3,3,3)`, `(2,3,3,4)`, and `(2,2,4,4)`. The middle multiset is impossible. Let the low row's twin columns have common degree `k`. If `k=3`, one non-low row avoids both and can use only two remaining columns. If `k=4`, all rows meet the twins, and the two remaining columns have degree two each. The degree-four row meets both remaining columns, while each degree-three row meets exactly one. If the two degree-three rows choose the same column, the other column has degree one; if they choose different columns, those two columns overlap only at the degree-four row. Both alternatives are impossible.

For row degrees `(2,2,4,4)`, the two low supports are disjoint and the high rows are full, so every column has degree three. By applying the same reasoning after transposition, the complete four-by-four possibilities are

* row and column degrees `(3,3,3,3)`; the four missing entries are a perfect matching;
* row degrees `(2,2,4,4)` and column degrees `(3,3,3,3)`; the two degree-four rows are full and the two degree-two rows use disjoint pairs;
* the transpose of the preceding case.

These are necessary shapes; no assertion that every shape admits a unitary is needed. If there is no excess-four component, the only way to partition the total excess four without an excess-one component is two excess-two components; their orders and the remaining zero-excess order are `3+3+2`. Otherwise the orders are `4+2+2`.

For every physical pair, its eight occupied `E` values are precisely the residual lines on one side of a cut, with multiplicities equal to line degrees. Thus every pair system has one of the two types

`D: 3,3,3,3,2,2,2,2`,  or  `Q: 4,4,2,2,2,2,2,2`.                         (2.2)

A `Q` system arises only from a mixed 4-by-4 component: its two 4-lines are full in that component and the four opposite lines there have degree three. In particular its complementary physical-pair system is `D`; two complementary pairs cannot both be `Q`. This statement is about actual components in an actual unitary cut.

## 3. A complex four-qubit obstruction and block consumer

There is no normalized four-qubit pure state whose three two-qubit reduced states are `I/4`. For a purported state, trace out any one qubit. The remaining three-qubit state has purity spectrum `(1/2,1/2,0,...,0)`, so `rho^2=rho/2`. Expand it in the Pauli-product basis as `rho=(I+M)/8`. All Pauli coefficients of weights one and two vanish because the corresponding marginals are maximally mixed; hence `M` is a real linear combination of weight-three Pauli products. The purity equation yields `M^2=3I+2M`. In `M^2` the weight-three part is zero: a product of two weight-three strings has weight three only when their Pauli matrices differ at all three sites, and then the two strings anticommute, so their two ordered cross terms cancel. Equal strings produce the identity. Comparing weight-three parts forces `M=0`, contradicting the same equation. No real-amplitude or phase restriction was used.

We will use this exact **isolated-block criterion**. Pick two symbols at each party. Suppose all four values of every physical pair within the resulting binary box occur in `E`, and each of these 24 pair values has all of its `E` extensions inside that same box. Since no `P` word shares an `E` pair value, the four selected rows and four selected columns in each of the three full flattenings have no entries outside the corresponding 4-by-4 subarray. That subarray is therefore a unitary block in each cut. It is the *same* subarray in all three cuts. Its squared entry norm is four, because one of its 4-by-4 flattenings is unitary; dividing its entries by two gives a normalized four-qubit state, with all three scaled flattenings unitary. This contradicts the preceding paragraph. In every application below we explicitly establish exhaustion of all four pair values in every cut, not just a graph disconnection in one cut.

## 4. Pair degrees, alphabet profiles, and binary slices

For a party `i` and symbol `x`, let `s_i(x)` be the number of `E` words using `x`. For a different party `j`, let `d_ij(x)` be the number of `j` symbols paired with `x` in `E`. Every pair value is occupied in the full tensor: it is a nonzero row of a unitary flattening. An occupied `E` pair value excludes `P`, and every other value in that pair projection is one singleton `P` word. Therefore the number of `P` words using `x` is `6-d_ij(x)`, independently of `j`. Write the common degree as `d_i(x)` and define

`h_i(x) = s_i(x)-2d_i(x) = sum_{y: (x,y) in E} (multiplicity(x,y)-2)`.

It follows that `h_i(x)>=0` and is independent of the partner, and that for every party

`sum_x d_i(x)=8`,  `sum_x h_i(x)=20-16=4`.                         (4.1)

If `d_i(x)=1` for a symbol occurring in `E`, each of the other three coordinates is fixed, so at most one `E` word contains `x`, contradicting the minimum multiplicity two of its occupied pair values. Thus every residual symbol has degree at least two. Let `q_i` be the number of residual symbols. Then `q_i<=4`. If `q_i=1`, the unique symbol would have eight partners in each other six-symbol party, impossible.

If `q_i=2`, the eight `E` values on every pair `(i,j)` fill a 2-by-4 rectangle, so all other `q_j=4`. For each of the two `i`-symbols there are `6-4=2` `P` words, and their other three symbols lie outside the respective four-symbol residual alphabets. These four `P` words form binary length-four words with pairwise Hamming distance at least three, because two agreeing coordinates would repeat a `P` pair. Their six pairwise distances would sum to at least 18, but each binary coordinate separates at most four of the six pairs, so the sum is at most 16. Thus each `q_i` is three or four.

For a `P` word `w`, let `k(w)` be the number of its coordinates in the four residual alphabets. Counting first by one party and then by a pair gives

`sum_P k = sum_i (6q_i-8)`,

`sum_P binom(k,2) = sum_{i<j}(q_i q_j-8)`.                         (4.2)

The second formula uses that the eight residual pair values are all inside the indicated alphabet box and every other pair value is occupied by exactly one `P` word. Since `binom(k,2)>=k-1`, the right side of the second equation is at least the first minus 28. Four 3-alphabets would give `(40,6)` and three 3-alphabets would give `(46,15)`, both impossible. Thus, up to permutation, the only alphabet profiles are

`4444`, `3444`, and `3344`.                         (4.3)

There are at least two 4-alphabet parties. At such a party all four residual symbols have degree two. In profile `3344`, the `E` pair support between the 3-alphabet parties consists of eight cells of a 3-by-3 box: the unique missing cell joins the unique degree-two symbol at either end, called its *star*.

Fix any residual symbol `x` with `d_i(x)=2`. Its slice is a set of `4+h_i(x)` vertices of a binary cube in the other three coordinates. It cannot have `h=1`: five vertices occupy more than four possible columns in each direction, so each direction contains at least one induced cube edge, and the corresponding two unitary lines cannot overlap once; there are at least two edges in each direction. But an induced five-vertex subgraph of the cube has at most five edges. Indeed, the three omitted vertices are incident to at least `9-2=7` of the cube's 12 edges, since among three cube vertices there are at most two edges. It cannot have `h=3` either: a seven-vertex slice has face counts three and four in each direction, whereas a pair system is wholly of type `D` or wholly of type `Q` and cannot contain both multiplicities. As the cube has at most eight vertices, `h` is in `{0,2,4}`.

## 5. Excluding the full cube, then high-symbol tools

Suppose a degree-two symbol `a` at party `i` has `h=4`. Its slice fills a binary cube on the other parties `j,k,l`. Choose `j` to have alphabet size four; this is possible by (4.3), even when `i` itself has size four. The two `i=a` values in each pair `ij,ik,il` have multiplicity four, so those three systems are `Q`. By the mixed-component theorem, each of the four binary values of each complementary pair `kl,jl,jk`, respectively, has total multiplicity three. The `a` cube supplies two extensions of every one of those values.

For either of the two binary `j` symbols, its `ij` multiplicity-four value contributes two to its excess. Since its degree is two and its total excess cannot exceed four, the `Q` structure in fact gives it exactly one 4-cell and one 2-cell: the only two 4-cells of `ij` already belong to `a`. Thus it occurs in six `E` words, four at `i=a` and two outside. The outside words must use each binary value of `k` and of `l` once for each `j` value, because every binary `jk` and `jl` value has exactly one extension left. Similarly every binary `kl` value has exactly one extension left. These four words form one of the two four-word parity codes on the binary `j,k,l` coordinates.

For each fixed `j` value, its two outside words have one common `i` symbol `b_j`: it has degree two toward `i`, one neighbor being `a`. If `b_0` and `b_1` differed, fix either binary `k` value. The actual rows `(i=a,k)` and `(i=b_0,k)` overlap in exactly one `(j,l)` column: the first row contains all four binary `(j,l)` columns, and among the four outside words exactly one with that `k` has `i=b_0`; other words cannot meet a column of the first row. This violates orthogonality. Hence `b_0=b_1=b`.

The full `a` cube and the parity slice at `b` form a binary four-party box. On pairs containing `i`, its `a` pair values have multiplicity four and its `b` pair values have multiplicity two, exactly as supplied by these words. On each complementary pair all four values have multiplicity three, supplied twice at `a` and once at `b`. Every relevant pair value is therefore exhausted, and the isolated-block criterion is a contradiction. Consequently **no degree-two symbol has `h=4`**.

At any 4-alphabet party each symbol has degree two, so (4.1) and the preceding exclusions give exactly two `h=2` symbols and two `h=0` symbols. Call them *high* and *low*. A high symbol has six words in a binary cube and hence misses two distinct vertices. In a `D` pair the two face multiplicities are `3,3`, so the missing vertices differ in that coordinate. In a `Q` pair they are `4,2`, so the missing vertices agree in that coordinate. In particular a 4-alphabet party cannot have three incident `Q` systems: the two missing vertices would agree in all three coordinates. If it has exactly one `D` neighbor, its six-word slice is the Cartesian product of the two symbols at that neighbor with a three-corner subset of the other binary square. The primary-primary corner is present, and only the secondary-secondary corner is missing, where *primary* means the value in the multiplicity-four cell.

If two 4-alphabet parties have a `D` pair, its support is exactly `H x H` plus `L x L`: each high endpoint has two multiplicity-three neighbors, which must be the two high symbols on the other side, and the degree-two low endpoints then fill `L x L`. Thus their high/low bits agree on every `E` word. For a `Q` pair of two 4-alphabet parties, the two multiplicity-four cells form a perfect matching between the two high sets.

We also need a 12-word **sector criterion**. Suppose two specified `h=2` symbols are chosen at each party, and exactly 12 `E` words lie in their binary box, six at every specified symbol. Every box pair value occurs: at a fixed specified symbol the six box words cannot fit into only one pair value, whose multiplicity is at most four. In a `D` pair, the two box values at that symbol must both have multiplicity three. In a `Q` pair, `h=2` permits at most one multiplicity-four cell at that symbol, so the two box values have multiplicities four and two. In either case the four box pair values have total multiplicity 12. The 12 box words thus exhaust all four values of every physical pair, contradicting the isolated-block criterion. This applies equally if a specified symbol belongs to a 3-alphabet party.

### Three synchronized 4-alphabet parties

Assume three 4-alphabet parties have identical high/low bits on all `E` words, and let the fourth party `A` have alphabet size three. Their common high sector has 12 words and their common low sector has eight. If an `A` edge to a synchronized party is `D`, the number of high-sector words with `A=x` is exactly `3h_A(x)`, and `h_A(x)<=2`. If another `A` edge is `Q`, all the `h_A(x)` are even. If instead all three `A` edges are `D`, an `h=1` symbol would contribute three high words while its unique high neighbor in each of the three other parties fixes all three coordinates; at most one such word exists. In either subcase the `h_A` values are `2,2,0`; the two `h=2` symbols each occur six times in the high sector, contradicting the sector criterion.

It remains to treat all three `A` edges as `Q`. Their `h_A` values are even. If some symbol `x` has `h=2`, it has exactly one multiplicity-four cell in each such pair. The endpoint at each 4-alphabet party is high, so `x` has either four or six high-sector words: the four at that 4-cell and possibly two at its other high neighbor. In the four-word alternative, all three high coordinates are fixed by the three 4-cells, impossible for four distinct words. In the six-word alternative, the three pair systems give `4,2` splits in all three directions of a binary cube, forcing its two omitted vertices to agree in all coordinates, also impossible. Since the `h_A` values sum to four, one symbol `a` must have `h=4`.

The two 4-cells from `a` to each of the three high sets give eight high-sector words, filling the binary cube at `A=a`. Each pair among the synchronized parties is `D`: a `Q` pair cannot have support only `H x H` plus `L x L`, because its two high cells form a matching and cannot account for all four high-high cells implied by each high symbol's two neighbors and synchronization. Equivalently, synchronization gives all four high-high values, each with multiplicity three. The cube supplies two extensions of each such value; the remaining four high words form a parity code. For any high symbol of any synchronized party, its two non-`a` words use one `A` symbol, because its degree toward `A` is two. The incidence graph of the parity code through its three coordinates is connected, so all four words have the same `A` symbol `b`. The cube at `a` plus the parity slice at `b` exhausts the `4,2` values on pairs containing `A` and the four multiplicity-three values on complementary pairs. The isolated-block criterion gives a contradiction. Thus **three synchronized 4-alphabet parties cannot coexist with a 3-alphabet fourth party**.                         (5.1)

## 7. Profile 3444

Let `A` have alphabet size three and let `C,D,F` have size four. If the `D` graph induced on `C,D,F` is connected, their bits synchronize and (5.1) applies.

Suppose it has exactly one `D` edge, say `DF`; both `CD` and `CF` are `Q`. The `CA` edge must be `D`, since `C` cannot have three `Q` edges. A high `C` slice is a product of two `A` values and a three-corner square on its two `D,F` alphabets. The `DF` edge synchronizes the high/low bits, so every edge of this three-corner square joins equal bits. The three-edge square is connected, and its primary-primary corner has two high endpoints because the two primary cells are 4-cells. Therefore all its endpoints are high. Every word with `C` high also has `D,F` high. Each individual high sector has exactly 12 words, so these three high sectors coincide, and (5.1) applies.

The remaining possibility is a `Q` triangle `CD,CF,DF`. Complementarity makes all three edges from `A` type `D`. On each `Q` edge its 4-cells match the high symbols. Label the high symbols so that the matchings `CD` and `CF` preserve labels `0,1`. The matching `DF` either swaps or preserves them.

**Swapped matching.** For a high `C` symbol `c`, write `d,f` for its matched high symbols at `D,F`. The three-corner `c` slice contains `(c,d,f)`. Since `d` and `f` are unmatched to one another, `f` is secondary for `d` and `d` is secondary for `f`. The primary-primary corner of the high `d` slice uses `c` and the other high `F` symbol, forcing that other high symbol to be `c`'s secondary `F` neighbor. The same argument using `f` forces `c`'s secondary `D` neighbor to be the other high symbol. Hence every `C`-high word uses high `D,F` symbols. Equality of the three 12-word high sectors gives synchronization and (5.1).

**Preserving matching.** No triple of high symbols with nonidentical labels can occur: at its minority-label party both other values would be secondary, but the secondary-secondary corner is absent. A high symbol's secondary neighbor at either other 4-alphabet party must therefore be low, since its primary neighbor at the third party occurs with it in the three-corner slice. For each label `t`, write `A_t` for the two `A` values in any high slice with primary label `t`. These sets are identical for the three parties: the primary high-high-high triple occurs with exactly the two `A` values from each of their product slices.

Fix two high parties with label `t`. Their shared 4-cell has four extensions. The two common `A` values and their common primary third-party high value provide two; a second common third-party neighbor is needed and must be the same low neighbor for both high parties. Thus for label `t` there are exactly four 4-party triples on `C,D,F`: the all-high primary triple and one triple with a low symbol at each of the three positions. Each has exactly the two `A_t` extensions. The two labels account for 16 distinct `E` words; by the six occurrences of each high symbol, they exhaust all words containing a high symbol of `C,D,F`.

If `A_0 != A_1`, these two 2-subsets of a 3-set meet in one symbol. Counting the 12 words in, say, the high `C` sector via the `D` edge `AC` gives `h_A=2` at their intersection and `h_A=1` at the other two symbols. Some `A` symbol has degree two because its three degrees sum to eight, and a degree-two symbol cannot have `h=1`; it must be the intersection. But the displayed triples make it adjacent, at each 4-alphabet party, to both high symbols and at least one low symbol, so its degree is at least three. Contradiction.

If `A_0=A_1`, each of those two `A` symbols sees both high symbols and at least one low symbol of each 4-alphabet party, so both have degree at least three. The sum of the three degrees is eight and each is at least two; the degrees are therefore `3,3,2`. At each 4-alphabet party the low neighbor associated with label 0 must equal the low neighbor associated with label 1, otherwise either `A` symbol would have four neighbors. On a `Q` edge, the common low endpoint on each side is adjacent to both high endpoints on the other side and has exhausted its degree two. The remaining low endpoint can then meet only the remaining low endpoint opposite it, giving degree at most one. This contradiction completes profile `3444`.

## 8. Profile 3344 with a D edge between the 4-alphabet parties

Call the 3-alphabet parties `A,B` and the 4-alphabet parties `C,D`. Assume `CD` is `D`. The high bits of `C,D` agree, and their common high sector `H` contains 12 words. Whenever a 3-alphabet party `X` has a `D` edge to `C` or `D`, its symbol `x` has `h_X(x)<=2` and exactly `3h_X(x)` words in `H`. If both its cross edges are `D` and `h_X(x)=1`, those three words have fixed `C,D` symbols, so their other 3-alphabet coordinate must take all three values once.

First suppose each of `A,B` has at least one `D` edge to `C,D`. If either has a `Q` edge as well, its `h` values are even and it has exactly two `h=2` symbols; all words of `H` use these two. An `h=1` symbol of the other party, whose two cross edges would both be `D`, would require all three symbols of the first party in `H`, impossible. Thus both parties have two `h=2` symbols, and the sector criterion applies.

If all four cross edges are `D`, suppose `A` has an `h=1` symbol. Its three `H` words use all three `B` symbols, including the `B` star. The star has degree two and hence `h` equal to zero or two. If zero, it has no `H` word. If two, its two neighbors at each of `C,D` are high, so all its `E` words lie in `H`; its pair with the chosen `A` symbol has exactly one extension, contrary to minimum pair multiplicity two. Therefore neither party has an `h=1` symbol, and the sector criterion again applies.

The only remaining cross-edge arrangement, after exchanging `A,B`, has `AC=AD=Q`; complementarity forces `BC=BD=D`. The values `h_A` are even. If they are `2,2,0`, an `h=2` `A` symbol has four or six words in `H`. Four would fix both `C,D` to their multiplicity-four neighbors, leaving only three possible `B` symbols, impossible for four words. Thus its two high symbols account for all 12 words in `H`. An `h=1` symbol of `B` would require all three `A` values there, impossible; both parties have the two-high-symbol sector and the sector criterion applies.

Otherwise `h_A=4` at one symbol `a`. Its two multiplicity-four `AC` cells meet both high `C` symbols, so eight words of `H` have `A=a`. Every `B` symbol has `h<=2`. If `B` has an `h=1` symbol, its `h` distribution is `2,1,1`; each of the two `h=1` symbols has exactly one `H` word at each `A` symbol. The two such words at `a` leave six of the eight words with the same remaining `B` symbol, contradicting the maximum pair multiplicity four. Hence `B` has two `h=2` symbols, and all 12 words of `H` use them. The eight words at `A=a` fill their binary cube with the high symbols of `C,D`. All three pair systems among `B,C,D` are `D`, so each binary pair value has total multiplicity three; the other four words form a parity code. At a high `C` or `D` symbol, its two non-`a` words have the same `A` symbol because its degree toward `A` is two. The parity code is connected through `C,D` values, hence this `A` symbol is constant. The cube plus parity slice exhausts its `4,2` and `3` pair values in all three cuts, contradicting the isolated-block criterion. Thus `CD=D` is impossible.

## 9. Profile 3344 with a Q edge between the 4-alphabet parties

Now `CD=Q`, so its complementary pair `AB` is `D`. Let `a_*` and `b_*` be the stars at the unique missing `AB` cell. From (4.2), `sum_P k=52` and `sum_P binom(k,2)=25`. If `n_j` counts `P` words with `k=j`, then

`n_0+n_3+3n_4 = 25-52+28 = 1`.                         (9.1)

In particular `n_4=0` and at most one `P` word has `k=3`. Four `P` words use `a_*`, because its degree is two. Their incidences with the residual alphabets of `B,C,D` number `(3-2)+(4-2)+(4-2)=5`. Thus one of them has at least two further residual coordinates, hence `k=3` because `n_4=0`. The same argument at `b_*` shows that this unique `k=3` word contains both stars. Exchange `C,D` if needed, so its `C` coordinate is a residual symbol `c_*` and its `D` coordinate is outside the residual alphabet. The two `C`-neighbor sets of `a_*,b_*` each have size two and both avoid `c_*`, because the relevant pairs occur in that `P` word. Two 2-subsets of the other three `C` symbols intersect.

A local fact applies with `C,D` interchanged: no high symbol of a 4-alphabet party can be adjacent to both stars. Its six `E` words would use two `A` symbols and two `B` symbols including the stars, with the star-star pair absent, and two values at the remaining 4-alphabet party. There are only six possible words, so all occur. The star pair at that high symbol then has multiplicity two and the other pair value has multiplicity four, both toward `A` and toward `B`. Both those edges would be `Q`, and with `CD=Q` the high symbol's party would have three `Q` edges, impossible by Section 5.

Choose a shared `C` neighbor `c_0` of the stars. By the local fact it is low and has exactly four `E` words.
