## 2. Singleton entries and the residual unitary components

Write `P` for the 28 entries of modulus 1 and `S` for the other 20 nonzero entries. A unit-modulus entry in a unitary matrix is the only nonzero entry in both its row and its column. Consequently, in every balanced cut the 28 pair values appearing in P are distinct, their complementary 28 pair values are distinct, and no pair value occupied by P is occupied by S. Exactly eight pair values remain on each side. Removing the 28 singleton rows and columns leaves an 8-by-8 unitary with exactly 20 nonzero entries, all of modulus strictly between 0 and 1. Every residual row and column has degree at least 2: a degree-1 unitary line would contain a modulus-1 entry. Two distinct unitary rows, or two distinct columns, cannot have exactly one common nonzero position, since their inner product would have one nonzero complex term.

We now establish the component classification needed for every cut. A connected support component with `r` rows and `c` columns is square: its rows are orthonormal in `C^c` and its columns are orthonormal in `C^r`, so `r<=c` and `c<=r`. For an order-`m` component let its excess be `e = #entries - 2m`, the sum of `degree-2` on either side. The total excess is `20-2*8=4` and every component has nonnegative excess.

An excess-zero component is a full 2-by-2 block. Indeed all its lines have degree 2; the two columns occupied by a row have identical row neighborhoods, because any row meeting only one would have a one-position overlap with that row. Their common degree-2 neighborhood consists of two rows, which have no other entries.

For a component of order greater than 2, call a degree-2 row low. Its two columns have identical row neighborhoods. Two low rows cannot use the same pair: those two orthonormal rows span the two-column space, forcing all other rows to vanish there and isolating a 2-by-2 block. Thus distinct low rows use disjoint column pairs. Each such pair has column degree at least 3: if the common degree were 2, the two columns would themselves be an orthonormal basis on the same two rows, forcing those rows to have no other entries and again isolating a 2-by-2 block. Each low row therefore consumes at least two units of column excess, so there are at most `floor(e/2)` low rows. Each other row consumes at least one unit of row excess, so there are at most `e` other rows. Hence

`m <= e + floor(e/2)`.                                                   (2.1)

There is no excess-1 component. An excess-2 component has order at most 3; it must have order 3 and exactly eight entries, that is, a 3-by-3 support missing one cell. Its line degrees on each side are `(2,3,3)`.

If a component has excess 4, (2.1) gives `m<=6`. It cannot have order 6. Equality in (2.1) would give two low rows and four degree-3 rows. The two disjoint low-row twin column pairs each have degree exactly 3, exhaust all column excess, and hence the other two columns have degree 2. Each twin pair meets its low row and two degree-3 rows. No degree-3 row can meet both twin pairs, so the two pairs divide the four higher rows into two pairs. Each higher row has exactly one entry in one of the other two columns. That extra column must also meet the other higher row of the same twin pair, or it would overlap a twin column in exactly one row. Its degree 2 is then exhausted, so the two groups use different extra columns. The supposed order-6 component splits, a contradiction.

In the whole order-8 residual matrix, every other component besides an excess-4 one has excess zero and order 2. Thus `8-m` is even. A positive-excess order-2 component is impossible, and order 6 was just excluded, so `m=4`. Its line degrees sum to 12 and lie between 2 and 4. The only candidate multisets are `(3,3,3,3)`, `(2,3,3,4)`, and `(2,2,4,4)`. The middle multiset is impossible. Its one low row has twin columns. If those columns have degree 3, one other row avoids both and can use at most the two remaining columns, contrary to its degree at least 3. If the twins have degree 4, the other two columns have degree 2. The two degree-3 rows each use one of those columns and the degree-4 row uses both: if the degree-3 rows choose the same column, the other column has degree 1; if they choose different columns, those columns overlap in exactly the degree-4 row. Both are impossible.

If the degree multiset on one side is `(2,2,4,4)`, its low lines have disjoint supports and its high lines are full, so all four opposite degrees are 3. The transpose gives the converse. If both sides have four degree-3 lines, the missing cells form a perfect matching. If there is no excess-4 component, the partition of total excess 4, excluding excess 1, is two excess-2 components; the residual component orders are then `3+3+2`. Otherwise they are `4+2+2`.

Thus every physical pair projection of S has exactly eight values, with multiplicity pattern either

* **D**: four 3s and four 2s; or
* **Q**: two 4s and six 2s.

Complementary pair systems cannot both be Q. In a Q system its two 4-lines are the two full lines of one 4-by-4 component; the four opposite lines of that component all have degree 3. These are necessary conditions on the three cuts of the *same* U, not independent graph choices. We use the no-single-overlap consequence of complex unitarity throughout; no claim is made that three- or four-term overlaps reduce to two terms.

## 3. The isolated binary-block obstruction

There is no normalized pure four-qubit state with all two-qubit marginals `I_4/4`. To prove this, trace out one qubit and write the remaining three-qubit density operator as `rho=(I+M)/8` in the Pauli-product basis. The one- and two-qubit marginals are maximally mixed, so M is a real linear combination of weight-exactly-3 Pauli strings. The omitted one-qubit marginal is `I_2/2`, so the Schmidt spectrum gives `rho^2=rho/2`, or `M^2=3I+2M`. A product of two weight-3 Pauli strings has weight 3 only if the nonidentity factors differ at every site; then the two strings anticommute, so their contributions cancel in the anticommutator. All other products have smaller weight. Thus the weight-3 part of `M^2` is zero, forcing `M=0`, contrary to the identity. This argument allows arbitrary complex state amplitudes.

We shall invoke the following block criterion. Suppose two symbols are specified at each party, all four pair values on each physical pair occur in the binary subarray, and every such pair value has all of its S extensions inside the subarray. P cannot occur in any of these pair values. Therefore, for each balanced cut, the same subarray is a 4-by-4 matrix block with no entries in its four rows or four columns outside that block. Since the full flattening of U is unitary, the block is unitary. This holds for all three cuts of the same subarray; division by 2 normalizes it and would produce the impossible four-qubit state.

## 4. Marginals, residual alphabets, and two-symbol slices

For party i and symbol x let `s_i(x)` be the number of S words using x, and let `d_ij(x)` be the number of symbols at party j paired with x in S. The P words at x use exactly `6-d_ij(x)` distinct j symbols, so `d_ij(x)` is independent of j; denote it by `d_i(x)`. Set `h_i(x)=s_i(x)-2d_i(x)`. Every occupied pair has multiplicity 2, 3, or 4, so h is nonnegative and, for any j, is the sum of multiplicity minus 2 over x's occupied ij pairs. For every party,

`sum_x d_i(x)=8`,   `sum_x h_i(x)=20-16=4`.                             (4.1)

Let `q_i` be the number of symbols occurring in S. An occurring symbol cannot have degree 1: all three other coordinates would be fixed, permitting only one word, whereas every occupied pair has at least two extensions. Thus its degree is at least 2 and `q_i<=4`. Also q cannot be 1, since one symbol has at most six neighbors but the degrees sum to 8.

If some q is 2, its two symbols each have all four symbols of every other residual alphabet as neighbors, and every other q is 4. The four P words using these two symbols, two at each, have their other coordinates in the two-symbol complements of the residual alphabets. They form a four-word binary code whose pair projections are injective, so every two words differ in at least three positions. The six distances then sum to at least 18, but each of four binary coordinates contributes at most 4, giving at most 16. Therefore every q is 3 or 4.

For `w in P` let k(w) be the number of coordinates in their respective residual alphabets. Every pair slot is occupied by exactly one P word or is a residual pair value. Therefore

`sum_P k = sum_i (6q_i-8)`,
`sum_P binom(k,2) = sum_{i<j}(q_i q_j-8)`.                             (4.2)

Since `binom(k,2)>=k-1`, four q=3 parties would give `(40,6)` for the two sums and require `6>=12`; three q=3 parties would give `(46,15)` and require `15>=18`. Up to permutation only `(4,4,4,4)`, `(3,4,4,4)`, and `(3,3,4,4)` remain. A q=4 party has degree 2 at every residual symbol. A q=3 party has degrees `(2,3,3)`. When two q=3 parties occur, their eight occupied pair values fill a 3-by-3 box minus one pair; its endpoints are the two degree-2 symbols, called the stars.

Consider any residual symbol x of degree 2. Its S slice lies in a binary cube and has `4+h(x)` vertices. If h=1, each of the three face splits is 2 versus 3. Each pair of opposite unitary lines has at least one common nonzero column and hence at least two; consequently the induced five-vertex cube subgraph has at least two edges in each direction, at least six total. But deleting the other three cube vertices removes at least `9-2=7` of the 12 cube edges, so at most five remain. If h=3, every face split is 3 versus 4, impossible in a pair system that is entirely D or entirely Q. Thus `h in {0,2,4}` at any degree-2 symbol.

We next exclude h=4. Let x at party I have its full eight-word binary slice, and choose a different q=4 party J; two such parties exist in every remaining profile. Let K,L be the other parties. Both I-J values at x have multiplicity 4, so I-J is Q and the four binary K-L values all have multiplicity 3 by the component classification. The same conclusion holds for the binary J-K and J-L values, using the Q systems I-L and I-K. Each of the two J symbols meets one of the two I-J 4-cells, so its h is 2 and it has six S words: four with I=x and two outside. The four outside words, across the two J symbols, use every binary J-K, J-L, and K-L pair exactly once, because the x slice supplies two of the three extensions of each pair. Hence they form a four-word parity or opposite-parity code on J,K,L.

For a fixed J symbol its two outside words share one I symbol because its degree is 2 and x is already one I neighbor. If the two J symbols had different outside I symbols, then for either K symbol the rows `(I=x,K)` and `(I=outside_0,K)` would overlap in exactly one `(J,L)` column: the former has the entire binary J-L box and exactly one parity word at that K has the selected outside I. Unitarity forbids this. Thus all four outside words use one common I symbol y. The x full cube plus the y parity slice has, on I-containing pairs, multiplicities 4 and 2 and, on complementary pairs, multiplicity 3 at all four binary values. These multiplicities exhaust all such pair values, so Section 3 gives a contradiction.

Consequently every q=4 party has precisely two h=2 symbols and two h=0 symbols, called **high** and **low**. A high symbol has six words in its binary cube, missing two distinct vertices. For a D edge its two face counts are 3 and 3, so the missing vertices differ in that coordinate; for a Q edge the counts are 4 and 2, so they agree. In particular no q=4 party is incident with three Q edges. With exactly one D neighbor its high slice is the product of the two values in that D direction and three corners of the other two directions; in the two Q directions the absent corner is the secondary-secondary corner.

## 5. High/low synchronization and a three-party lemma

A D edge between q=4 parties has exactly the high-high and low-low 2-by-2 pair boxes: each high symbol has h=2, hence its two D neighbors are 3-cells and must both be high; degree 2 then forces the low-low box. Every S word has the same high/low bit at its two endpoints. For a Q edge between q=4 parties the two 4-cells form a matching of their high symbols, since each high symbol has one 4-cell and a low symbol has none.

We will use this 12-word sector criterion. Suppose each party has two specified h=2 symbols, exactly 12 S words lie in their binary box, and each specified symbol occurs in six of those words. At any specified symbol, six words cannot fit into a single pair cell of multiplicity at most 4, so all four binary pair values occur. A D box has four 3-cells: each of the two values at an endpoint must have multiplicity at least 3 to contain its six words. A Q box has two matched 4-cells and two 2-cells: h=2 at each endpoint permits exactly one incident 4-cell, and six words force it to occur inside the box. In either case the total multiplicity in the four cells is 12. Hence every binary pair value is exhausted by the sector; Section 3 forbids it. Notice that this criterion needs six *sector* words at each symbol, not a blanket claim that a degree-3, h=2 symbol has only six words globally.

Now suppose three q=4 parties X,Y,Z have synchronized high/low bits, and the fourth party A has q=3. There are 12 common-high and 8 common-low words. If A has a D edge to one of X,Y,Z, every A symbol x has `h_A(x)<=2`, since only the two high symbols at the opposite endpoint can be incident with 3-cells, and exactly `3h_A(x)` high-sector words use x. If A also has a Q edge, all its h values are even. If all three A edges are D, an h=1 symbol would have exactly three high-sector words but only one high neighbor in each of X,Y,Z; the three coordinates would all be fixed, permitting one word. Thus the A h-values are `(2,2,0)`. Its two h=2 symbols each contribute six high-sector words and the 12-word sector criterion applies.

If all three A edges are Q, every A h is even and their sum is 4. An h=2 symbol has one 4-cell in each A-to-high-party pair system, and therefore either 4 or 6 words in the common-high sector. If 4, the unique 4-cell in each direction fixes all three other high coordinates for all four words, impossible. If 6, all six are vertices of the high binary cube with 4/2 face splits in each direction, so the two missing vertices agree in all coordinates, also impossible. Thus there is a single h=4 A symbol a. Its two 4-cells in any A-high-party system give eight common-high words, filling the binary cube at A=a. Complementarity of the three A Q edges makes XY, XZ, YZ all D. Each high-high pair has multiplicity 3 and receives two a-extensions; the remaining four high words consequently form a parity code. At each high X or Y symbol those two remaining words have the same A symbol, because the high symbol has degree 2 and already sees a. Connectivity of the parity code across X and Y makes that second A symbol common to all four words. The full cube plus parity slice exhausts all 4/2 and 3 binary pair values, giving an isolated block. Hence three synchronized q=4 parties are impossible with a q=3 fourth party.                             (5.1)

## 6. Four q=4 parties

## 7. Three q=4 parties and one q=3 party

Let A have q=3 and X,Y,Z have q=4. If the D graph induced on X,Y,Z is connected, their bits synchronize and (5.1) applies. If it has exactly one edge, say XY is D, then ZX and ZY are Q, so ZA is D. A high Z slice is a product of two A values with three corners in X,Y. The three-corner graph is connected, and its X and Y endpoints must have equal bits because XY is D. Both primary Q neighbors of the high Z symbol are high, so all four endpoints of this corner graph are high. Every word with Z high therefore has X,Y high. Each of these three high sectors has exactly 12 words, so the three bits synchronize and (5.1) applies.

It remains that XY, XZ, YZ are all Q. Their complementary A edges are all D. Label the high symbols at X,Y,Z so the primary 4-cell matchings XY and XZ preserve labels 0,1. The YZ matching either swaps or preserves them. For each high symbol its six-word slice is the product of two A values and a three-corner box in the other two q=4 coordinates. In that box, each primary direction has count 4 and the secondary-secondary corner is missing.

**Swapped matching.** Fix high `x` and its primary high neighbors `y,z`. Their triple occurs, but y and z are secondary to one another. At y, its primary Z neighbor is the other high Z symbol z'; the three-corner property forces `(x,y,z')` to occur. Thus z' is x's secondary Z neighbor. Symmetrically x's secondary Y neighbor is the other high Y symbol. This holds for both high x symbols; every q=4 pair then contains the entire high-high box and, by degree 2, the low-low box. The three bits synchronize, contrary to (5.1).

**Preserving matching.** A triple of three high symbols with nonidentical labels cannot occur: at the minority-label symbol, the other two are both secondary and their corner is absent. If a high symbol's secondary neighbor at another q=4 party were the other high symbol, its primary neighbor at the third party would give such a forbidden triple. Hence every secondary neighbor of a high symbol is low.

For each label l, the primary high-high-high triple and the three triples obtained by replacing one of its symbols with the relevant low secondary neighbor are the only three-party triples containing a high symbol of that label. To see that the two high endpoints share the same low neighbor at the third party, fix their 4-cell: their respective allowed third-party sets are `{primary high, secondary low}` and each permits only two A values. If their low neighbors differed, their intersection would have one third-party value and the 4-cell could have at most two extensions, contradiction. The 4-cell consequently uses both common third-party values and both A values, so the two A-neighbor sets agree. Applying this to all three high pairs gives one two-element A set `A_l`; each of the four triples has exactly those two A extensions. The two labels give 16 distinct words and exhaust all words containing any q=4 high symbol: for each label and each party, three triples times two extensions account for its six high-symbol words.

If `A_0 != A_1`, the sets meet in one A symbol. On any D edge from A to a q=4 party, their common symbol has h=2 and the other two have h=1. The unique degree-2 A symbol cannot have h=1, so it must be the common symbol. But it sees both high symbols and at least one low symbol at each q=4 party, contradicting degree 2.

If `A_0=A_1`, each of its two symbols sees both high symbols and at least one low symbol at each q=4 party. The A degrees are `(3,3,2)`, so the low neighbor associated with label 0 must equal the low neighbor associated with label 1 at each party. On any q=4 Q edge, both high symbols on each side now connect to the same low symbol on the other side. Those two low symbols have degree 2 and are saturated by the high neighbors. The remaining low symbol on either side has no high neighbor and can meet only the remaining low symbol, contradicting its degree 2. This excludes `(3,4,4,4)`.

## 8. Two q=3 parties: the q=4 pair is D

Let A,B have q=3, let C,D have q=4, and suppose CD is D. C,D high bits synchronize; the high sector has 12 words. If all four cross edges are D, suppose A has an h=1 symbol. Its three high words use all B symbols, including B's degree-2 star. That star has h=0 or 2 by Section 4. The eight a-words fill the binary cube on B-high, C-high, D-high. Since BC,BD,CD are D, every binary pair has multiplicity 3, two supplied by that cube; the other four high words form a parity code. ## 9. Two q=3 parties: the q=4 pair is Q

Now CD is Q, and so AB is D. Let `a*` and `b*` be the stars. For profile `(3,3,4,4)`, (4.2) gives `sum k=52` and `sum binom(k,2)=25`. If `n_j` counts P words with j residual-alphabet coordinates, then

`n_0+n_3+3n_4 = 28-52+25 = 1`.                                         (9.1)

Their pair incidences into the B,C,D residual alphabets total `1+2+2=5`: at B only the missing star-star pair is available to P, and at each q=4 party two of four values are available. Four words with at most two such incidences each must include a 3-coordinate word. Applying the same argument to b* shows that the unique such P word contains both stars. Exchange C,D if necessary and write it `(a*,b*,c*,d_out)`, where c* belongs to C's residual alphabet and d_out is outside D's. Since P and S pair values are disjoint, c* is in neither of the two-element C-neighbor sets of a* and b*. Both sets lie in the other three C symbols and hence intersect.

We use a local fact, with C,D interchangeable: a high symbol of a q=4 party cannot be adjacent to both stars while CD is Q. Its six words use two A symbols and two B symbols, including both stars, and two symbols at the other q=4 party. The star-star pair is missing, so there are at most `3*2=6` words; all six must occur. Its pair with either star has multiplicity 2 and its pair with the other corresponding q=3 symbol has multiplicity 4. Its edges to both A and B are Q, so together with CD it would have three Q edges, contrary to Section 4.

Choose a common C neighbor c0 of both stars; it is low by the preceding fact. Its four words include at least two with a* and at least two with b*, disjoint because the star-star pair is absent. Its two A neighbors are a* and some a, its two B neighbors are b* and some b, and its four words are exactly `(a*,b)` and `(a,b*)`, each with both symbols of a two-element D set T. The stars have degree 2, so T is each star's entire D-neighbor set. Both symbols in T are adjacent to both stars and are therefore low. Let cA and cB be the stars' respective C neighbors other than c0. Since AB is D and the stars have h=0, each occupied AB pair incident to a star has multiplicity 2. Each star's D pair with either member of T has multiplicity 2, one extension already at c0; hence its two remaining words, at cA or cB respectively, use both members of T and the other AB neighbor. If cA and cB differed, each D symbol in T would have three distinct C neighbors c0,cA,cB, contradicting its degree 2. Thus `cA=cB=c1`, and c1 is low because it is adjacent to both stars.

The four low-low CD cells `{c0,c1} x T` each already contain the two disjoint star words and have multiplicity 2. They exhaust both low symbols on each side and all eight low-sector words; degree 2 forces the remaining CD support to be the complete high-high box. ## 10. Conclusion and phase-equation boundary
