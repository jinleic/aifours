# An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold

Fedor Vorobyev Affiliation: Independent researcher Email: <><fedorvorobev@gmail.com>

October 2026

###### Abstract

We prove that a random 3-SAT formula with  [TEX: n]  nn variables and  [TEX: \lfloor 4.268n\rfloor]  ⌊4.268​n⌋\lfloor 4.268n\rfloor clauses is unsatisfiable with high probability.
The satisfiability threshold has attracted considerable attention: successive
upper bounds reached 4.4898 in the work of Díaz, Kirousis, Mitsche, and
Pérez-Giménez, while the cavity method predicts a value near 4.267.
Our bound brings the rigorous upper estimate close to this prediction.

The proof builds on earlier interpolation methods, including the energetic
approach of Achlioptas and Menchaca-Méndez.
We study the smallest number of clauses that any assignment must violate,
comparing the random formula with a simpler system of constraints on
individual variables. This comparison reduces the bound to a finite
calculation, which we verify using exact rational arithmetic.
The proof is formalized in Lean 4.

## 1 Introduction

A random 3-SAT formula is formed by sampling clauses independently.
For each clause, choose three distinct variables uniformly and negate
each independently with probability  [TEX: 1/2]  1/21/2. Repeated clauses are allowed.
As the number of clauses increases, the probability that the formula is
satisfiable decreases. We study where this transition occurs, measured
by the ratio of clauses to variables.

Friedgut [[11](#bib.bib11)] proved that this transition is *sharp*.
As the number of variables grows, the probability of satisfiability drops
from near one to near zero over an interval of clause-to-variable ratios
whose width tends to zero. For example, the ratios at which this
probability falls below  [TEX: 90\%]  90%90\\% and  [TEX: 10\%]  10%10\\% become arbitrarily close.

Sharpness describes the width of the transition, but does not by itself
show that its location settles at a fixed value. The transition may be
centred at a ratio  [TEX: a_{n}]  ana_{n} that depends on the number  [TEX: n]  nn of variables.
Whether  [TEX: a_{n}]  ana_{n} converges to a constant is still open for random 3-SAT.
The cavity prediction is that such a limit exists and is approximately  [TEX: 4.267]  4.2674.267 [[21](#bib.bib21)]. Ding, Sly, and Sun [[5](#bib.bib5)] proved the existence
and the predicted value of a limiting threshold for random  [TEX: k]  kk-SAT when  [TEX: k]  kk is sufficiently large; their result does not cover  [TEX: k=3]  k=3k=3.

We bound the location of the transition from above, without assuming
that it converges. Throughout the paper, “with high probability” means
that the probability tends to one as the number of variables grows.

###### Theorem 1.1.

A random 3-SAT formula in this model with  [TEX: n]  nn variables and  [TEX: \lfloor 4.268n\rfloor]  ⌊4.268​n⌋\lfloor 4.268n\rfloor clauses is unsatisfiable with high probability as  [TEX: n\to\infty]  n→∞n\to\infty.

The proof gives an explicit inequality whose verification uses exact
rational arithmetic. The argument, including this calculation, is
formalized in Lean 4.

#### Bounds on the threshold.

A lower bound identifies ratios below which a random formula is
satisfiable with high probability; an upper bound identifies ratios
above which it is unsatisfiable with high probability. These bounds make
sense even without a limiting threshold constant.

Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ Bounds on the threshold. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold") shows selected results from both directions.
The early upper bound of about  [TEX: 5.191]  5.1915.191 comes from counting satisfying
assignments [[9](#bib.bib9)]. Later arguments lowered it to  [TEX: 4.506]  4.5064.506 [[7](#bib.bib7)] and then  [TEX: 4.4898]  4.48984.4898 [[4](#bib.bib4)]. From below, analyses of algorithms for
finding satisfying assignments reached  [TEX: 3.52]  3.523.52 [[13](#bib.bib13), [17](#bib.bib17)].
The cavity method predicts a value near  [TEX: 4.267]  4.2674.267 [[21](#bib.bib21)].
Our upper bound of  [TEX: 4.268]  4.2684.268 comes close to that prediction; it does not
establish the predicted value or close the gap to the lower bound.

#### Approach.

Interpolation methods compare a random formula with a simpler system
by replacing clauses with constraints on individual variables. Our proof
belongs to the line of work developed by Franz and Leone [[10](#bib.bib10)],
Panchenko and Talagrand [[22](#bib.bib22)], and Lelarge and Oulamara [[20](#bib.bib20)].
These methods also supply the upper-bound part of the large- [TEX: k]  kk result
of Ding, Sly, and Sun [[5](#bib.bib5)].

A particularly close predecessor is the energetic interpolation of
Achlioptas and Menchaca-Méndez [[1](#bib.bib1)]. They work directly with the
minimum number of violated constraints and compare it with the minimum
for a system of one-variable constraints. Their trial model is controlled
by a single probability. Here we use a distribution of warning rates
and an exponential weight for the minimum violation count. We average
over auxiliary warnings before taking a logarithm. The resulting bound
can be verified by a finite calculation with exact rational arithmetic.
The proof below establishes this comparison directly and formalizes it
in Lean.

#### Proof outline.

For a formula  [TEX: F]  FF, consider the number of clauses violated by each truth
assignment. We denote the smallest of these numbers by  [TEX: \mathcal{E}(F)]  ℰ⁡(F)\mathcal{E}(F).
Thus  [TEX: \mathcal{E}(F)=0]  ℰ⁡(F)=0\mathcal{E}(F)=0 exactly when  [TEX: F]  FF is satisfiable. We will show that, for
a random formula at the stated density, this minimum is positive with
high probability.

Set  [TEX: \alpha=4.268=1067/250]  α=4.268=1067/250\alpha=4.268=1067/250. We first work with a simpler random model:
variables may repeat within a clause, and the number of clauses has a
Poisson distribution with mean  [TEX: \alpha n]  α​n\alpha n. We compare these formulas
with a system of random constraints, each involving just one variable.
The comparison gives an inequality for the expected minimum number of
violated clauses. An explicit calculation, verified using exact rational
arithmetic, shows that this expectation grows at least linearly with  [TEX: n]  nn.

We then return to a fixed number  [TEX: \lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]  ⌊α​n⌋\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor of clauses.
The expected minimum remains at least  [TEX: cn]  c​ncn for some constant  [TEX: c&gt;0]  c>0c>0.
Changing one clause changes the minimum by at most one, which lets us
bound its variance by  [TEX: \alpha n]  α​n\alpha n. Chebyshev’s inequality then shows
that the probability of a zero minimum is  [TEX: O(1/n)]  O⁡(1/n)O(1/n). Finally, conditioning
on every clause having three distinct variables increases this bound
by at most a constant factor.

## 2 Counting violated constraints and the comparison model

### 2.1 Adding one constraint

### 2.2 Replacing clauses with one-variable constraints

Imagine replacing the second and third literals of a clause by independent
coin tosses. If both come up “false,” the first literal must be true to
avoid a violation. Otherwise, there is no constraint. We call this random
one-variable constraint a *site*, following Lelarge and
Oulamara [[20](#bib.bib20), Section 4]. Such one-variable penalties also appear as
univariate factors in Achlioptas and Menchaca-Méndez [[1](#bib.bib1), Section 4].
Our comparison gradually replaces clauses by sites. Once only sites remain,
each variable can be chosen separately to minimize the number of violations.

Here is the precise construction. Start with a random clause on  [TEX: n\geq 1]  n≥1n\geq 1 variables. Choose its three variable indices  [TEX: i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}]  i1,i2,i3i_{1},i_{2},i_{3} independently
and uniformly from  [TEX: \{1,\ldots,n\}]  {1,…,n}\\{1,\ldots,n\\}, and choose each literal’s sign by a
fair coin toss. Write  [TEX: J_{j}]  JjJ_{j} for the value of variable  [TEX: i_{j}]  iji_{j} that makes
literal  [TEX: j]  jj false:  [TEX: J_{j}=0]  Jj=0J_{j}=0 for a positive literal and  [TEX: J_{j}=1]  Jj=1J_{j}=1 for a
negative literal. The clause’s violation indicator is

|  |  [TEX: C_{x}(\sigma)=\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i_{1}}=J_{1}\}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i_{2}}=J_{2}\}\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i_{3}}=J_{3}\}.]  Cx(σ)=𝟏{σi1=J1}𝟏{σi2=J2}𝟏{σi3=J3}.C_{x}(\sigma)=\mathbf{1}\\{\sigma_{i_{1}}=J_{1}\\}\mathbf{1}\\{\sigma_{i_{2}}=J_{2}\\}\mathbf{1}\\{\sigma_{i_{3}}=J_{3}\\}. |  |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |

In this auxiliary model the indices may repeat; we return to clauses
with three distinct variables in Section [7](#S7 "7 Fixed clause count and three distinct variables per clause ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold").

The replacement tosses need not be fair. For each position  [TEX: j]  jj, independently
choose a probability  [TEX: R_{j}]  RjR_{j} from the distribution

|  |  [TEX: \mathbb{P}(R=1/20)=1/4,\qquad\mathbb{P}(R=2/5)=1/2,\qquad\mathbb{P}(R=17/20)=1/4.]  ℙ⁡(R=1/20)=1/4,ℙ⁡(R=2/5)=1/2,ℙ⁡(R=17/20)=1/4.\mathbb{P}(R=1/20)=1/4,\qquad\mathbb{P}(R=2/5)=1/2,\qquad\mathbb{P}(R=17/20)=1/4. |  | (3) |

|  |  [TEX: S_{x,W}(\sigma)=\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i_{1}}=J_{1}\}W_{2}W_{3}.]  Sx,W(σ)=𝟏{σi1=J1}W2W3.S_{x,W}(\sigma)=\mathbf{1}\\{\sigma_{i_{1}}=J_{1}\\}W_{2}W_{3}. |  |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |

Thus the site penalizes a false first literal with probability  [TEX: R_{2}R_{3}]  R2​R3R_{2}R_{3},
and otherwise contributes zero. We refer to  [TEX: W_{2},W_{3}]  W2,W3W_{2},W_{3} as auxiliary *warnings*. Their probabilities are chosen for the comparison;
they are not inferred from the original formula. The particular
distribution ([3](#S2.E3 "In 2.2 Replacing clauses with one-variable constraints ‣ 2 Counting violated constraints and the comparison model ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold")) gives the numerical bound verified in

For the calculations below, write  [TEX: x_{j}=(i_{j},J_{j},R_{j})]  xj=(ij,Jj,Rj)x_{j}=(i_{j},J_{j},R_{j}) and call this

### 2.3 The quantity we compare

Consider  [TEX: K]  KK clauses and  [TEX: L]  LL sites, with all literals and coin probabilities
already chosen. Tossing the coins determines which sites impose a constraint.
For each outcome, we find the minimum number of violations over all assignments.
Write  [TEX: a]  aa for the collection of coin outcomes and  [TEX: H_{a}(\sigma)]  Ha​(σ)H_{a}(\sigma) for the
number of constraints violated by assignment  [TEX: \sigma]  σ\sigma. The minimum is  [TEX: E_{a}=\min_{\sigma}H_{a}(\sigma)]  Ea=minσ⁡Ha​(σ)E_{a}=\min_{\sigma}H_{a}(\sigma).

We first average the weight  [TEX: e^{-yE_{a}}]  e−y​Eae^{-yE_{a}} over the coin tosses, keeping the
literals and coin probabilities fixed. Call this average  [TEX: Z_{y}]  ZyZ_{y}, and its
logarithm  [TEX: \Phi_{y}]  Φy\Phi_{y}. We then average  [TEX: \Phi_{y}]  Φy\Phi_{y} over the choices of literals
and coin probabilities—the rows defined above—to obtain  [TEX: f(K,L)]  f⁡(K,L)f(K,L):

|  |  [TEX: Z_{y}=\mathbb{E}_{a}e^{-yE_{a}},\qquad\Phi_{y}=\log Z_{y},\qquad f(K,L)=\mathbb{E}_{\rm rows}\Phi_{y}.]  Zy=𝔼a​e−y​Ea,Φy=log⁡Zy,f⁡(K,L)=𝔼rows​Φy.Z_{y}=\mathbb{E}_{a}e^{-yE_{a}},\qquad\Phi_{y}=\log Z_{y},\qquad f(K,L)=\mathbb{E}_{\rm rows}\Phi_{y}. |  | (4) |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |

Here  [TEX: \mathbb{E}_{a}]  𝔼a\mathbb{E}_{a} averages only over the independent coin tosses with their
chosen probabilities;  [TEX: \mathbb{E}_{\rm rows}]  𝔼rows\mathbb{E}_{\rm rows} averages over the rows.
The order matters: we take the logarithm between the two averages.
The quantity  [TEX: f(K,L)]  f⁡(K,L)f(K,L) lets us compare systems with different numbers
of clauses and sites.

We will use two simple bounds. At most  [TEX: K+L]  K+LK+L constraints can be violated,
so  [TEX: 0\leq E_{a}\leq K+L]  0≤Ea≤K+L0\leq E_{a}\leq K+L and hence

|  |  [TEX: -y(K+L)\leq\Phi_{y}\leq 0.]  −y⁡(K+L)≤Φy≤0.-y(K+L)\leq\Phi_{y}\leq 0. |  | (5) |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | --- |

Also, adding one clause or site increases the minimum by at most one
for every outcome of the tosses. The weight  [TEX: e^{-yE_{a}}]  e−y​Eae^{-yE_{a}} therefore
decreases by a factor between  [TEX: e^{-y}]  e−ye^{-y} and  [TEX: 1]  11, as does its average  [TEX: Z_{y}]  ZyZ_{y}.
Taking logarithms, the change in  [TEX: \Phi_{y}]  Φy\Phi_{y} lies in  [TEX: [-y,0]]  [−y,0][-y,0].
The same bound holds after averaging over the rows, and thus for  [TEX: f]  ff.
All the averages in this section are finite sums.

## 3 The local cubic inequality

We compare the effects of adding a clause and adding a site so that we
can replace the original formula by a simpler system while keeping a
bound on its minimum number of violations. Once only sites remain,
this bound can be computed explicitly. The key comparison follows from
a cubic polynomial that factors as  [TEX: (B-C)^{2}(B+2C)\geq 0]  (B−C)2​(B+2​C)≥0(B-C)^{2}(B+2C)\geq 0 for  [TEX: B,C\geq 0]  B,C≥0B,C\geq 0.
This inequality controls how our comparison quantity changes during
the replacement.

Keep the literals and coin probabilities fixed. We now reweight the
possible coin outcomes: multiply the probability of each outcome  [TEX: a]  aa by  [TEX: e^{-yE_{a}}]  e−y​Eae^{-yE_{a}}, then divide by  [TEX: Z_{y}]  ZyZ_{y} so that the probabilities sum to one.
This defines

|  |  [TEX: \mu_{y}(a)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(a)e^{-yE_{a}}}{Z_{y}}.]  μy​(a)=ℙ⁡(a)​e−y​EaZy.\mu_{y}(a)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(a)e^{-yE_{a}}}{Z_{y}}. |  |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |

Here  [TEX: \mathbb{P}(a)]  ℙ⁡(a)\mathbb{P}(a) is the probability of the coin outcomes with the rows

###### Lemma 3.1.

## 4 From clauses to sites with Poisson counts

## 5 The site-only system and its moments

To bound the site-only quantity in Section 4, we calculate the probability
that a variable receives conflicting constraints: some active sites
penalize zero and others penalize one. Such a variable must violate at
least one site, whichever value we assign to it.

### 5.1 Conflicting constraints

Hold the site rows fixed. Let  [TEX: \mathcal{S}_{v,s}]  𝒮v,s\mathcal{S}_{v,s} be the set of sites
whose retained variable is  [TEX: v]  vv and whose penalized value is  [TEX: s\in\{0,1\}]  s∈{0,1}s\in\\{0,1\\}.
Let  [TEX: N_{v,s}]  Nv,sN_{v,s} count the active sites in this set, meaning those for which
both replacement coins come up “false.”
Every assignment violates at least one site at a variable where both  [TEX: N_{v,0}]  Nv,0N_{v,0} and  [TEX: N_{v,1}]  Nv,1N_{v,1} are positive. Therefore the minimum number of violated sites
is at least the number of such variables.

Let  [TEX: A_{v}]  AvA_{v} be the probability, over the coin tosses, that no active site
penalizes zero at  [TEX: v]  vv, and let  [TEX: B_{v}]  BvB_{v} be the corresponding probability for
one. Let  [TEX: G_{v}]  GvG_{v} be the probability that at least one of these values is
unpenalized. Then

|  |  [TEX: A_{v}=\prod_{\mathcal{S}_{v,0}}(1-R_{2}R_{3}),\quad B_{v}=\prod_{\mathcal{S}_{v,1}}(1-R_{2}R_{3}),\quad G_{v}=A_{v}+B_{v}-A_{v}B_{v}.]  Av=∏𝒮v,0(1−R2​R3),Bv=∏𝒮v,1(1−R2​R3),Gv=Av+Bv−Av​Bv.A_{v}=\prod_{\mathcal{S}_{v,0}}(1-R_{2}R_{3}),\quad B_{v}=\prod_{\mathcal{S}_{v,1}}(1-R_{2}R_{3}),\quad G_{v}=A_{v}+B_{v}-A_{v}B_{v}. |  |

|  |  [TEX: \Phi_{y}\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}\log\bigl(G_{v}+(1-G_{v})\delta\bigr).]  Φy≤∑v=1nlog⁡(Gv+(1−Gv)​δ).\Phi_{y}\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}\log\bigl(G_{v}+(1-G_{v})\delta\bigr). |  | (14) |

## 7 Fixed clause count and three distinct variables per clause

We now return from the auxiliary model to a fixed number of clauses,
each containing three distinct variables, and prove unsatisfiability
with high probability. Let  [TEX: F_{n}]  FnF_{n} denote the random formula in
Theorem [1.1](#S1.Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1.1. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold").
Choose one finite  [TEX: y&gt;0]  y>0y>0 such that  [TEX: 128e^{-y}\leq\varepsilon/4]  128​e−y≤ε/4128e^{-y}\leq\varepsilon/4, and keep
it fixed as  [TEX: n]  nn grows. For example,  [TEX: y=\log(512\cdot 10^{5})]  y=log⁡(512⋅105)y=\log(512\cdot 10^{5}) gives  [TEX: 128e^{-y}=\varepsilon/4]  128​e−y=ε/4128e^{-y}=\varepsilon/4 exactly. Let  [TEX: M=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]  M=⌊α​n⌋M=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor and  [TEX: N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\alpha n)]  N∼Poisson⁡(α​n)N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\alpha n). Generate one infinite sequence of independent
clauses and compare the formulas formed by its first  [TEX: N]  NN and first  [TEX: M]  MM clauses. Each additional clause changes the minimum violation count by
at most one, so

|  |  [TEX: \displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{N})-\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M})\bigr|]  |𝔼​ℰ​(FNrep)−𝔼​ℰ​(FMrep)|\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{N})-\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M})\bigr| |  [TEX: \displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}|N-M|]  ≤𝔼​|N−M|\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}|N-M| |  |      |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |
|  |                                                                                                                                                                                                          |  [TEX: \displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(N-M)^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\alpha n+1}.]  ≤𝔼​(N−M)2≤α​n+1.\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(N-M)^{2}}\leq\sqrt{\alpha n+1}. |  | (24) |

Here  [TEX: \mathbb{E}(N-M)^{2}=\alpha n+(\alpha n-M)^{2}]  𝔼​(N−M)2=α​n+(α​n−M)2\mathbb{E}(N-M)^{2}=\alpha n+(\alpha n-M)^{2}. Equations
([21](#S5.E21 "In Proposition 5.2. ‣ 5.2 Mixed Poisson moments ‣ 5 The site-only system and its moments ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold")), ([22](#S6.E22 "In 6 The rational certificate ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold")), and ([24](#S7.E24 "In 7 Fixed clause count and three distinct variables per clause ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold")) yield

|  |  [TEX: -\frac{y}{n}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M})\leq-\varepsilon+128e^{-y}+\frac{y}{n}\sqrt{\alpha n+1}.]  −yn​𝔼​ℰ​(FMrep)≤−ε+128​e−y+yn​α​n+1.-\frac{y}{n}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M})\leq-\varepsilon+128e^{-y}+\frac{y}{n}\sqrt{\alpha n+1}. |  |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |

Eventually the last term is at most  [TEX: \varepsilon/4]  ε/4\varepsilon/4, so, for  [TEX: c=\varepsilon/(2y)&gt;0]  c=ε/(2​y)>0c=\varepsilon/(2y)>0,

|  |  [TEX: \mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M})\geq cn]  𝔼​ℰ​(FMrep)≥c​n\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M})\geq cn |  | (25) |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |

for all sufficiently large  [TEX: n]  nn. This fixes  [TEX: y]  yy before taking  [TEX: n\to\infty]  n→∞n\to\infty;
there is no exchange of two limits.

A positive expected minimum alone does not establish unsatisfiability
with high probability. We must also bound fluctuations around that mean.
Replacing one clause changes every assignment’s violation count by at
most one, hence changes its minimum by at most one. Reveal the  [TEX: M]  MM clauses
one at a time. After the first  [TEX: j]  jj clauses are revealed, take the
conditional expectation of the final minimum  [TEX: \mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M})]  ℰ⁡(FMrep)\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M}).
This sequence of conditional expectations is the Doob martingale. Conditional on the preceding clauses, the
conditional expectations corresponding to different values of the next
clause differ by at most one: use the same unrevealed clauses in both cases.
Each martingale increment therefore has absolute value at most one.
Each increment has conditional mean zero. Distinct increments have zero
covariance, so their variances add, giving

|  |  [TEX: \operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M}))\leq M\leq\alpha n.]  Var⁡(ℰ⁡(FMrep))≤M≤α​n.\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M}))\leq M\leq\alpha n. |  | (26) |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |

The formal proof uses the equivalent finite-product variance decomposition.
Chebyshev’s inequality and ([25](#S7.E25 "In 7 Fixed clause count and three distinct variables per clause ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold")) imply eventually

|  |  [TEX: \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M})=0)\leq\frac{\alpha}{c^{2}n}.]  ℙ⁡(ℰ⁡(FMrep)=0)≤αc2​n.\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}(F^{\rm rep}_{M})=0)\leq\frac{\alpha}{c^{2}n}. |  | (27) |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |

Let  [TEX: A]  AA be the event that each of the  [TEX: M]  MM clauses has distinct variable
positions. Conditional on  [TEX: A]  AA, each clause has three uniformly chosen
distinct variables, and clauses and signs remain independent, with fair
signs. This is exactly the original model. Conditioning increases our
probability bound by at most the constant below: for  [TEX: n\geq 6]  n≥6n\geq 6,

|  |  [TEX: \mathbb{P}(A)^{-1}=\left(\frac{n^{2}}{(n-1)(n-2)}\right)^{M}\leq e^{6M/n}\leq e^{6\alpha}.]  ℙ​(A)−1=(n2(n−1)​(n−2))M≤e6​M/n≤e6​α.\mathbb{P}(A)^{-1}=\left(\frac{n^{2}}{(n-1)(n-2)}\right)^{M}\leq e^{6M/n}\leq e^{6\alpha}. |  | (28) |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |

Indeed, for  [TEX: r=n^{2}/((n-1)(n-2))]  r=n2/((n−1)​(n−2))r=n^{2}/((n-1)(n-2)), use  [TEX: \log r\leq r-1\leq 6/n]  log⁡r≤r−1≤6/n\log r\leq r-1\leq 6/n; the latter inequality reduces to  [TEX: 3n^{2}-16n+12\geq 0]  3​n2−16​n+12≥03n^{2}-16n+12\geq 0, valid for  [TEX: n\geq 6]  n≥6n\geq 6.
Since  [TEX: \mathbb{P}(B\mid A)\leq\mathbb{P}(B)/\mathbb{P}(A)]  ℙ⁡(B∣A)≤ℙ⁡(B)/ℙ⁡(A)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)\leq\mathbb{P}(B)/\mathbb{P}(A) for any event  [TEX: B]  BB,
([27](#S7.E27 "In 7 Fixed clause count and three distinct variables per clause ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold")) and ([28](#S7.E28 "In 7 Fixed clause count and three distinct variables per clause ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold")) give

|  |  [TEX: \mathbb{P}(F_{n}\text{ is satisfiable})\leq e^{6\alpha}\frac{\alpha}{c^{2}n}\longrightarrow 0.]  ℙ⁡(Fn​ is satisfiable)≤e6​α​αc2​n⟶0.\mathbb{P}(F_{n}\text{ is satisfiable})\leq e^{6\alpha}\frac{\alpha}{c^{2}n}\longrightarrow 0. |  |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |

This proves Theorem [1.1](#S1.Thmtheorem1 "Theorem 1.1. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold"). The conditioning constant is deliberately
coarse; the argument supplies an asymptotic conclusion, not a useful
small-instance estimate.

The conclusion also holds when repeated clauses are forbidden.
There are  [TEX: N_{n}=8\binom{n}{3}]  Nn=8​(n3)N_{n}=8\binom{n}{3} distinct signed clauses, ignoring the order
of their literals. Among  [TEX: M=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor]  M=⌊α​n⌋M=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor independent samples,
the probability of any repetition is at most  [TEX: \binom{M}{2}/N_{n}=O(1/n)]  (M2)/Nn=O⁡(1/n)\binom{M}{2}/N_{n}=O(1/n).
Conditioning on no repetitions gives uniform sampling without replacement,
and divides the satisfiability bound by a probability tending to one.

## 8 Relation to the partition-moment formulation

This section connects our proof to earlier interpolation formulas;
it is not needed to prove the main theorem.
Keep the same cost function  [TEX: H_{a}]  HaH_{a} and the same probabilities for the
auxiliary coin outcomes. For fixed outcomes  [TEX: a]  aa, define the *partition function*

|  |  [TEX: Z_{\beta}(a)=\sum_{\sigma}e^{-\beta H_{a}(\sigma)},\qquad\beta\geq 0.]  Zβ​(a)=∑σe−β​Ha​(σ),β≥0.Z_{\beta}(a)=\sum_{\sigma}e^{-\beta H_{a}(\sigma)},\qquad\beta\geq 0. |  |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |

The parameter  [TEX: \beta]  β\beta penalizes violations: larger values give less
weight to assignments with more violations. Unlike  [TEX: Z_{y}]  ZyZ_{y}, which averages
a weight of the minimum over coin outcomes,  [TEX: Z_{\beta}(a)]  Zβ​(a)Z_{\beta}(a) sums weights
over all assignments for fixed coin outcomes. There are  [TEX: 2^{n}]  2n2^{n} assignments,
and at least one attains the minimum, so

|  |  [TEX: e^{-\beta E_{a}}\leq Z_{\beta}(a)\leq 2^{n}e^{-\beta E_{a}}.]  e−β​Ea≤Zβ​(a)≤2n​e−β​Ea.e^{-\beta E_{a}}\leq Z_{\beta}(a)\leq 2^{n}e^{-\beta E_{a}}. |  |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |

Introduce an exponent  [TEX: m&gt;0]  m>0m>0. Raise the bounds to this power, average
over the auxiliary coin outcomes, and take logarithms (with  [TEX: \beta&gt;0]  β>0\beta>0):

|  |  [TEX: \Phi_{m\beta}\leq\log\mathbb{E}_{a}[Z_{\beta}(a)^{m}]\leq\Phi_{m\beta}+mn\log 2.]  Φm​β≤log⁡𝔼a​[Zβ​(a)m]≤Φm​β+m​n​log⁡2.\Phi_{m\beta}\leq\log\mathbb{E}_{a}[Z_{\beta}(a)^{m}]\leq\Phi_{m\beta}+mn\log 2. |  | (29) |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- | ---- |

Thus at fixed  [TEX: m\beta=y]  m​β=ym\beta=y, the difference between the middle expression
and  [TEX: \Phi_{y}]  Φy\Phi_{y}, divided by  [TEX: n]  nn, is at most  [TEX: m\log 2]  m​log⁡2m\log 2.
This two-sided bound, also formalized in Lean, relates our comparison
quantity to the logarithm of an averaged power of a partition function,
as in
Franz–Leone [[10](#bib.bib10), equation (45)].

The comparison in Section 4 retains the clause/site balance of their
Poisson interpolation [[10](#bib.bib10), equations (51)–(54)], but proves it
using finite replacement. Equation ([29](#S8.E29 "In 8 Relation to the partition-moment formulation ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold")) uses an identical
distribution of auxiliary coin outcomes on its two sides; it does not
identify that distribution with the reweighted measure in their
equation (46). Nor do we claim the exact
integer-cost implementation appears in that paper. The direct insertion
identity is sufficient here, at the cost of restricting the argument to
integer unit costs rather than a general finite-temperature model.
For clauses with an odd number of literals, the cited Franz–Leone
preprint makes additional assumptions about overlaps, quantities
measuring correlations between assignments. Our comparison uses the
explicitly nonnegative quantities  [TEX: B_{h},C_{h}]  Bh,ChB_{h},C_{h} from Section 3, for which
the cubic inequality holds directly without such assumptions.

## Appendix A Formalization and reproduction details

### A.1 Formal statement and correspondence with the paper

The model expresses probability as the number of satisfiable formulas
divided by the total number of formulas. Each clause is represented by
three distinct variable indices and three Boolean values specifying when
its literals are false. A formula is an ordered list of independently
sampled clauses. The final theorem statement is

```
Filter.Tendsto
  (fun n : Nat => SatUpper.satisfiabilityProbability n (1067*n/250))
  Filter.atTop (nhds 0)
```

Natural-number division gives exactly  [TEX: \lfloor 1067n/250\rfloor]  ⌊1067​n/250⌋\lfloor 1067n/250\rfloor.
For  [TEX: n&lt;3]  n<3n<3, there are no clauses with three distinct variables, and the
probability definition uses Lean’s division-by-zero convention. These
finitely many indices do not affect the limit. The interpolation,
numerical, and concentration arguments are proved in the development;
they are not assumptions of the final theorem.

The following table identifies the modules corresponding to the main
steps of the paper.

| Mathematical step                      | Modules below lean/SatUpper/                                         |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Model and final theorem                | Model, Threshold                                                     |
| Minimum violation count                | Ground/Minimum, Ground/Finite                                        |
| Insertion and cubic sign               | Ground/Insertion, LogInsertion, ReplicaCubic                         |
| Finite replacement and Poisson average | BinomialPoisson, PoissonExpectation, Ground/Comparison               |
| Site bound and mixed moments           | Ground/Site, Ground/SiteBound, PoissonPair                           |
| Rational certificate                   | GridExponential, NumericalMoments, NumericalLogs, NumericalEnclosure |
| Fixed count and asymptotics            | ReplacementPoissonMean, ModelTransfer, Obligations                   |
| Variance and conditioning              | FiniteVariance, VarianceControl, LegalConditioning                   |
| Partition-moment bound                 | Ground/Minimum, Ground/Finite                                        |

The audit asks Lean which axioms are used anywhere in each project
theorem’s chain of dependencies and separately checks the final theorem
statement above. It permits only propext, Classical.choice,
and Quot.sound, the standard axioms for propositional
extensionality, choice, and quotients. It rejects proof placeholders and
additional evaluation axioms. The audit inspects Lean’s environment;
the proofs themselves are checked by Lean’s kernel.

The Python calculation is a separate implementation of the numerical
bound; its output is not a premise of the Lean proof.
Lean proves the finite rational inequality ([23](#S6.E23 "In 6.2 Logarithmic enclosures and exact arithmetic ‣ 6 The rational certificate ‣ An upper bound of 4.268 for the random 3-SAT satisfiability threshold")) usingdecide +kernel, which performs computation checked by Lean’s kernel.

### A.2 Reproduction

The formalization uses Lean 4.24.0 and mathlib [[23](#bib.bib23)], pinned to
revision

f897ebcf72cd16f89ab4577d0c826cd14afaafc7.

With the pinned toolchain installed, the proof and audit are reproduced
from the repository root by

```
sh lean/run.sh exe cache get
python3 lean/verify.py
python3 lean/test_verify.py
```

The independent certificate uses only the Python standard library:

```
python3 src/richer_certificate.py --alpha 4.268 --cutoff 128 \
  --surveys .05,.85,.5 .4,.4,.5 --output certificate.json
```

The output field psi_upper gives a rational upper bound on  [TEX: \Psi_{128}]  Ψ128\Psi_{128}; its exact entry is the fraction used for checking,
while decimal_for_display is only an approximation.
The flag certified_negative records whether this rational upper
bound is negative.
The repository additionally includes numerical cross-checks, source
provenance, and instructions for rebuilding the paper and release bundle.
