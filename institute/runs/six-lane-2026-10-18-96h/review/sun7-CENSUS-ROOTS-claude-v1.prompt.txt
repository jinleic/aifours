00044|have coprime indices; FC23 below proves Sun(2)). Let A_5 = {x, y}. For j not in A_5, gcd(n_x, n_j) is in [2,6], not
00045|divisible by 5 and has no prime >= 7 (it is <= 6), so 2 or 3 divides both; hence x in A_2 cup A_3, likewise y.
00046|gcd(n_x, n_y) is divisible by 5 and <= 6, so it equals 5: x and y share neither 2 nor 3. They cannot both lie in A_3, so
00047|one of them lies in A_2; call it x. Then y is not in A_2, so y in A_3, and x is not in A_3. For j in C, gcd(n_x, n_j) in [2,6] with 3, 5 not dividing it (3 does not
00048|divide n_x, 5 does not divide n_j) forces 2 | n_j; gcd(n_y, n_j) forces 3 | n_j. So C = A_2 cap A_3 and A_5 cap C = empty:
00049|n_x = 10 r_x (3 does not divide r_x), n_y = 15 r_y (r_y odd), n_c = 6 r_c (5 does not divide r_c). Then
00050|  gcd(n_x, n_y) = 5 gcd(r_x, r_y), gcd(n_x, n_c) = 2 gcd(r_x, r_c), gcd(n_y, n_c) = 3 gcd(r_y, r_c), gcd(n_c, n_c') = 6 gcd(r_c, r_c'),
00051|because in each case the displayed extra primes 2, 3, 5 divide only one side (by (S1)). The bound <= 6 gives (S2), and
00052|gcd(r_x, r_c) <= 3 with 3 not dividing r_x gives (S3). (<=) The four identities give gcds 5, 2 or 4, 3, 6, all <= 6; A_5 = {x, y}
