                                                                                                         Abstract
                                                       How large can a set of mutually orthogonal quantum Latin squares (MOQLS) get? We show that a
                                                   set of n − 2 MOQLS of order n is necessarily classical and construct large non-classical sets of MOQLS
                                                   of orders that are prime powers, improving both the previously known lower and upper bounds.

                                           Keywords. Quantum Latin square; Mutually orthogonal quantum Latin squares; Unitary pattern;
                                           Frobenius ring; Directions determined by a function.

                                           1      Introduction
                                           Mutually orthogonal quantum Latin squares1 (MOQLS) were introduced by Musto [10] as a generalisation
                                           of mutually orthogonal Latin squares (MOLS). Quantum Latin squares, and more generally, MOQLS,
                                           have applications in quantum teleportation, dense coding, complex Hadamard matrices, quantum graph
                                           isomorphism, mutually unbiased bases, AME states and perfect tensors [1, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 14]. A natural
                                           problem regarding these objects is their existence.
                                           Problem 1. Given n ≥ 2, what is the largest value of t such that t MOQLS(n) exist?
                                           Since we are mostly interested in the non-classical case, we define M (n) as the largest value of t such that
                                           a non-classical set of t MOQLS(n) exist, following the notation in [14]. If such a set does not exist, we let
                                           M (n) = 0.
                                           The following results on the existence of non-classical sets of MOQLS are known.
                                           Theorem 2 ([2]). A set of n − 1 MOQLS(n) is classical. That is, M (n) ≤ n − 2.
                                           Theorem 3 ([14]). If there exist t1 MOQLS(n1 ) and t2 MOQLS(n2 ) and if t = min{t1 , t2 } ≥ 2, then
                                           there exists a non-classical set of t MOQLS(n1 n2 ).
                                           Theorem 4 ([2, 5, 7, 14]).               1. There exists a non-classical QLS(n) if and only if n ≥ 4.
                                               2. There exists a non-classical set of 2 MOQLS(n) if and only if n ≥ 7.
                                               3. If n ≥ 16, then there exists a non-classical set of 3 MOQLS(n).

2     Preliminaries
2.1    Latin squares
A Latin square of order n is an n × n matrix containing elements of a set of size n such that each row and
each column contains every element exactly once. Two Latin squares A = (aij )1≤i,j≤n and B = (bij )1≤i,j≤n
are orthogonal if all n2 tuples (aij , bij ) are different. A set of t mutually orthogonal Latin squares of order
n, abbreviated t MOLS(n), is a set of t Latin squares of order n that are pairwise orthogonal.

2.2    Quantum Latin squares
A quantum Latin square of order n is an n × n matrix over Cn such that each row and each column forms
an orthonormal basis of Cn . Two quantum Latin squares are isotopic if they can be obtained from one
another by
 (i) multiplying each entry by a phase factor,
 (ii) permuting rows and columns, and
(iii) applying a unitary transformation on all entries.
A quantum Latin square is classical or not genuinely quantum if, up to isotopy, all entries are contained
in {|1⟩ , . . . , |n⟩}, a fixed orthonormal basis. This property is independent of the chosen basis by item (iii)
in the definition of isotopy.

2.3    Mutually orthogonal quantum Latin squares (MOQLS)
Two quantum Latin squares (ψij )1≤i,j≤n and (ϕij )1≤i,j≤n are orthogonal if
                                          {ψij ⊗ ϕij : i, j ∈ {1, . . . , n}}
                               n     n
is an orthonormal basis of C ⊗ C . A set of t mutually orthogonal quantum Latin squares of order n,
denoted t MOQLS(n), is a set of t quantum Latin squares of order n such that any two of them are
orthogonal. Two sets of t MOQLS(n) are isotopic if they can be obtained from one another by

 (i) multiplying each entry by a phase factor,
 (ii) permuting rows and columns, simultaneously among all squares, and
(iii) applying, separately to each square, a unitary transformation to all of its entries.
We say that a set of t MOQLS(n) is classical or not genuinely quantum if, up to isotopy, all entries
are contained in {|1⟩ , . . . , |n⟩}. Again, by item (iii) above, this property does not depend on the chosen
orthonormal basis.

2.4    Patterns of quantum Latin squares
The pattern of a matrix is the binary matrix that is obtained from it by replacing each nonzero entry by
a one. A unitary pattern is a pattern of a unitary matrix. Similarly, the pattern of a vector is the vector
obtained from it by replacing each nonzero entry by a one. The support of a vector is the set of coordinates
where it is nonzero. The weight of a vector is the size of its support, that is, the number of ones in its
pattern. The pattern of a quantum Latin square is the matrix that is obtained from it by replacing each
entry by its pattern. For example:

Note that the pattern depends on the chosen orthonormal basis {|1⟩ , . . . , |n⟩}. We recall the following
“quantum sudoku rules” that were introduced in [2]. We refer to that paper for a more detailed explanation.

    #1 (Standard form.) We can apply unitary transformations and assume that, up to isotopy, the
       squares are in standard form where the first row in the pattern of each quantum Latin square is

                                         10. . . 0   010. . . 0   ···    0. . . 01

    #2 (Unitary patterns.) Each row and each column of a quantum Latin square form a unitary
       pattern, by definition of quantum Latin squares. In particular, if there is an entry of weight one
       with support {k}, then all other entries in the same row and column have a zero in position k.
    #3 (Zero overlap.) From the second row onward, at a fixed cell (i, j) and coordinate k, two orthog-
       onal squares cannot both have a pattern in that cell with a 1 in the kth coordinate. Indeed,
       since the quantum Latin squares are mutually orthogonal, ψij ⊗ ϕij and ψ1k ⊗ ϕ1k = |k⟩ ⊗ |k⟩
       are orthogonal, so ⟨k|ψij ⟩ ⟨k|ϕij ⟩ = 0.


2.5    Frobenius rings
Let R be a finite ring of order n with identity. We denote the set of its invertible elements by R∗ . An
additive character of R is a group morphism χ : (R, +) → (T, ·) where T = {z ∈ C : |z| = 1} is the complex
unit circle.
A Frobenius ring is a ring R that admits a generating character : an additive character χ such that every
additive character of R is uniquely of the form x 7→ χ(ax) for some a ∈ R.
Examples of Frobenius rings are the modular ring Z/nZ (with generating character x 7→ ω x , where ω is
a primitive nth root of unity) and the finite field Fq (with generating character x 7→ ω tr(x) , where ω is a
primitive pth root of unity and q = ph , p prime), see also [13].

3     A set of n − 2 MOQLS(n) is classical
We make use of the following classical result.
Theorem 9 ([12]). A set of n − 3 MOLS(n) can be extended to a set of n − 1 MOLS(n) if n ̸= 4.
In particular, a set of n − 2 MOLS(n) can be extended to a set of n − 1 MOLS(n). This also holds for
n = 4, which is not so difficult to verify directly.
Lemma 10 ([2, Lemma 13]). If Ψ is a quantum Latin square without entries of weight three or more,
then there exists a classical quantum Latin square Ψ′ such that whenever Ψ and Φ are orthogonal, Ψ′ and
Φ are orthogonal as well.
Proof. Let n be the order of Ψ. We may assume that n ≥ 4, otherwise the statement is trivial. We show
that, as long as Ψ has entries of weight two, we can replace Ψ by a quantum Latin square with strictly
fewer entries of weight two and no entries of weight three or more, while still being orthogonal to Φ. We
can repeat this operation until all entries have weight one.
Choose an entry ψij of weight two, and assume without loss of generality that it has pattern 110. . . 0. Let U
be the unique (up to phase factors) unitary transformation that converts ψij into |1⟩ and fixes |3⟩ , . . . , |n⟩.
Let Ψ′ be the quantum Latin square obtained from Ψ by applying U to every entry with pattern 110. . . 0,
while keeping the other entries the same. First, the operation never increases the weight of any entry, and
decreases the weight of ψij . Second, the operation preserves the orthogonality with other quantum Latin
squares because whenever two entries in Ψ are orthogonal, the corresponding entries in Ψ′ are orthogonal
as well. The only case where this could go wrong is when one of the two orthogonal entries has pattern
110. . . 0, but then the other entry has either the same support {1, 2} or a support disjoint from {1, 2}.
We are now ready to prove Theorem 5.
Proof of Theorem 5. Suppose, for a contradiction, that {Ψ1 , . . . , Ψn−2 } is a set of n − 2 MOQLS of order
n where at least one of them, say Ψ1 , is not classical. By rule #1, we may assume them to be in standard
form. At an off-first-row cell (i, j), rule #2 excludes coordinate j from every support, while rule #3
makes the supports from the n − 2 squares pairwise disjoint. Therefore, all entries have weight at most
(n − 1) − (n − 3) = 2. Since Ψ1 is assumed to be non-classical, it has an entry of weight two. The first
row has only entries of weight one, so suppose without loss of generality that the entry (Ψ1 )21 has pattern
0110. . . 0. In the same row, the other n − 1 patterns cannot be all of the form *00*. . . *, because they are
orthogonal and hence span a space of dimension n − 1. So there exists a column j for which (Ψ1 )2j has a
pattern of the form 01*. . . * or 0*1*. . . *. Since (Ψ1 )21 and (Ψ1 )2j are orthogonal and the weight of (Ψ1 )2j
is at most two, the pattern of (Ψ1 )2j is equal to 0110. . . 0 as well. Similarly, there is another entry (Ψ1 )i1
with pattern 0110. . . 0 in the first column.

factors, so Φi1 = |2⟩. Now both (ψ1′ )2j ⊗ ϕ2j and (ψ1′ )i1 ⊗ ϕi1 are equal to |3⟩ ⊗ |2⟩, contradicting the
orthogonality between Ψ′1 and Φ.

To maximize the number of undetermined directions, we can choose |H| to be the largest proper divisor

Proof of Corollary 7. We use Theorem 6 together with Example 15, in the optimal case when |H| is the
largest proper divisor of q − 1. When applying Theorem 6, we let M be the set of undetermined directions.
Note that the second condition on M is trivially fulfilled.
To complete the proof, we verify that the function f from Example 15 is non-additive (that is, not Fp -
linear). Since H is a proper subgroup of (Fq \ {0}, ·), there exists x ∈ (Fq \ {0}) \ H. Additivity would
imply f (x + 1) = f (1) = 1, but also f (x + 1) ∈ {0, x + 1} by definition of f , a contradiction.


5    Conclusion and future directions
We constructed new lower and upper bounds on the largest number t for which there exist non-classical
sets of t MOQLS(n). Specific values of these new bounds for small n can be found in Table 2.
We pose two open problems for future research:
Problem 16. Is a set of n − 3 MOQLS(n), n ̸= 4, necessarily classical?
Problem 17. Are there numbers t and n for which there exist t MOQLS(n) but no t MOLS(n)?
The smallest open case for the second problem is n = 10. It is not known that 7 MOLS(10) do not exist
(combining Theorem 9 with the non-existence of a projective plane of order 10, see [4]), but it is still an
open problem whether 7 MOQLS(10) exist. It would already be interesting to find 3 MOQLS(10), because
the existence of 3 MOLS(10) is not known.
