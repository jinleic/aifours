                                                       A Latin square can be extended by replacing its symbols with probabilities or quan-
                                                   tum states. We relate these two extensions through explicit examples, from classical
                                                   and fractional Latin squares to quantum Latin squares and a real solution of Euler’s
                                                   quantum 36 officers problem. A simultaneous cyclic-shift ansatz on a four-torus of ten-
                                                   sor coefficients gives a real absolutely maximally entangled (AME) state of four six-level
                                                   systems. A local orthogonal change of basis yields a representative with 96 nonzero
                                                   amplitudes. Two single-party arrays obtained from the corresponding quantum orthog-
                                                   onal Latin square yield fractional Latin squares. Signs matter: comparison with the
                                                   independent real construction of Bruzda and its sign variant distinguishes three local-
                                                   unitary classes, two of which have identical probabilities. We extend this probability
                                                   representation to existing seven- and nine-party AME states with six levels each. Their
                                                   amplitude matrices reveal 48 complex Hadamard blocks of order 33 and six of order 64 ,
                                                   respectively. These constructions separate what can be read from a probability picture
                                                   from the coherent conditions responsible for maximal entanglement.



                                            1 Introduction
                                            Euler’s 36 officers problem asks for an arrangement of six regiments and six ranks in a square,
                                            with every regiment and every rank represented once in each row and column. Euler conjectured
                                            [1] and Tarry proved [2] that such a configuration is not possible classically. Pairs of orthogonal
                                            Latin squares exist at every order d ≥ 2 except two and six [3]. The situation changes if the
                                            problem becomes ’quantum’ and each cell is allowed to contain a superposition. Such an approach
                                            allowed Rather et al. to obtain a quantum orthogonal Latin square of order six – a solution to
                                            the quantum version of the 36 officers problem, and at the same time, to construct an absolutely
                                            maximally entangled golden state of a four-dice system [4, 5].
                                                Subsequent complex constructions use biunimodular vectors [6], two-unitary complex Hadamard
                                            matrices [7], and analytic constructions from qutrit Clifford sectors [8]. In particular, flat complex
                                            AME(4, 6) representatives are already known. These results enlarge the class of quantum solutions,
                                            but do not lead to a real solution.
                                                The above observation leaves room for two rather different extensions of a Latin square. In
                                            a fractional square, each cell can be divided into smaller figures whose relative areas specify a
                                            probability distribution over symbols. In a quantum Latin square each cell corresponds to a
                                            normalized pure state, while each row and column forms an orthonormal basis. Squared amplitudes
                                            connect the two descriptions, but do not identify them. Probabilities determine the relative area of
                                            each coloured region in a given cell, while relative phases of the superposition coefficients determine
                                            whether the vectors represented by that picture are orthogonal.
                                                The connection between maximally mixed reductions and quantum error-correcting codes was
                                            developed by Scott [9], who related multipartite entanglement measures to the purity of reduced


                                                                                                         1
states. Facchi et al. formulated the search for maximally multipartite entangled states through
the average purity over balanced bipartitions [10] and studied the frustration that arises when its
minimum cannot be reached in every cut [11]. AME states attain this minimum simultaneously
and are therefore perfect maximally multipartite entangled states in that terminology. Let us
emphasize that AME(N, d) states do not exist for all pairs (N, d): in particular, AME(4, 2) and
AME(7, 2) do not exist [12, 13]. Due to effective usage of techniques relying on artificial intelligence,
a significant progress in this field started in July 2026, with several results beginning to appear in
August, as models Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol were released.
    Our main construction is a real AME(4, 6) state (of n = 4 systems with d = 6 levels each),
invariant under a simultaneous cyclic shift of all four indices of the tensor. A suitable local
orthogonal transformation reduces its dense cyclic form to a sparse representative with 96 nonzero
amplitudes. This gives a positive answer to problem (T2) of Ref. [14], which asks whether real
coefficients suffice, or equivalently whether an orthogonal two-unitary matrix of order 36 exists. We
give both bases and their connecting transformations, so the role of the symmetry can be followed
independently of the numerical search.
    The sparse basis is particularly convenient for a Latin-square description. Each quantum officer
contains either two or four equally weighted product-basis terms. Their areas are halves or quarters,
their two output labels provide two alphabets, and their signs retain the cancellations required for
maximal entanglement. We compare this state with Bruzda’s independent 60-term construction
[15] and with a tensor obtained by reversing one of its signs. An exact contraction distinguishes
the three states even when permutations of the parties are allowed. The last two have the same
probabilities, making the information lost by an area picture especially transparent.
    The central result is the exact real AME(4, 6) state and its local transformations. The Latin-
square viewpoint then gives a second result: fixed-position colour assembly can be possible in one
local basis and impossible in another. In such a way we establish a link between classical fractional
Latin squares, quantum Latin squares and quantum orthogonal Latin squares.
    For more than four parties, cells become states of several output systems rather than individual
qudits, and one uses Latin cubes instead of squares. We illustrate this passage using the non-
stabilizer states AME(7, 6) of Shi et al. [16] and AME(9, 6) of Kitamura [17]. The corresponding
probability arrays are shown to form a fractional Latin cube and a four-dimensional fractional
Latin hypercube. Their pictures are very different: the seven-party example uses two alternating
palettes, whereas the nine-party example has uniform probabilities and a nontrivial phase pattern.
Grouping the amplitudes reveals 48 Hadamard blocks of order 27 in the seven-party case, and six
of order 64 in the nine-party case.
    This work is organized as follows. The next two sections recall fractional and quantum Latin
squares. Section 4 explains their relation to quantum-officer arrays, including an order-four example
giving AME(4, 4). Section 5 presents the cyclic real construction and its sparse representative.
Section 6 compares three real four-party states, while Section 7 treats the seven- and nine-party
examples. The appendices contain the local rotations, complete sparse coefficients, a 192-term basis
with fixed-position colour sums, the sign witness and exact Hadamard-block proofs. Geomagic
representations provide a related geometric viewpoint [15, 18]; here our emphasis is on the Latin
constraints and the amplitudes that lift them to quantum states.


2 Latin squares and fractional entries
A Latin square of order d contains d symbols arranged in a d × d array, with each symbol appearing
once in every row and column. Starting from the familiar cyclic example, Lij = i + j (mod d), one
can express the same rule in a form suited to generalization: replace the symbols by a three-index
incidence tensor,
                                   L
                                 Pijk = δk,Lij ,   0 ≤ i, j, k < d.                             (1)
For fixed i, j, the third index records the symbol in a cell; the three indices thus encode the row,
column and symbol on an equal footing. The Latin rule says that summing over any one of them,
with the other two fixed, gives one.

precisely the incidence tensors of ordinary Latin squares.

of areas Pij0 , . . . , Pij,d−1 , taking the area of a cell to be one. The first condition then fills each cell,
while the other two ensure that any given colour occupies total area one along a row or column.
These equal areas follow from the Latin conditions. Whether the corresponding regions can also
be assembled into a prescribed shape will depend on their geometry.

4.2 Normalization and the three balanced cuts
An AME(N, d) state is a pure state of N parties with local dimension d whose reductions to
any set of at most ⌊N/2⌋ parties are maximally mixed. Constructions from classical maximum-
distance-separable codes and applications to quantum secret sharing are discussed by Helwig and
Cui [27].

6 Three real AME(4,6) states, two supports and one changing sign
The coefficients offer two rather different views of a state. Their moduli determine the fractional
Latin arrays and the coloured area pictures, whereas the relative signs determine the cancellations
needed for orthogonality. A comparison with the construction of Bruzda [15] makes this distinction
particularly transparent. Throughout this section, Tijkl = 6⟨i, j, k, l|ψ⟩ for the normalized state
under consideration. Our 96-term tensor is denoted by T HZ = T (96) and Bruzda’s 60-term tensor
by T WB . The third tensor below is not an independent seed: it is obtained from T WB by reversing
one coefficient. We call the original representative Bruzda A and this sign variant Bruzda B.

6.1 What the blocks do, and do not, tell us
For each balanced cut, join a row label to a column label whenever the corresponding matrix entry
is nonzero. The connected components of this bipartite graph determine the largest separation
into blocks obtainable by row and column permutations alone. For an orthogonal matrix every
component has the same number of row and column vertices. Figure 6 displays these components
for the three flattenings of each state.
    In the 01|23 cut, our tensor contains twelve 2 × 2 blocks and one connected 12 × 12 block. Its
other two cuts contain six 2×2 and six 4×4 blocks. Bruzda’s tensor instead has twelve scalar blocks
and twelve 2 × 2 blocks in every cut. The 12 × 12 block cannot be split further by permutations
alone; this statement concerns the displayed support, not arbitrary local changes of basis or the
smallest support in the LU orbit.
    The permutations in this figure only reorder the composite row and column labels. They need
not factor into permutations on the individual particles and are therefore not being used as physical
local operations.

6.3 The two real endpoints belong to a complex phase family
The same isolated coefficient may acquire an arbitrary phase rather than only a minus sign. The
tensor
                                T WB (θ) = T WB + (eiθ − 1)E0132                            (43)
remains two-unitary for every real θ, since this phase never enters an off-diagonal row inner product.

                                    K(T WB (θ)) = 56 + 16 cos θ.                                  (44)
It varies strictly monotonically for 0 ≤ θ ≤ π and therefore distinguishes a continuous family
of complex LU classes with identical coefficient probabilities. The displayed real endpoints are
T WB and T WB,− . The value 108 of our real tensor lies outside the interval [40, 72], so no state in
this particular family is locally equivalent to it. This is not a classification of all real or complex
AME(4, 6) states.

8 Concluding remarks
For each state considered here, squared amplitudes define fractional Latin arrays, and the figures
represent these probabilities by areas. The probabilities alone, however, do not determine the state:
relative phases are also needed to obtain the orthogonality conditions.
    For the quantum 36 officers problem, a simultaneous cyclic-shift ansatz gives a real solution
whose exact sparse representative has 96 terms. The two local bases serve different purposes. One
reveals the symmetry that made the search practical; the other displays 36 entangled officers with
only two coefficient moduli. Their rational areas provide a direct connection to fractional Latin
squares, without requiring the coefficients themselves to be rational.
    A further single-party basis change gives a 192-term representative whose colour regions cover
the target square at fixed coordinates along every row and column. Its probability array is an
explicit mixture of eight Latin squares, whereas the displayed 96-term array lies outside the convex

    Comparison with Bruzda’s real solution and its one-sign variant distinguishes three entangle-

their invariant values differ. Conversely, a basis change may substantially alter a support picture
without changing the class. Neither visible sparsity nor colour balance is a substitute for an LU
invariant.
    The higher-party examples extend the same distinction. In the seven-party cube, colours alter-
nate between two palettes; in the nine-party hypercube every probability cell is uniform. In the
chosen matrix cuts, 48 Hadamard blocks of order 27 cover the nonzero support of the seven-party
state, whereas six blocks of order 64 cover the nine-party array. The latter also admits a finer
partition into 65 squares of order 36, indexed by five fixed local labels. The character form makes
their orthogonality transparent, although the conditions from the remaining cuts still enter the
AME property.
    Several questions remain open. The displayed sparse bases do not establish the minimum
support in their local-unitary orbits. Flat complex AME(4, 6) constructions are known [6–8]; what
remains open here is whether the specific LU class of T HZ contains one. At the probabilistic level,
it would be useful to characterize which rational fractional Latin arrays admit coherent lifts and
which permit a fixed-position assembly for both output alphabets simultaneously. Proposition 1
answers the one-alphabet question. Sign equations and finite-field phases give concrete settings in
which to pursue these questions.

   Let us conclude this work with two more general remarks.
    A) Several multipartite states with extremal properties correspond to quantum designs. Classi-
cal probability vectors can be replaced by quantum pure states, which can be used to construct a
quantum superposition or convex mixture. In an analogous way, classical designs can be quantized,
and their quantum superpositions and mixtures can then be considered.
    B) In the search for new quantum structures, including AME states, a brute-force search in a
space of very large dimension is unlikely to succeed. We therefore suggest the following steps.
    i) Make an educated guess about a condition that the desired state may satisfy, in order to
reduce the dimension of the search space. In the case of a real AM E(4, 6) state, this may be the
shift symmetry (24) on the 4–torus, imposed on the coefficients of the tensor.
    ii) With suitable computational tools (and some luck) you might be able to find a solution of
this simplified problem.
    iii) Exploit the gauge freedom of the numerical solution (for instance, local unitary equivalence
for AME states) to bring it to a special, transparent form, which may make an analytic solution
possible.

B.2.3   Why the 96-term representation is different

By Proposition 1, a fixed-position layout exists exactly when the colour array is a mixture of Latin
squares. A zero entry forbids the corresponding symbol in every square with nonzero weight, so
only Latin squares supported by the array need be considered. Subdividing a colour region to
carry the second output label does not change this necessary colour condition.
   For the 96-term tensor, complete exact enumeration gives eight Latin squares compatible with
the support of P in Eq. (38). Every one has L0,2 ∈ {1, 5}. A convex combination of them would
therefore give

                 P0,2,1 + P0,2,5 = 1              for every such convex combination,
                                                                                                 (65)
                 P0,2,1 + P0,2,5 = 14 + 14 = 12   for the actual array.

This contradiction rules out a fixed-position layout for these cells and this colour index, even with
arbitrary measurable subdivisions. It does not rule out reassembly by translations or rotations,
nor a different local basis. In particular, neither optimal support 192 nor the impossibility of every
96-term representative in the LU orbit is asserted. Equally, we do not know whether disconnected
regions are necessary in every layout of the new basis. The geometry records a choice of probability
representation; the signed tensor remains the object whose AME property is verified.
