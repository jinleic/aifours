33: many row and column vertices: the restricted rows are nonzero orthonormal vectors supported in its column space,

182: 2.1

184: Latin squares
185: 
186: A Latin square of order n is an n × n matrix containing elements of a set of size n such that each row and
187: each column contains every element exactly once. Two Latin squares A = (aij )1≤i,j≤n and B = (bij )1≤i,j≤n
188: are orthogonal if all n2 tuples (aij , bij ) are different. A set of t mutually orthogonal Latin squares of order
189: n, abbreviated t MOLS(n), is a set of t Latin squares of order n that are pairwise orthogonal.
190: 
191: 2.2
192: 
193: Quantum Latin squares
194: 
195: A quantum Latin square of order n is an n × n matrix over Cn such that each row and each column forms
196: an orthonormal basis of Cn . Two quantum Latin squares are isotopic if they can be obtained from one
197: another by
198: (i) multiplying each entry by a phase factor,
199: (ii) permuting rows and columns, and
200: (iii) applying a unitary transformation on all entries.
201: A quantum Latin square is classical or not genuinely quantum if, up to isotopy, all entries are contained
202: in {|1⟩ , . . . , |n⟩}, a fixed orthonormal basis. This property is independent of the chosen basis by item (iii)
203: in the definition of isotopy.
204: 
205: 2.3
206:

208: 
209: Two quantum Latin squares (ψij )1≤i,j≤n and (ϕij )1≤i,j≤n are orthogonal if
210: {ψij ⊗ ϕij : i, j ∈ {1, . . . , n}}

215: is an orthonormal basis of C ⊗ C . A set of t mutually orthogonal quantum Latin squares of order n,
216: denoted t MOQLS(n), is a set of t quantum Latin squares of order n such that any two of them are
217: orthogonal. Two sets of t MOQLS(n) are isotopic if they can be obtained from one another by

221: (ii) permuting rows and columns, simultaneously among all squares, and
222: (iii) applying, separately to each square, a unitary transformation to all of its entries.
223: We say that a set of t MOQLS(n) is classical or not genuinely quantum if, up to isotopy, all entries
224: are contained in {|1⟩ , . . . , |n⟩}. Again, by item (iii) above, this property does not depend on the chosen
225: orthonormal basis.

306: Frobenius rings
307: 
308: Let R be a finite ring of order n with identity. We denote the set of its invertible elements by R∗ . An
309: additive character of R is a group morphism χ : (R, +) → (T, ·) where T = {z ∈ C : |z| = 1} is the complex
310: unit circle.
311: A Frobenius ring is a ring R that admits a generating character : an additive character χ such that every
312: additive character of R is uniquely of the form x 7→ χ(ax) for some a ∈ R.
313: Examples of Frobenius rings are the modular ring Z/nZ (with generating character x 7→ ω x , where ω is
314: a primitive nth root of unity) and the finite field Fq (with generating character x 7→ ω tr(x) , where ω is a
315: primitive pth root of unity and q = ph , p prime), see also [13].

336: A set of n − 2 MOQLS(n) is classical

338: We make use of the following classical result.
339: Theorem 9 ([12]). A set of n − 3 MOLS(n) can be extended to a set of n − 1 MOLS(n) if n ̸= 4.
340: In particular, a set of n − 2 MOLS(n) can be extended to a set of n − 1 MOLS(n). This also holds for
341: n = 4, which is not so difficult to verify directly.
342: Lemma 10 ([2, Lemma 13]). If Ψ is a quantum Latin square without entries of weight three or more,
343: then there exists a classical quantum Latin square Ψ′ such that whenever Ψ and Φ are orthogonal, Ψ′ and
344: Φ are orthogonal as well.
345: Proof. Let n be the order of Ψ. We may assume that n ≥ 4, otherwise the statement is trivial. We show
346: that, as long as Ψ has entries of weight two, we can replace Ψ by a quantum Latin square with strictly
347: fewer entries of weight two and no entries of weight three or more, while still being orthogonal to Φ. We
348: can repeat this operation until all entries have weight one.
349: Choose an entry ψij of weight two, and assume without loss of generality that it has pattern 110. . . 0. Let U
350: be the unique (up to phase factors) unitary transformation that converts ψij into |1⟩ and fixes |3⟩ , . . . , |n⟩.
351: Let Ψ′ be the quantum Latin square obtained from Ψ by applying U to every entry with pattern 110. . . 0,
352: while keeping the other entries the same. First, the operation never increases the weight of any entry, and
353: decreases the weight of ψij . Second, the operation preserves the orthogonality with other quantum Latin
354: squares because whenever two entries in Ψ are orthogonal, the corresponding entries in Ψ′ are orthogonal
355: as well. The only case where this could go wrong is when one of the two orthogonal entries has pattern
356: 110. . . 0, but then the other entry has either the same support {1, 2} or a support disjoint from {1, 2}.

637: Conclusion and future directions
638: 
639: We constructed new lower and upper bounds on the largest number t for which there exist non-classical
640: sets of t MOQLS(n). Specific values of these new bounds for small n can be found in Table 2.
641: We pose two open problems for future research:
642: Problem 16. Is a set of n − 3 MOQLS(n), n ̸= 4, necessarily classical?
643: Problem 17. Are there numbers t and n for which there exist t MOQLS(n) but no t MOLS(n)?
644: The smallest open case for the second problem is n = 10. It is not known that 7 MOLS(10) do not exist
645: (combining Theorem 9 with the non-existence of a projective plane of order 10, see [4]), but it is still an
646: open problem whether 7 MOQLS(10) exist. It would already be interesting to find 3 MOQLS(10), because
647: the existence of 3 MOLS(10) is not known.
