22:                                            1      Introduction
23:                                            Mutually orthogonal quantum Latin squares1 (MOQLS) were introduced by Musto [10] as a generalisation
24:                                            of mutually orthogonal Latin squares (MOLS). Quantum Latin squares, and more generally, MOQLS,
25:                                            have applications in quantum teleportation, dense coding, complex Hadamard matrices, quantum graph
26:                                            isomorphism, mutually unbiased bases, AME states and perfect tensors [1, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 14]. A natural
27:                                            problem regarding these objects is their existence.
28:                                            Problem 1. Given n ≥ 2, what is the largest value of t such that t MOQLS(n) exist?
29:                                            Since we are mostly interested in the non-classical case, we define M (n) as the largest value of t such that
30:                                            a non-classical set of t MOQLS(n) exist, following the notation in [14]. If such a set does not exist, we let
31:                                            M (n) = 0.
32:                                            The following results on the existence of non-classical sets of MOQLS are known.
33:                                            Theorem 2 ([2]). A set of n − 1 MOQLS(n) is classical. That is, M (n) ≤ n − 2.
34:                                            Theorem 3 ([14]). If there exist t1 MOQLS(n1 ) and t2 MOQLS(n2 ) and if t = min{t1 , t2 } ≥ 2, then
35:                                            there exists a non-classical set of t MOQLS(n1 n2 ).
36:                                            Theorem 4 ([2, 5, 7, 14]).               1. There exists a non-classical QLS(n) if and only if n ≥ 4.
37:                                                2. There exists a non-classical set of 2 MOQLS(n) if and only if n ≥ 7.
38:                                                3. If n ≥ 16, then there exists a non-classical set of 3 MOQLS(n).

83: 2     Preliminaries
84: 2.1    Latin squares
85: A Latin square of order n is an n × n matrix containing elements of a set of size n such that each row and
86: each column contains every element exactly once. Two Latin squares A = (aij )1≤i,j≤n and B = (bij )1≤i,j≤n
87: are orthogonal if all n2 tuples (aij , bij ) are different. A set of t mutually orthogonal Latin squares of order
88: n, abbreviated t MOLS(n), is a set of t Latin squares of order n that are pairwise orthogonal.
89: 
90: 2.2    Quantum Latin squares
91: A quantum Latin square of order n is an n × n matrix over Cn such that each row and each column forms
92: an orthonormal basis of Cn . Two quantum Latin squares are isotopic if they can be obtained from one
93: another by
94:  (i) multiplying each entry by a phase factor,
95:  (ii) permuting rows and columns, and
96: (iii) applying a unitary transformation on all entries.
97: A quantum Latin square is classical or not genuinely quantum if, up to isotopy, all entries are contained
98: in {|1⟩ , . . . , |n⟩}, a fixed orthonormal basis. This property is independent of the chosen basis by item (iii)
99: in the definition of isotopy.
100: 
101: 2.3    Mutually orthogonal quantum Latin squares (MOQLS)
102: Two quantum Latin squares (ψij )1≤i,j≤n and (ϕij )1≤i,j≤n are orthogonal if
103:                                           {ψij ⊗ ϕij : i, j ∈ {1, . . . , n}}
104:                                n     n
105: is an orthonormal basis of C ⊗ C . A set of t mutually orthogonal quantum Latin squares of order n,
106: denoted t MOQLS(n), is a set of t quantum Latin squares of order n such that any two of them are
107: orthogonal. Two sets of t MOQLS(n) are isotopic if they can be obtained from one another by
108: 
109: 
110:                                                           2
111:  (i) multiplying each entry by a phase factor,
112:  (ii) permuting rows and columns, simultaneously among all squares, and
113: (iii) applying, separately to each square, a unitary transformation to all of its entries.
114: We say that a set of t MOQLS(n) is classical or not genuinely quantum if, up to isotopy, all entries
115: are contained in {|1⟩ , . . . , |n⟩}. Again, by item (iii) above, this property does not depend on the chosen
116: orthonormal basis.

118: 2.4    Patterns of quantum Latin squares
119: The pattern of a matrix is the binary matrix that is obtained from it by replacing each nonzero entry by
120: a one. A unitary pattern is a pattern of a unitary matrix. Similarly, the pattern of a vector is the vector
121: obtained from it by replacing each nonzero entry by a one. The support of a vector is the set of coordinates
122: where it is nonzero. The weight of a vector is the size of its support, that is, the number of ones in its
123: pattern. The pattern of a quantum Latin square is the matrix that is obtained from it by replacing each
124: entry by its pattern. For example:

134: Note that the pattern depends on the chosen orthonormal basis {|1⟩ , . . . , |n⟩}. We recall the following
135: “quantum sudoku rules” that were introduced in [2]. We refer to that paper for a more detailed explanation.
136: 
137:     #1 (Standard form.) We can apply unitary transformations and assume that, up to isotopy, the
138:        squares are in standard form where the first row in the pattern of each quantum Latin square is
139: 
140:                                          10. . . 0   010. . . 0   ···    0. . . 01
141: 
142:     #2 (Unitary patterns.) Each row and each column of a quantum Latin square form a unitary
143:        pattern, by definition of quantum Latin squares. In particular, if there is an entry of weight one
144:        with support {k}, then all other entries in the same row and column have a zero in position k.
145:     #3 (Zero overlap.) From the second row onward, at a fixed cell (i, j) and coordinate k, two orthog-
146:        onal squares cannot both have a pattern in that cell with a 1 in the kth coordinate. Indeed,
147:        since the quantum Latin squares are mutually orthogonal, ψij ⊗ ϕij and ψ1k ⊗ ϕ1k = |k⟩ ⊗ |k⟩
148:        are orthogonal, so ⟨k|ψij ⟩ ⟨k|ϕij ⟩ = 0.

151: 2.5    Frobenius rings
152: Let R be a finite ring of order n with identity. We denote the set of its invertible elements by R∗ . An
153: additive character of R is a group morphism χ : (R, +) → (T, ·) where T = {z ∈ C : |z| = 1} is the complex
154: unit circle.
155: A Frobenius ring is a ring R that admits a generating character : an additive character χ such that every
156: additive character of R is uniquely of the form x 7→ χ(ax) for some a ∈ R.
157: Examples of Frobenius rings are the modular ring Z/nZ (with generating character x 7→ ω x , where ω is
158: a primitive nth root of unity) and the finite field Fq (with generating character x 7→ ω tr(x) , where ω is a
159: primitive pth root of unity and q = ph , p prime), see also [13].

174: We make use of the following classical result.
175: Theorem 9 ([12]). A set of n − 3 MOLS(n) can be extended to a set of n − 1 MOLS(n) if n ̸= 4.
176: In particular, a set of n − 2 MOLS(n) can be extended to a set of n − 1 MOLS(n). This also holds for
177: n = 4, which is not so difficult to verify directly.
178: Lemma 10 ([2, Lemma 13]). If Ψ is a quantum Latin square without entries of weight three or more,
179: then there exists a classical quantum Latin square Ψ′ such that whenever Ψ and Φ are orthogonal, Ψ′ and
180: Φ are orthogonal as well.
181: Proof. Let n be the order of Ψ. We may assume that n ≥ 4, otherwise the statement is trivial. We show
182: that, as long as Ψ has entries of weight two, we can replace Ψ by a quantum Latin square with strictly
183: fewer entries of weight two and no entries of weight three or more, while still being orthogonal to Φ. We
184: can repeat this operation until all entries have weight one.
185: Choose an entry ψij of weight two, and assume without loss of generality that it has pattern 110. . . 0. Let U
186: be the unique (up to phase factors) unitary transformation that converts ψij into |1⟩ and fixes |3⟩ , . . . , |n⟩.
187: Let Ψ′ be the quantum Latin square obtained from Ψ by applying U to every entry with pattern 110. . . 0,
188: while keeping the other entries the same. First, the operation never increases the weight of any entry, and
189: decreases the weight of ψij . Second, the operation preserves the orthogonality with other quantum Latin
190: squares because whenever two entries in Ψ are orthogonal, the corresponding entries in Ψ′ are orthogonal
191: as well. The only case where this could go wrong is when one of the two orthogonal entries has pattern
192: 110. . . 0, but then the other entry has either the same support {1, 2} or a support disjoint from {1, 2}.
193: We are now ready to prove Theorem 5.

392: 5    Conclusion and future directions
393: We constructed new lower and upper bounds on the largest number t for which there exist non-classical
394: sets of t MOQLS(n). Specific values of these new bounds for small n can be found in Table 2.
395: We pose two open problems for future research:
396: Problem 16. Is a set of n − 3 MOQLS(n), n ̸= 4, necessarily classical?
397: Problem 17. Are there numbers t and n for which there exist t MOQLS(n) but no t MOLS(n)?
398: The smallest open case for the second problem is n = 10. It is not known that 7 MOLS(10) do not exist
399: (combining Theorem 9 with the non-existence of a projective plane of order 10, see [4]), but it is still an
400: open problem whether 7 MOQLS(10) exist. It would already be interesting to find 3 MOQLS(10), because
401: the existence of 3 MOLS(10) is not known.
