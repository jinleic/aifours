## 2. Lemma ROW-2 (LFC at w in W) [NEW]

At w in W the neighbours are four unmarked u (labels alpha_wu), t (label 3^A alpha_wt) and p (label 5^B alpha_wp).
Write k5, k3 for the numbers of unmarked u with alpha_wu = 5, resp. 3. Then:
- **(a')** if k5 >= 1 then k5 = 4 and alpha_wt = 5;
- **(b')** if k3 >= 1 then k3 >= 2, no unmarked entry is 4 or 5, alpha_wt != 4, and if k3 = 2 then alpha_wp = 3 and the two
  other unmarked entries are equal and lie in {1, 2};
- **(c')** if every unmarked entry lies in {1,2,4}, then alpha_wt, alpha_wp lie in {1,2,4} and the full row satisfies the
  conclusions (i)-(iii) of ROW-U.

*Proof.* gcd(t, u) and gcd(p, u) exceed the alpha-gcd only through 3 | alpha_wu (for t) or 5 | alpha_wu (for p);
gcd(t, p) = 2^min(v2 alpha_wt, v2 alpha_wp) * 3^[3 | alpha_wp] * 5^[5 | alpha_wt]. Unmentioned weights are 0; a set through
a weight-0 element has the same sum as without it, so standard weights on unmarked classes stay feasible.
- (a') If 1 <= k5 <= 3: y = 1/k5 on the unmarked fives (5) plus >= 1 from the other unmarked (standard). If k5 = 4 and
  alpha_wt != 5: y = 1/4 on the fives (5); t is not 5-compatible, so its only possible partner is p; y_p = 0, y_t = 1
  adds alpha_wt >= 1. Total >= 6.
- (b') By (a'), k5 = 0. k3 = 1: R3 value 3 plus min(3, 4) = 3 from the rest. k3 = 2 (remaining unmarked x, y): y = 1/2

  if they are ones (p's partners are then only t): adds >= 1. If alpha_wp = 3 and alpha_wt = 4: the four elements

  feasible: 2 + 4/3 + 1, plus 2 from {x, y} (ones isolated; twos at 1/2, and {t, two, two} has no gcd >= 3): 6 + 1/3.
  k3 = 3 (remaining x): x = 4 gives y_x = 1, 3 + 4. For x in {1,2} and alpha_wt = 4 put y = 1/3 on threes and on t

## 3. Proof of Theorem K7-35

**(0) The rows of t.** At t, the only marked neighbour is p (markers {5}). ROW-1 at the apex t (ROW-1-deg transfer):
k5 in {0, 5}; k5 = 5 forces alpha*_tw = 1 on W (INV (b)), and then in the inverted configuration the five labels w_w
and 5^B w_p alpha*_tp are pairwise coprime, so y = 1 gives 5 + alpha*_tp >= 6: impossible. If some unmarked entry is 3,
ROW-1 (b) gives k3 >= 3, no 4 or 5 and alpha_tp in {1, 2}; p is not 3-compatible, and y = 1/3 on threes, 1 on ones, 1/2 on
twos, y_p = 1 (alpha_tp = 1: p coprime to all) or 1/2 (alpha_tp = 2: p's partners are twos only) gives 6: impossible.
So ROW-1 (c) applies: the alpha-row of t satisfies ROW-U (i)-(iii); the same for alpha* (inverted configuration, same
markers). Put F_t := {j : alpha_tj = 4}, F*_t := {j : alpha*_tj = 4}: |F_t| = |F*_t| = 3, F_t disjoint-union F*_t =
W cup {p}, and alpha_tj in {1,2,4} for all j.

**(1) No 5 inside W.** If alpha_ww' = 5 (w, w' in W), ROW-2 (a') at w gives alpha_wt = 5, contradicting (0).

**(2) No 3 inside W.** Let W3 := {w in W : alpha_ww' = 3 for some w' in W}. By ROW-2 (b') each w in W3 has at least two
W-neighbours with alpha = 3, all in W3, so |W3| >= 3; and alpha_wt != 4, so W3 avoids F_t: W3 subset W cap F*_t, which
has at most 3 elements. Hence W3 = F*_t (so p in F_t), |W3| = 3, and each w in W3 has exactly two 3-entries, so by ROW-2
(b') alpha_wp = 3 and alpha_wu1 = alpha_wu2 in {1, 2}, where {u1, u2} = W cap F_t. If these entries are 1, u1 has three
entries 1 (to W3) and no 3 or 5 in its W-row, so ROW-2 (c') at u1 allows at most one entry 1: contradiction. If they
are 2, the W-row of u1 is (2, 2, 2, alpha_u1u2) with alpha_u1u2 in {1,2,4}; ROW-2 (c') at u1 needs three entries 4 among
alpha_u1u2, alpha_u1p, alpha_u1t = 4, so alpha_u1p = 4. Then the row of p has the unmarked entries 3 (to W3) and 4 (to u1),
contradicting ROW-1 (b) at p (its marked neighbour t carries only the 3-marker): an entry 3 forbids any entry 4.

**(3) Parity.** Now every W-entry lies in {1,2,4}; ROW-2 (c') at every w in W gives |A4(w)| >= 3 (A4(v) := {j : alpha_vj = 4})
and alpha_wp in {1,2,4}; ROW-1 (c) at p then gives |A4(p)| >= 3; and |A4(t)| = 3 by (0). Steps (0)-(3) apply verbatim to
the inverted configuration (same markers, alpha** = alpha), giving |A4*(v)| >= 3 for all v. By INV (b) A4(v) and A4*(v)
are disjoint subsets of six neighbours, so |A4(v)| = 3 for all seven vertices; a 3-regular graph on 7 vertices does not
exist. QED
