On the graph G of **distinct actual complex A-rays**, with vertex weight m_v equal to its actual multiplicity, this yields

`D(v) := sum_(u perpendicular v) m_u >= 8`.                                  (D)

The total vertex weight is 24. G has no four-cycle: two distinct rays span a complex two-plane and have only one common orthogonal ray. ### 5.2 Weighted Moore inequality, including unmatched neighbors

Put m=m_v, d=degree(v), and let U be the sum of weights of neighbors unmatched within N(v). For each neighbor u, inequality (D) leaves outside `{v} union N(v)` at least `8−m−m_partner(u)` weight, taking partner weight zero if unmatched. These outside-neighbor sets are disjoint, or G would have a four-cycle. Summing and cancelling the matched-neighbor weight gives exactly

`24 >= m + d(8−m) + U`.                                                     (WM)

Since weights are at most three, (D) implies d >= 3. For m=2, d >= 4 would already require 26, so d=3 and a weight-two vertex cannot neighbor weight one. For m=1, d >= 4 would require 29; at d=3 its three neighbors must all have weight three (a weight-one neighbor fails (D), and a weight-two neighbor has just been excluded). An odd matching leaves at least weight three unmatched, making (WM) require `1+21+3=25`. Thus weight one is impossible.

Every light (weight-two) vertex now has degree three, and every heavy (weight-three) vertex has degree three or four. If a degree-four neighborhood were not perfectly matched, two unmatched vertices would have weight at least four, whereas (WM)'s base is 23; this exceeds 24. Thus degree-four vertices belong to exactly two triangles and all their edges are triangular. Degree-three vertices belong to exactly one triangle and have one bare edge. Bare edges connect only degree-three vertices and form a perfect matching on them.

### 5.3 Exhaustive triangle-intersection bookkeeping

Triangles share at most one ray; a shared ray has degree four and lies in exactly two triangles. Let J have one vertex per triangle, with an edge for each shared ray. J is simple, maximum degree three, and has girth at least five. A 3-cycle would make the three intersection rays an orthogonal triple, whose unique completions identify the original triangles; a 4-cycle would give four distinct rays making a forbidden 4-cycle in G.

If N is the number of rays, q the number of triangles and r the number of degree-four rays, then

`3q=N+r`, and the degree-three ray count is `2N−3q`.

The latter is even because its bare edges form a perfect matching. Therefore q is even and `N/3 <= q <= 2N/3`. A bare edge's endpoint triangles cannot be at distance zero, one, or two in J. At distance one, with shared ray a and third ray z in the first triangle, `x−z−a−y−x` is a 4-cycle. At distance two, intersection rays a,b give `x−a−b−y−x`. All displayed rays are distinct because bare endpoints have unique triangles and a degree-four ray belongs to only two.

Writing `3h+2l=24`, every possible heavy count is h=0,2,4,6,8. I checked each, not merely the all-heavy case:

- **h=0:** each degree-three light vertex has weighted degree six, contradicting (D).

- **h=4, l=6:** each light needs at least two heavy neighbors. - **h=6, l=3, N=9:** q is four or six. If q=6, J has six vertices and nine edges, impossible for girth at least five: a forest has at most five edges; a 6-cycle has no allowable chord; a 5-cycle has no chord and its sixth vertex attaches at most once. If q=4, J has four vertices and three edges, hence is P4 or a three-leaf star. In the star all distinct triangles are distance at most two. In P4, an internal triangle has one bare stub and no triangle at distance at least three. Neither permits the required perfect matching of bare stubs.
- **h=8, N=8:** q must be four, so J has four vertices and four edges, forcing a cycle of length at most four.
