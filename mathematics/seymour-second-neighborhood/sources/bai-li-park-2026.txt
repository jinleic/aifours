                                                                                                   Abstract

                                                        A longstanding conjecture of Seymour, called Seymour’s second neighborhood conjecture, states
                                                                                                            ++          +
                                                     that every oriented graph D contains a vertex x with |ND  (x)| ≥ |ND (x)|. The conjecture was




arXiv:2607.18047v2 [math.CO] 24 Jul 2026
                                                     verified in a few special classes of oriented graphs, and it remains open for general oriented graphs.
                                                     We study a stronger property, asking for a vertex x such that there exists a complete matching from
                                                      +         ++
                                                     ND (x) to ND  (x). We prove that this stronger version holds for every oriented graph with minimum
                                                     out-degree at most 5, and also for every 5-anti-transitive oriented graph. This implies that every
                                                     oriented planar graph satisfies the stronger version.

Conjecture 1.1 ( [8]). Every oriented graph contains a Seymour vertex.

   In a complete symmetric digraph, every two vertices form a 2-cycle and each vertex has an empty
second out-neighborhood. Thus, not every digraph contains a Seymour vertex, and the assumption that
the digraph is an oriented graph cannot be omitted. The restriction of Conjecture 1.1 to tournaments
was called Dean’s conjecture. It was first verified by Fisher [11] and Havet and Thomassé [14] provided
another proof using median orders. The second neighborhood conjecture has received considerable
attention over the years (see [1, 2, 4–7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 15–19]).
   Kaneko and Locke [16] showed that Conjecture 1.1 holds for oriented graphs D with δ + (D) ≤ 6. A
digraph D is k-anti-transitive if (v0 , vk ) ̸∈ A(D) for every path v0 v1 · · · vk of length k. Let at(D) be the
smallest k such that D is k-anti-transitive. Daamouch [6] proved Conjecture 1.1 when at(D) ≤ 5 for
an oriented graph D, and Hassan, Khan, Poshni, Shabbir [13] proved the analogous result for oriented
graphs D with at(D) ≤ 6. Despite various results on special cases and attempts to tackle the original
form, Conjecture 1.1 remains open for general oriented graphs.
   A matching of an undirected graph is a set of disjoint edges. For two disjoint vertex sets X and Y
in an oriented graph D, a matching from X to Y is a set M = {(xi , yi ) ∈ A(D) | xi ∈ X, yi ∈ Y, i =
1, . . . , m} of arcs when {xi yi | i = 1, . . . , m} is a matching in the underlying graph. If every vertex of
X is a tail of some arc of a matching M , then it is called a complete matching from X to Y . We call a
                                                                                              +
vertex x a strong Seymour vertex in an oriented graph D if there is a complete matching from ND (x)
    ++
to ND  (x). Clearly, a strong Seymour vertex is also a Seymour vertex, but the converse does not hold
in general; see, e.g., an oriented graph in Figure 1, in which x is a Seymour vertex but is not a strong
Seymour vertex.

   The following is stronger than Conjecture 1.1.

Conjecture 1.2. Every oriented graph contains a strong Seymour vertex.

   It is clear from the definition that if Conjecture 1.2 is true then Conjecture 1.1 is true. This
property raises new challenges in the case of tournaments, where the existing proofs of Conjecture 1.1
do not apply directly: the median order method, which provides an elegant proof of Conjecture 1.1 for
tournaments, no longer works here. For this reason we state the tournament case separately.

Conjecture 1.3. Every tournament contains a strong Seymour vertex.

                                                        2
    After the first version of this paper appeared, David Dzitsoev was the first to communicate to us
a 36-vertex oriented graph with no strong Seymour vertex, disproving Conjecture 1.2. Replacing each
independent set of that construction by a transitive tournament yields a 36-vertex tournament with
the same property, so Conjecture 1.3 is false as well. The construction is presented in Section 3.
    Nevertheless, a strong Seymour vertex does exist in several natural classes. Our main results are
the following.

Theorem 1.4. Let D be an oriented graph with δ + (D) ≤ 4. There is a vertex with a minimum
out-degree that is also a strong Seymour vertex.

Theorem 1.5. Let D be an oriented graph such that δ + (D) ≤ 5 or at(D) ≤ 5. Then D has a strong
Seymour vertex.

    The condition δ + (D) ≤ 4 in Theorem 1.4 is essential since there is a tournament D with δ + (D) = 5
such that a vertex with a minimum out-degree cannot be a strong Seymour vertex, which is discussed in
Section 3. Theorem 1.5 extends the result of Daamouch [6] to a strong Seymour vertex, where the result
was the existence of a Seymour vertex in a class of oriented graphs D with at(D) ≤ 5. Moreover, since
a planar graph has minimum degree at most five, the following immediately holds from Theorem 1.5.

Corollary 1.6. Conjecture 1.2 holds for planar oriented graphs.

    The paper is organized as follows. We give proofs of Theorems 1.4 and 1.5 in Section 2, and then
we provide in Section 3 some observations on tournaments.
