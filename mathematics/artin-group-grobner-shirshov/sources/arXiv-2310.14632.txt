                                                                                Abstract
                                                    Our main theorem is that the word problem in the Artin group
                                                          G = ha, b, c | aba = bab, ac = ca, n (b, c) = n (c, b)i,
                                                for n ≥ 5 can be solved using a system R of length preserving rewrite
                                                rules that, together with free reduction, can be used to reduce any
                                                word over {a, b, c} to a geodesic word in G, in quadratic time. This
                                                result builds on work of Holt and Rees, and of Blasco, Cumplido and
                                                Morris-Wright, which proves the same result for all Artin groups that
                                                are either sufficiently large or 3-free.
                                                    Since every rank 3 Artin group is either spherical or in one of the
                                                categories covered by the previous results on which we build, it follows
                                                that any rank 3 Artin group has quadratic Dehn function. However
                                                we note that this and much more is a consequence of very recent work
                                                of Haettel and Huang; our contribution is to provide a particular kind
                                                or rewriting solution to the word problem for the non-spherical rank
                                                3 Artin groups (and more).


                                           1    Introduction
                                           Our main result in this article are the following.
                                           Theorem 1.1. Let G be the Artin group
                                                         G = ha, b, c | aba = bab, ac = ca, n (b, c) = n (c, b)i,
                                           for n ≥ 5. Then there is a system R of length preserving rewrite rules that,
                                           together with free reduction, can be used to reduce any word over {a, b, c} to
                                           a geodesic word in G, in quadratic time.
                                           Furthermore, any two geodesic words representing the same element of G
                                           are related by a sequence of rules in R, each of those involving just two
                                           generators.

                                                                                     1
The notation n (x, y) used in the statement of Theorem 1.1 denotes an alter-
nating string xyxy · · · of length n beginning with x, as explained in Section 2.
Throughout this article, G will be as defined in the statement of Theorem 1.1.
The faat any rank 3 Artin group has quadratic solution to the word problem
follows immediately from the combination of Theorem 1.1 and the proofs
of quadratic bound for the word problem in spherical type Artin groups
(derived in [5] for all Garside groups), in large type groups (derived in [7]
as a consequence of their automaticity), and for 3-free Artin groups (derived
in [1]). However we note that this result is already a consequence of the
combination of Corollary G and Theorem D of a very recent preprint of
Haettel and Huang [6].
Example 4.5 below shows that the condition that n be at least 5 is necessary
for Theorem 1.1 to hold.
That the word problem in the group G is soluble was already known; it is
rather the type of solution given by Theorem 1.1 that we consider to be of
interest, and what this tells us about not just the complexity of the word
problem but also the structure of geodesics in G.
We note that for n > 5 the group G is locally non-spherical (also known as
2-dimensional ) and the solubility of its word problem is a consequence of
the main theorem of [3] (Chermak’s algorithm reduces a word representing
the identity to the empty word using a combination of steps none of which
increases its length, but does not claim to reduce any input word to a geodesic
representative); for n ≤ 5 the group is of spherical type and the word problem
is solved in [4] (the result is generalised to Garside groups in [5]). However,
our result shows that the method developed in [7, 8] and then extended in
[1] can be extended further to apply to our group G. The method allows
any non-geodesic word to be rewritten to a geodesic in a series of length
reducing steps, each step a sequence of length preserving moves followed by
free reduction, taking linear time.
We observe that the structure of geodesics in rank three Artin groups is an
important component in the proof of the main result of [2]; the groups G for
n ≥ 6 were the only rank 3 groups that could not be included in that result at
the time of its publication, but now we have the machinery to deal with them.
We note also that Arye Juhasz has claimed a proof using small cancellation
theory that certain Artin groups have isoperimetric function that is at most
polynomial of degree 6; his work considers all 3-free groups, as well as our
groups G when n ≥ 6 ([9], and private communication).
The length preserving rules of the system R that are referred to above and
in the statement of Theorem 1.1 generalise the τ -moves of [7, 8, 1]. Each rule

                                       2
has the effect of replacing a critical subword u of the input word by another
word τ (u) of the same length, whose first and last letters both differ from
the corresponding letters within u. When a sequence of such moves leads
to a reduction of the input word, it does so by moving from left to right
within the word, replacing successive overlapping subwords, until the letter
at the righthand end of the final replacement subword is the inverse of the
subsequent letter in the word, and so a free cancellation is possible.
In the large and sufficiently large groups studied in [7, 8], the word problem
can be solved as just described using critical subwords which by definition are
2-generated with particular constraints. In [1] the same type of solution was
found to work with a more general definition of a critical word that allowed it
to be pseudo 2-generated but not necessarily 2-generated. In this article we
define 3-generated critical words, and prove that, if these are used alongside
the others, then the same type of solution works in our group G.
We observe that our arguments can be applied without modification to prove
the result of Theorem 1.1 for those rank 3 Artin groups for which the param-
eter 3 is replaced by any integer greater than 3, and in that case no τ -moves
to 3-generator critical words of type {a, b, c} are necessary. Of course in that
case G is 3-free, and so the results of [1] apply. So this observation indicates
the very close connection between our methods and those of [1]
Our article is structured as follows. After this introduction and a section
explaining our notation, we define critical words of three different types and
the τ -moves that deal with them in Section 3, recalling the definitions of
critical words that are 2-generated or pseudo 2-generated from [7] and [1],
and then providing a definition for 3-generated critical words. We observe
some basic properties of these words and their associated τ -moves. Then
in Section 4 we define rightward reducing sequences of τ -moves. We define
a set W to consist of all words that do not admit such a sequence, and
describe a procedure, Procedure 4.10, that can be used to reduces any word
to a representative of the same element within W in quadratic time; its
correctness is proved in Proposition 4.11. Finally Section 5 contains the proof
of Theorem 1.1. That section starts with the statement of Theorem 5.1, which
restates the result of Theorem 1.1, providing more detail; in particular the
set W is proved to be the set of all geodesic representatives of the elements
of G, and the set R of Theorem 1.1 is found to be the set of all rightward
reducing sequences. Technical details needed in the proof of Theorem 5.1
are provided in Propositions 5.2–5.5, stated and proved after the proof of the
theorem.
We end this section with an example, which indicates why we needed to
generalise the work of [1] in order to deal with the group G.

2     Notation
We define S := {a, b, c}, the set of standard generators of the Artin group
G, and A := S ∪ S −1 , the set of standard generators and their inverses.

                                           4
For X ⊆ A, we defined a word over X to be a string of elements from X ∪X −1 ;
the element of that string are called the letters of w. The length of a word
w is its length as a string. Elements of S = {a, b, c} are called positive
generators, and elements of S −1 := {a−1 , b−1 , c−1 } negative generators; a
word is called positive if it involves only positive generators, negative if it
involves only negative generators, and otherwise is called unsigned ; a letter
could be positive or negative. We define the name of a letter x to be the
positive generator within {x, x−1 } to which it corresponds. A word is called
geodesic in G if it has minimal length as a representative of the element of
G that it represents.
For x, y, z ∈ A with distinct names, we refer to words over {x, y} as {x, y}-
words, also as 2-generator words, and we refer to words over {x, y, z} as
{x, y, z}-words, also as 3-generator words.
For x, y ∈ A, as above, we define n (x, y) to be the alternating string xyxy · · ·
of length n beginning with x and (y, x)n to be the alternating string · · · yxyx
of length n ending with x If x, y are both positive, such a string is called
positive alternating of length n, while if x, y are both negative, it is called
negative alternating of length n.
For any word w, we denote by f[w] and lw its first and last letter, and by
pref[w] and suf[w] its maximal proper prefix and maximal proper suffix. Note
that w = f[w]suf[w] = pref[w]lw.


3     Critical words and τ -moves
The concept of a critical word as a particular type of 2-generator word in
an Artin group was introduced in [7], and in that article and [8] those words
were an essential part of a process for reduction in large and sufficiently large
Artin groups. In this article we shall need also to define critical 3-generator
words; these will be of two kinds: pseudo 2-generated words (introduced in
[1]) and 3-generator critical words of type {a, b, c}, both defined in Section 3.2
below.


3.1    Critical words and τ -moves involving just 2 gener-
       ators
Critical 2-generator words are geodesic in 2-generated Artin groups [10] and
vital to the recognition of and reduction to geodesics in those Artin groups
that are studied in [7, 8, 1]. We recall the definition and some technical

shall need in this article; the proofs could easily be omitted from a first
reading.
Let X = {x, y} be a subset of S with x 6= y, and let mxy be the integer
associated with x, y in the presentation of G. The element ∆ of the subgroup
hx, yi of G that is represented by the alternating product (x, y)mxy is known as
the Garside element of hx, yi and is also represented by the other alternating
product (y, x)mxy . Let v be a word w over X. Following [7] we define p(v)
to be the minimum of mxy and the maximal length of a positive alternating
subword of v, and n(v) to be the minimum of mxy and the maximal length
of a negative alternating subword of v.

Definition 3.1 (2-generator critical words). The 2-generator word u is de-
fined to be critical if
   (i) p(u) + n(u) = mxy ; and
  (ii) either
         (a) u is positive of the form mxy (x, y)ξ or ξ(x, y)mxy where p(ξ) <
             mxy , or
         (b) u is negative of the form mxy (x−1 , y −1)ξ or ξ(x−1 , y −1)mxy where
             n(ξ) < mxy , or
         (c) u is unsigned, and has one of the two forms p(u) (x, y)ξ(z −1 , t−1 )n(u)
             or n(u) (x−1 , y −1)(x, y)ξ(z, t)p(u) , where {z, t} = {x, y}.

Lemma 3.5. Let w and w ′ be geodesic words over the standard generators
of a 2-generator Artin group that represent the same element of the group.
Then we can transform w to w ′ using a sequence of 2-generator τ -moves.

Proof. By [7, Theorem 2.4] we can transform each of w and w ′ to their
(equal) shortlex normal forms using a sequence of (2-generator) τ -moves.
Since the reverse of a 2-generator τ -move is also a 2-generator τ -move, the
result follows.

Lemma 3.8. Let v be an {a, b}-word such that f[v] and lv both have name
a. Then we can decide in linear time whether v can be transformed using a
sequence of τ -moves to a word bi aj bk with i, j, k nonzero integers and, if so,

Proof. We can calculate p(v) and n(v) in linear time and if p(v) + n(v) 6= 3
then no such transformation is possible, and we return false. So we can
assume that p(v) + n(v) = 3 and hence that v is a geodesic word. If v is a
positive word then, by Lemma 3.7, the transformation in question exists if
and only if either j = k = 1 and v = ak ba, or i = j = 1 and v = abai , which

So suppose that v is unsigned. Then v is critical by Lemma 3.7. We describe
a linear-time process that will transform v to a word of the form bi aj bk and
return true if possible, and return false otherwise. We shall not discuss how
this process could be implemented in linear time, except to note that such an
implementation might involve storing the word v and the transformed word
as doubly linked lists.
We read the word v from both ends simultaneously, i.e. leftwards from the
beginning of v and rightwards from its end, and we shall denote the central
part of v that has not yet been read by v ′ . We shall maintain a variable flip
that is true or false, and initially false. When flip is true the letters a
and a−1 of v ′ are regarded as being replaced b and b−1 respectively, and vice
versa. We shall call the letters of the word v ′ after making this replacement
if necessary the interpreted letters of v ′ (which are the same as the letters of
v ′ itself when flip is false).
At the beginning of the process we are reading the first and last letters of
v which, since v is critical, must be a and a−1 , not necessarily respectively.
Suppose, for example, that v has the prefix ab and the suffix a−1 ; all other
cases are similar. Then we replace the prefix ab by b−1 and the suffix a−1 by
ab. We then set the flag flip to true. Note that, if we were to replace the
letters of the unread part v ′ of v by their interpreted letters at this stage, then
we would have executed the τ -move on v, but we do not do that explicitly.
As we continue to read v ′ from both ends, if an interpreted letter has name
b, then we replace the original letter (if necessary) by the interpreted letter,
and we do this until the interpreted letters at both ends of v ′ have name a.
If this never happens (i.e. if v ′ becomes empty without this occurring) then
we return false. Otherwise, if the two interpreted letters are equal, then
we check whether the remainder of the interpreted word v ′ is just a power of
a, in which case, after replacing v ′ by its interpreted word if necessary, the
resulting transformed word has the required from, so we return true. If it is
not a power of a then we return false.
On the other hand, if the interpreted letters at the beginning and end of v ′
are a and a−1 then, by Lemma 3.7, if the required transformation is possible,

                                         9
then the interpreted word v ′ must be critical, which we can test for by looking
at the second letters from the beginning and end of v ′ . If it is not critical then
we return false. Otherwise we restart the process at this point, by making
the necessary changes to the beginning and end of v ′ , and then changing the
value of flip.


3.2    Critical words and τ -moves involving more than 2
       generators
In addition to the 2-generator critical words defined in Section 3.1, we shall
need two kinds of critical words involving 3 generators, both words that are
pseudo 2-generated as defined in [1] and some further words that we shall
call {a, b, c}-critical, and define below. In the article [1] a word that is pseudo
2-generated can involve any number of generators in addition to its selected
pair of non-commuting pseudo generators; but in this article a pseudo 2-
generated (P2G) word u must have pseudo-generators {x, b}, where either
x = a or x = c and at most one further generator z, commuting with x,
and so equal to c or a. We call such words P2G words of type {x, b} and
define them now. Since there is only one further generator within G, we can
simplify some details of the definition of P2G words from [1].

In order to reduce a word w using a sequence of τ -moves (including the new

definition of [1], which was already based on a definition within [7, 8]. We
use notation similar to that of [1] to facilitate comparison.

As in [1], we can think of an RRS as a way of applying successive τ -moves

by τ (u1 ) (or by suf[w1 ]f[w1 ] when m = 0). For m > 0, this results in a word

the freely reducing subword f[γ]−1 f[γ].

We define an optimal RRS for a word in a (2, 3, n)-group G as follows (fol-
lowing [1]).
Definition 4.6 (Optimal RRS). We say that an RRS U = u1 , . . . , um+1 for
w, with associated decomposition w = µw1 · · · wm+1 γ, is optimal if
   (i) the first letter of w1 is at least as far to the right as in any other RRS
       for w, i.e. µ is as long as we found possible,
  (ii) f[γ] does not appear in wm+1 ,
 (iii) if, for some i > 1 with 1 < i ≤ m, ui−1 is a P2G word of type {x, y}
       and αi = ǫ, then ui is
        either a P2G word of type {z, t} with |{x, y} ∩ {z, t}}| = 1
          or a critical word of type {a, b, c} with |{x, y} ∩ {b, c}}| = 1.

Note that our condition (iii) is slightly different from the third condition for
optimality of [1]; that is because we have no generator in G that commutes
with two other generators. We prefer to state our condition (iii) in this form
for greater readability. Note also that it is possible to have two successive
τ -moves of type {a, b, c} in an optimal RRS.
We shall use the following properties of an optimal RRS.

5     Proof of the main theorem
We recall Definition 4.9 of W as the set of words admitting no RRS. Our
main theorem will be an immediate consequence of the following result, which
specifies the rewrite system R.
Theorem 5.1. Let G be the Artin group with presentation
                G = ha, b, c | aba = bab, ac = ca, n (b, c) = n (c, b)i,
for n ≥ 5, and let W be as defined above. Then the words in W are precisely
the words that are geodesics in G.
There is a process that, given an input word w := x1 · · · xn , runs in quadratic
time to find geodesic representatives w0 , · · · , wn in W of successive prefixes

                                           26
of w, in each case by applying an RRS of τ -moves to the word wi−1 xi , and
hence finding a geodesic representative wn for w, and thereby solving the word
problem in G.
Furthermore, if w, w ′ ∈ W represent the same element of G then there is a
sequence of commutations and τ -moves applied to critical 2-generator sub-
words that transforms w to w ′ .

This section contains the proof of Theorem 5.1. We describe the steps of the
proof now. The proof uses the results of Section 4 together with details that
are verified in Propositions 5.2–5.5 that follow.
We need to prove that a word w is in the set W of words admitting no RRS
(see Definition 4.1) if and only if it is geodesic. We observed as we defined W
that it must contain all geodesic words. We recall that if a word w admits an
RRS then it admits an optimal RRS which, by Proposition 4.11, is unique
for words w with pref[w] ∈ W .
In order to establish the first statement of Theorem 5.1, we need to show that
a non-geodesic word must admit an RRS. To do that, we define a binary
relation ∼ on A∗ by w ∼ w ′ if and only if w ′ can be obtained from w by
carrying out a sequence of commutations and τ -moves on critical 2-generator
subwords. It is clear that ∼ is an equivalence relation, and Propositions 5.2 (i)
and 5.3 (i) below ensure that it restricts to an equivalence relation on W . For
w ∈ W , we denote the equivalence class of w by [w] and we denote the set
of all equivalence classes within W by Ω.
We aim to prove that Ω corresponds bijectively to G, where g ∈ G corre-
sponds to [w] for any geodesic representative w of g. Our strategy is to define
a right action of G on Ω. This action will have the property that, whenever
x ∈ A and w, wx ∈ W , we have [w]x = [wx]. We shall now complete the
proof of Theorem 5.1, and provide the details of the action subsequently.
Let w, w ′ ∈ W be two representatives of the same element of G. Then,
since all prefixes of w and w ′ lie in W , we see that the elements [w], [w ′ ]
of Ω are equal to the images of [ǫ] under the action of the elements of G
represented by w, w ′. Since w, w ′ represent the same element, it follows that
[w] = [w ′ ]. This establishes the final assertion of Theorem 5.1. Conversely,
the definition of ∼ ensures that any two words related by ∼ must represent
the same element. Hence we have a correspondence between the elements of
G and the equivalence classes of ∼ on W .
But now if w, w ′ ∈ W represent the same element, with w ′ non-geodesic
but w non-geodesic then, since the two words represent the same element
we must have [w] = [w ′], so w ∼ w ′. But the definition of ∼ ensures two

                                       27
equivalent words have the same length, which is a contradiction. It follows
that every word in W is geodesic.
The proof of the first statement within Theorem 5.1 is now complete. So
now suppose that w = x1 · · · xn over A is input. Setting w0 = ǫ, for each
i = 1, . . . , n we apply Proposition 4.11 to find in time linear in i a word
wi ∈ W that represents wi−1 xi . Hence in time quadratic in n, we find wn ∈ W
that represents W . The first statement within Theorem 5.1 ensures that wn
is geodesic, and hence we can decide whether w =G 1. This completes the
proof of Theorem 5.1.

Proposition 5.4. We have [wac] = [wca] for all w ∈ W .

Proof. Suppose first that wa ∈ W . Then wac 6∈ W if and only if wac admits
an RRS the application of which replaces the final a of wa by c−1 , in which
case w is equivalent to a word of form w ′ c−1 . But that is the case if and
only if wc 6∈ W , in which case [wac] = [wca] = [w ′ a]. Otherwise wac and
wc are both in W , in which case so is wca by Proposition 5.2 and, since wac
and wca are equivalent words in W , we again have [wac] = [wca]. The proof
when wc ∈ W is analogous.
It remains to deal with the case when neither wa no wc is in W . Then w is
equivalent to a word of form w ′ a−1 . Since w ′ a−1 c 6∈ W , reasoning as in the
previous paragraph, we see that w ′ is equivalent to a word of form w ′′ c−1 ,
and then [wac] = [wca] = w ′′ .
