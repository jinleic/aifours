                                                         On lexicographic representatives in braid monoids




arXiv:1808.02755v2 [math.GR] 10 Aug 2018
                                                                           Ramón Flores, Juan González-Meneses∗

                                                                                            August, 2018



                                                                                               Abstract
                                                      The language of maximal lexicographic representatives of elements in the positive braid monoid
                                                  An with n generators is a regular language. We describe with great detail the smallest Finite State
                                                  Automaton accepting such language, and study the proportion of elements of length k whose maximal
                                                  lexicographic representative finishes with the first generator. This proportion tends to some number
                                                  Pn,1 , as k tends to infinity, and we show that Pn,1 ≥ 18 for every n ≥ 1. We also provide an explicit
                                                  formula, based on the Fibonacci numbers, for the number of states of the automaton.



                                           1      Introduction
                                           The positive braid monoid with n generators, An , also known as the spherical type Artin-Tits monoid
                                           of type An , or as the positive braid monoid on n + 1 strands Bn+1 , is the monoid with the following
                                           presentation:                                                                   
                                                                                            ai aj = aj ai    if |j − i| > 1
                                                                    An = a1 , . . . , an
                                                                                         ai aj ai = aj ai aj if |j − i| = 1

                                           Since the appearance of the seminal survey included in [1], which in turn reunited different notions that
                                           have circulated some years around the group-theoretic community, there has been a growing interest in
                                           the role of Finite State Automata in Group Theory. In particular, in the context of Artin groups and
                                           monoids, different automata have been proposed, see for example [2] or [6].
                                           In the present paper, we deeply analyze a Finite State Automaton proposed by the second author and V.
                                           Gebhardt in [4]. In that paper, a polynomial algorithm is given to select a random element of An , among
                                           all elements of given length, with uniform probability. The difficulty of this task comes from the fact
                                           that a given positive braid β may admit many different words in the generators a1 , . . . , an representing
                                           it (although all representatives have the same length, as the relations in An are homogeneous). In order
                                           to be able to count the elements in An of length k, one can choose a unique representative for each
                                           element. For instance, one can order lexicographically the (finite set of) words representing β, setting
                                           a1 < a2 < · · · < an , and choose the smallest element. With this ordering, it is shown in [4] that the
                                           language Ln of smallest lexicographic representatives of elements of An is a regular language (a fact that
                                           was previously known), and the mentioned Finite State Automaton Γn accepting this language is defined.
                                           It is proved in the mentioned paper that Γn is the smallest (in terms of number of states) Deterministic
                                           Finite State Automaton accepting Ln . Actually, in [4], the language and the automaton for An are
                                           denoted Ln+1 and Γn+1 , respectively, as the index indicates the number of strands instead of the number
                                           of generators, but in the present paper we prefer to shift the indices to simplify the notations.
                                           Our immediate motivation to study the mentioned Finite State Automaton comes from our paper [3].
                                           One of our interests there is to determine the limit of the growth rates of the monoids An , as n tends

The proportion of elements in the language Ln finishing with a given generator has a sense only if we
fix the length of the words, so let Ln,k be the (finite) set of words in Ln having length k. We see that
Ln,0 = {ǫ} (the empty word) and Ln,1 = {a1 , . . . , an }. Also,

                 Ln,2 = {a1 a1 , a1 a2 } ∪ {a2 a1 , a2 a2 , a2 a3 } ∪ · · · ∪ {an a1 , an a2 , · · · , an an }.

Notice that the words ai aj with j ≥ i + 2 do not appear, as in that case ai aj = aj ai in An , and the latter
is the biggest lexicographic representative. The case of Ln,3 is more involved, as we must also take into
account the relations ai ai+1 ai = ai+1 ai ai+1 .
Recall that Ln is a regular language. This implies that, when k tends to infinity, the proportion of words
in Ln,k which end at a given state s of the automaton tends to a well defined limit 0 ≤ ps < 1. The set
of numbers ps for all states s in Γn is called the stationary distribution of Γn . We can understand ps as
the probability that a very long word in Ln finishes at the state s (considering all words of given length
in Ln with uniform probability).
We will see that all words in Ln which end at a given state in Γn finish with the same letter. That is,
if we see Γn as a directed graph, whose arrows are labeled by the generators a1 , . . . , an , all the incoming
arrows of a given state s have the same label. It follows that, when k tends to infinity, the proportion of
words of length k finishing with ai , tends to the sum of the numbers ps for all states s whose incoming
arrows are labeled by ai . This limit is thus well defined, and it is the probability that a very long word
in Ln finishes with ai . Let us denote it by Pn,i .
It seems natural that for each n > 2, the proportion Pn,1 is greater than Pn,i for i ≥ 1. The question arises
about how far is this proportion from the uniform distribution n1 . It turns out that the lexicographic
representatives ending with a1 are much more abundant than one could think, since the proportion Pn,1
has a uniform lower bound, independent of n. We can show the following:
Theorem 5.4. Let Pn,1 be the limit, when k tends to infinity, of the proportion of maximal lexicographic
representatives of length k in An finishing with a1 . Then Pn,1 > 81 for every n ≥ 1.

2     The automaton
A standard reference about finite state automata in group theory is [1], and a modern approach can be
found in [5].
In this paper, a finite state automaton Γ will be given as a directed graph, whose vertices will be called
states, and whose arrows, called transitions, are labeled by letters from a finite alphabet {a1 , . . . , an }.
There will be one special vertex called the initial state. Γ is a Deterministic Finite State Automaton
(DFSA) if for every state s and every i ∈ {1, . . . , n}, there is at most one arrow starting at s and labeled
ai . All states in Γ will be considered to be accepted.
A path α in Γ is a sequence of arrows α1 , . . . , αt such that the target of αi equals the source of αi+1 , for
i = 1, . . . , t − 1. The source of the path α is the source of α1 , and the target of α is the target of αt .
The word associated to α is the concatenation of the labels of α1 , α2 , . . . , αt . A word w in the alphabet
{a1 , . . . , an } is accepted by Γ if there is a path in Γ whose source is the initial state, and whose associated
word is w. The set of accepted words form the language accepted by Γ.
Given the way in which we are going to deal with the natural inclusions A1 ⊂ A2 ⊂ A3 ⊂ · · · and
the notation of [3], it will be convenient for us to consider the biggest lexicographic representatives,
instead of the smallest as in [4]. This is not a big issue, as the map ρ sending ai to an+1−i is a monoid
automorphism of An (as it can be immediately deduced from the monoid presentation) and, applied
to words, it sends the smallest lexicographic representative of a braid β to the biggest lexicographic
representative of ρ(β), and vice-versa. In other words, the language of biggest representatives Ln for
elements in An with a1 < a2 < · · · < an corresponds (via ρ) to the language of smallest representatives,
also with a1 < a2 < · · · < an . So, for every n ≥ 1, we will use the definition of the DFSA in [4] to describe
a DFSA Γn , with alphabet {a1 , . . . , an }, whose accepted language is the set Ln of biggest lexicographic
representatives of elements of An .
Recall that An is the monoid of positive braids with n generators. In this monoid we say that β1 is a
prefix of β, and we write β1 4 β, if there exists β2 ∈ An such that β = β1 β2 . Given a braid β ∈ An , we
denote by ω(β) ∈ Ln its biggest lexicographic representative. Conversely, given a word w in the alphabet
{a1 , . . . , an }, we will denote by w ∈ An the braid it represents. It is clear that ω(β) = β for every β ∈ An ,
and that ω(w) = w if and only if w ∈ Ln .
Given a word w in the alphabet {a1 , . . . , an }, we define the support of w, denoted supp(w), as the set of
letters which appear in w. Notice that applying a relation from the presentation of An does not change
the support of a word. It follows that all words representing a given braid β have the same support, so
we can talk about the support of β, denoted supp(β), as the support of any of its representatives. Also,
applying a relation from the presentation of An does not change the length of a word, so all representatives
of a braid have the same length, and we can talk about the length |β| of a braid β, as the length of any
of its representatives.
Given β ∈ An , we say that a decomposition β = β1 β2 is permitted if ω(β) = ω(β1 )ω(β2 ), and is forbidden
otherwise. Notice that, if |β| = k, there is exactly one permitted decomposition of β for each number
k1 = 0, . . . , k. Indeed, the word w = ω(β) has a unique prefix w1 of length k1 . Hence, if we write



                                                        3
w = w1 w2 , we have that β = w1 w2 is the only permitted decomposition whose left factor has length k1 .
For example, if we consider the braid β = a1 a2 a1 ∈ An , the decomposition given by β1 = a1 and
β2 = a2 a1 is forbidden, because ω(β1 ) = a1 , ω(β2 ) = a2 a1 , but ω(β) = a2 a1 a2 , which is not the same
word as ω(β1 )ω(β2 ) = a1 a2 a1 . The only permitted decompositions of β are those obtained from prefixes
of its lexicographic representative a2 a1 a2 .
Notice that the biggest lexicographic representative of a braid β is determined by its permitted decom-
positions. Hence, it is also determined by its forbidden decompositions.
If a decomposition β = β1 β2 is forbidden, we say that β2 is forbidden after β1 . It is important to notice
that if β2 is forbidden after β1 , then β2 β3 is also forbidden after β1 , for every β3 ∈ An . Therefore, in
order to determine all braids which are forbidden after β1 , it suffices to know those which are minimal
with respect to the prefix order. This justifies the following definition.
Definition 2.1. [4] Given a braid β1 ∈ An , the set of minimal forbidden prefixes after β1 is:

                              Fn (β1 ) = min{β2 ∈ An ; ω(β1 β2 ) 6= ω(β1 )ω(β2 )}.
                                          4


As we mentioned before, Fn (β1 ) determines the whole set of forbidden braids after β1 , which in turn
determines the set of words in Ln that start with ω(β1 ). The advantage of this notion is that there is
only a finite number of sets of the form Fn (β1 ), as we will see. Therefore, we can construct a DFSA
which accepts the language Ln in the following way:
Definition 2.2. [4] The automaton Γn is a directed graph defined as follows.

    • The vertices of Γn correspond to the sets of the form Fn (β1 ) for β1 ∈ An .
    • Whenever aj ∈/ Fn (β1 ), there is an arrow labeled aj whose source is Fn (β1 ) and whose target is
      Fn (β1 aj ).
Theorem 2.3. [4] Γn is a well defined DFSA, whose accepted language is Ln . Moreover, it is the smallest
DFSA (in terms of number of states) accepting Ln .

We have an immediate consequence of the above result:
Corollary 2.4. Let β1 , β1′ , β2 ∈ An . If β1 β2 is a permitted decomposition, and Fn (β1 ) = Fn (β1′ ), then
β1′ β2 is a permitted decomposition and Fn (β1′ β2 ) = Fn (β1 β2 ).

Proof. Let β = β1 β2 , w = ω(β), w1 = ω(β1 ) and w2 = ω(β2 ). Since the decomposition β = β1 β2 is
permitted, we have w = w1 w2 . We can then read the word w1 w2 in the automaton Γn , starting at the
initial state. After reading w1 , we are placed at the state Fn (β1 ). The final state (Fn (β)) only depends
on the current state Fn (β1 ), and the remaining part of the word, w2 .
Now let w1′ = ω(β1′ ). Since Fn (β1 ) = Fn (β1′ ), it follows that there is a path in Γn labeled by w1′ , starting
at the initial state and finishing at Fn (β1 ). If we continue reading the word w2 , we will finish at the state
Fn (β). Hence, there is a path in Γn labeled by w1′ w2 , starting at the initial state and finishing at Fn (β).
This implies that w1′ w2 ∈ Ln , that β1′ β2 is permitted, and that Fn (β1′ β2 ) = Fn (β) = Fn (β1 β2 ).

As said in the introduction, one of the main goals of this paper is a detailed study of the automaton Γn .
This goal will be achieved by identifying segment configurations with sets of minimal forbidden prefixes
after positive braids. More precisely, we will describe the map ψn just defined, following the next steps:

    • ψn is injective, and hence different segment configurations give rise to different sets of braids
      (Lemma 3.5).
    • Every set of the form Fn (β) is in the image of ψn (Proposition 3.6).
    • Every set in the image of ψn is equal to Fn (β) for some braid β (Proposition 3.8).

automaton, as we will see in the proof of Proposition 3.8. From now, we will undertake the previous

It seems clear that the probability of ending at the initial state tends to 0 as k tends to infinity, since

In the previous section we have shown two important properties of Γn . First, the states in sh(Γn−1 ) are
transient: once the process reaches the subgraph Γ∗n , it will never go back to sh(Γn−1 ). This is why the
probability of ending at a state in sh(Γn−1 ) tends to 0 as k tends to infinity. On the other hand, the
states in Γ∗n (all the remaining states in Γn ) are recurrent: for every ordered pair (v1 , v2 ) of states in Γ∗n ,
there is always a path going from v1 to v2 . This means that the probability of ending at such a state will
tend to a positive number.

Appendix: an algorithm to generate transition matrices.
In this paper we have described in detail the automaton Γn which accepts the language of maximal
lexicographic representatives for the braid monoid An . Thanks to this analysis, we have been able to
develop a computer program that generates the primitive transition matrix Rn corresponding to the
subgraph Γ∗n , from which the automaton Γn can be easily obtained: just recall that Γ1 = Γ∗1 , and that
Γn consists of a copy of Γn−1 (that we denoted sh(Γn−1 )) and a copy of Γ∗n , together with an arrow
(labeled a1 ) starting at each vertex of sh(Γn−1 ) and pointing to the first vertex of Γ∗n . If one is interested
in computing the actual automaton, the only information missing will be the labels of the arrows. These
labels can be obtained from the proof of Proposition 3.8.
Our main goal here is the computation of the matrix Rn , as its Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue corresponds
to the growth rate of An , and its Perron-Frobenius left-eigenvector shows the proportion of maximal
lexicographic representatives finishing at each state.
Recall (see Figure 4) that the automaton Γ∗n is very similar to the automaton sh• (Γ∗n ), the only difference
being that the arrows labeled a1 in Γ∗n point to the states t1,i , and the arrows labeled a2 in sh• (Γ∗n ) point
to either Γ∗1 or to the states t2,i . We will then use the same algorithm in both cases, with a flag (named
‘closed’) indicating whether we are computing Γ∗n (closed = 1) or sh• (Γ∗n ) (closed = 0).
We will order the rows of Rn (that is, the states of Γ∗n ) in a systematic way. See Figure 4. The first
n rows will correspond to the states t1,1 , . . . , t1,n . The next rows will correspond to sh• (Γ∗n−1 ). And
the final rows are ordered as they appear in Figure 4, by columns: first Γ∗1 , t2,3 , t2,4 , . . . , t2,n , then the
states in Γ∗2 , t3,4 , . . . , t3,n , and so on, until the final rows which correspond to the states of Γ∗n−1 . Inside
sh• (Γ∗n−1 ) (whose states correspond to those of Γ∗n−1 ), and inside each copy of Γ∗i for i = 1, . . . , n − 1,
the states will be ordered in the same way, by recurrence.
Notice that we can easily compute the position of any state in Figure 4 thanks to Theorem 4.2, as the
number of states of Γ∗n is precisely sn − sn−1 . We then need to pre-compute the numbers s1 , . . . , sn , where
si is the number of states in Γi . We will not write this explicitly, as it follows from the single formula in
Theorem 4.2. Or, even better, from the formula in Proposition 4.1. For i = 1, . . . , n, let s∗i = si − si−1
be the number of states in Γ∗i . We can assume we have computed all these numbers, for i = 1, . . . , n.
We will define a procedure Submatrix(R, j, H, s, closed), that will take a sparse matrix R (that will
eventually become Rn ) and will compute the submatrix corresponding to either Γ∗j (if closed = 1) or
sh• (Γ∗j ) (if closed = 0). It will place that submatrix starting at position (1 + s, 1 + s) of R (s is just the
shifting indicating where to place the submatrix). The input H is a vector that should be pre-computed,
and contains the exact positions of the source states of the horizontal arrows in Figure 4 going from Γ∗i
to Γ∗i+1 , for i = 1, . . . , j − 2.

why they can be pre-computed. By the proof of Proposition 3.8 we know that the mentioned arrows go

Therefore, in order to compute the transition matrix Rn , one just needs to do the following:

   1. Compute s∗1 , . . . , s∗n , using Proposition 4.1 (where s∗i = si − si−1 ).
   2. Compute H using Algorithm 1, taking j = n − 1.
   3. Initialize R as a s∗n × s∗n sparse zero matrix.
   4. Call Submatrix(R, n, H, 0, 1).

We know that the number of rows of Rn grows exponentially with respect to n, so we cannot compute
these matrices for big values of n; however, we can provide the computations for n = 2, . . . , 9. We
compute in this case the matrix Rn , the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue λn (which is the growth rate of
An ), the first entry of the Perron-Frobenius eigenvector whose coordinates add up to 1 (which is equal
to Pn,a1 ), and the sum of the first n entries of the same eigenvector (which is equal to Pn,1 ). The results
of this computation are shown in Table 1.
