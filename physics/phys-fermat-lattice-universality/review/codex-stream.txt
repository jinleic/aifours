    19	Write `Bad` for the set of real irrational numbers `x` for which there is `c>0` such that `|x-p/q| >= c/q^2` for all integers `p` and `q>=1`. Equivalently, the positive digits of the infinite simple continued fraction of `x` are bounded. This bounded-digit criterion is stated in Tevet §4, p. 6; the elementary continued-fraction identities used below are also derived where needed. Integer translation and negation preserve `Bad` directly from its definition.
    20	
    21	The non-elementary imported input is **McMullen's absolute-winning theorem**, with its exact accompanying invariances:
    22	
    23	1. For a hyperbolic lattice `Gamma` with a cusp at infinity, the set `D(Gamma)` of endpoints of bounded projected geodesic rays is absolutely winning. For `Gamma=SL_2(Z)`, this set is exactly `Bad`. This is Theorem 1.3 and the preceding identification, author manuscript p. 3.
    24	2. An absolute-winning subset of `R^n` remains absolute winning under any global quasisymmetric homeomorphism of `R^n`. This is Theorem 2.2, manuscript p. 5.
    25	3. Countable intersections of absolute-winning sets are absolute winning: §1, “Absolute winning,” property (2), p. 3. Such a set meets every nonempty open interval: start the defining game with Bob's closed interval inside that open interval and use a legal play with radii tending to zero.
    26	
    27	Primary source: Curtis T. McMullen, *Winning sets, quasiconformal maps and Diophantine approximation*, author manuscript dated 11 August 2009; published in *Geometric and Functional Analysis* (2010), DOI [10.1007/s00039-010-0078-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00039-010-0078-3). [Author PDF](https://abel.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/papers/home/text/papers/winning/winning.pdf), pinned locally as `source/mcmullen-winning.pdf`.
    28	
    29	The proof does **not** import a claim that a generic dense geodesic is bounded, a symbolic-dynamics specification theorem, or Tevet's shortest-neighbor Proposition 8. It does not infer local density from global Hausdorff dimension alone.

    31	## 2. Put an arbitrary lattice into two badly approximable ratios
    32	
    33	Identify the plane with `C`. Choose any negatively oriented basis `V,W0` of `Lambda`, so `det(V,W0)=-pi`. Adding an integer multiple of `V` to `W0` changes neither the lattice nor the determinant. Choose that integer large enough that the resulting basis `V,W` satisfies
    34	
    35	`W/V = x-i*h`, with `x>1`, `h>0`.
    36	
    37	Indeed `h=pi/|V|^2`. For a real parameter
    38	
    39	`h/(x+1) < t < h/x`,
    40	
    41	put `A=|V|/sqrt(1+t^2)`, `C=t*A`, and choose the rotation `R` of angle `arg(V)-arctan(t)`. Then
    42	
    43	`R^(-1)V = A+i*C`,
    44	
    45	`R^(-1)W = (x-i*h)(A+i*C) = A*(x+h*t) + i*A*(x*t-h)`.
    46	
    47	Consequently
    48	
    49	`R^(-1)V = (A,C)`,  `R^(-1)W = (A*beta,-C*u)`,
    50	
    51	where
    52	
    53	`beta=x+h*t > 1`,  `u=h/t-x in (0,1)`.
    54	
    55	The admissible beta interval is the nonempty open interval
    56	
    57	`J = (x+h^2/(x+1), x+h^2/x)`.
    58	
    59	On it,
    60	
    61	`u=g(beta)=h^2/(beta-x)-x`,  `g'(beta)=-h^2/(beta-x)^2`.
    62	
    63	Choose a nondegenerate closed interval `[a0,b0]` strictly inside `J`. Extend `g` from this interval to the whole real line using its tangent line at `a0` to the left and its tangent line at `b0` to the right. The extension `G` is a decreasing `C^1` bijection, and everywhere
    64	
    65	`0 < h^2/(b0-x)^2 <= |G'| <= h^2/(a0-x)^2 < infinity`.
    66	
    67	Thus `G` and its inverse are globally bi-Lipschitz, hence quasisymmetric. McMullen's results imply that
    68	
    69	`Bad intersect G^(-1)(Bad)`
    70	
    71	is absolute winning and therefore meets `(a0,b0)`. Choose `beta` in that intersection and set `u=g(beta)`. Both numbers are badly approximable, while every strict geometric inequality remains valid. Orientation reversal is allowed by McMullen's metric definition of quasisymmetry; alternatively apply the theorem to `-G`, since `-Bad=Bad`.
    72	
    73	Finally the determinant gives the exact identity
    74	
    75	`pi = A*C*(beta+u)`.                                                   (1)
    76	
    77	This proves the required geometric lemma for **every** target lattice, including highly elongated and nonalgebraic ones. No computability assumption on the entries of its basis was used. The winning-set selection is an existence argument, not an advertised finite algorithm for arbitrary real input.
    78	
    79	## 3. One bounded continued fraction realizes the two ratios
    80	
    81	Set `a=floor(beta)` and `r=beta-a`. Because `beta` is irrational, `a>=1` and `0<r<1`; both `r` and `u` are badly approximable. Write
    82	
    83	`r=[0;r1,r2,...]`,  `u=[0;u1,u2,...]`.
    84	
    85	There is a finite integer `M` bounding `a`, `1`, every `ri` and every `ui`.
    86	
    87	Construct one infinite digit word as follows. Starting with an empty prefix, at stage `k>=1`, let `ell` be the length already written. Choose `epsilon_k in {0,1}` so that `ell+epsilon_k+k` is even. Append `epsilon_k` copies of the digit **1**, then the block
    88	
    89	`r_k,r_(k-1),...,r_1, a, u_1,u_2,...,u_k`.                             (2)
    90	
    91	Let `j_k=ell+epsilon_k+k`, the position immediately before the central digit `a`. Thus `j_k` is even and the central digit has position `j_k+1`. The padding is **before** the prescribed block, so it does not damage either local digit window.
    92	
    93	Let `alpha=[0;d1,d2,...]` be the number represented by the resulting infinite word. Every digit lies in `{1,...,M}`, so `alpha` is irrational and badly approximable. This is one fixed number, not a new number at each stage, and the bound `M` does not increase with `k`.
    94	
    95	Use convergents `p_j/q_j=[0;d1,...,dj]`, with `p_-1=1,p_0=0,q_-1=0,q_0=1`. Define
    96	
    97	`r_j=q_(j-1)/q_j=[0;d_j,d_(j-1),...,d_1]`,
    98	
    99	`u_j=[0;d_(j+2),d_(j+3),...]`.
   100	
   101	At `j=j_k`, these have initial digit strings `r1,...,rk` and `u1,...,uk`, respectively. To check convergence without invoking a coding theorem, recall the elementary cylinder formula. If `P_l/Q_l` are the convergents of `[0;e1,...,el]`, all continuations are
   102	
   103	`(P_l+eta*P_(l-1))/(Q_l+eta*Q_(l-1))`,  `0<=eta<=1`.
   104	
   105	The endpoint difference is
   106	
   107	`1/(Q_l*(Q_l+Q_(l-1))) <= 1/Q_l^2`,
   108	
   109	because `|P_l*Q_(l-1)-P_(l-1)*Q_l|=1`, obtained immediately from the convergent recurrence. Positive digits make `Q_l` grow at least as Fibonacci denominators. Therefore, along the specified even indices,
   110	
   111	`r_j -> r`,  `u_j -> u`,  `q_(j+1)/q_j=a+r_j -> beta`.                 (3)
   112	
   113	For completeness, the complete quotient at position `j+1` is `a+u_j`, so
   114	
   115	`alpha=(p_j*(a+u_j)+p_(j-1))/(q_j*(a+u_j)+q_(j-1))`.
   116	
   117	Subtract `p_j/q_j`, use the determinant recurrence, and use even `j` to get
   118	
   119	`c_j=q_j*(q_j*alpha-p_j)=1/(a+u_j+r_j) -> c=1/(beta+u)`,                (4)
   120	
   121	`q_(j+1)*alpha-p_(j+1)=-u_j*(q_j*alpha-p_j)`.                          (5)
   122	
   123	The signs and parity in (4)–(5) are essential; they are not recovered from squared quantities.
   124	
   125	## 4. The exact local lattices approach the chosen basis
   126	
   127	For a positive integer `n`, let `rho=sqrt(n)` and define the full lattice
   128	
   129	`L_n(alpha) = {(q/(2*rho), 2*pi*rho*(q*alpha-p)) : (q,p) in Z^2}`.     (6)
   130	
   131	Its covolume is exactly `pi`. For the even index `j=j_k`, the integer pairs `(q_j,p_j)` and `(q_(j+1),p_(j+1))` have determinant `+1`. Hence they form an **entire basis**, not merely two independent vectors in a possible sublattice, of the integer input lattice in (6).
   132	
   133	Choose `n_k` to be a nearest positive integer to `(q_j/(2*A))^2`, discarding finitely many early terms if necessary. Then `n_k -> infinity` and
   134	
   135	`q_j/(2*sqrt(n_k)) -> A`.
   136	
   137	In the basis above, `L_(n_k)` has matrix
   138	
   139	`B_k = [[q_j/(2*sqrt(n_k)), q_(j+1)/(2*sqrt(n_k))],`
   140	`       [2*pi*sqrt(n_k)*(q_j*alpha-p_j),`
   141	`        2*pi*sqrt(n_k)*(q_(j+1)*alpha-p_(j+1))]]`.
   142	
   143	Equations (3)–(5) and (1) yield
   144	
   145	`B_k -> B = [[A,A*beta],[C,-C*u]]`,  `det(B)=-pi`.                     (7)
   146	
   147	Indeed the first lower entry tends to `pi*c/A=C`, and the second tends to `-C*u`. This proves an actual limit to the chosen lattice coordinates, not just a dense approximation among different angles.
   148	
   149	Convergence of nonsingular basis matrices implies local convergence of their lattices: every `B*z`, `z in Z^2`, is approximated by `B_k*z`; conversely, bounded `B_k*z_k` have bounded integer `z_k` since the inverses `B_k^(-1)` are uniformly bounded, so every convergent subsequence has a further subsequence with constant `z_k`. Thus `L_(n_k) -> B Z^2` in the Chabauty–Fell topology.
   150	
   151	## 5. Uniform local comparison with the actual spiral
   152	
   153	This elementary lemma was developed jointly with the independent companion classification worker; it is written out here so that the proof and reviews are self-contained. No companion implementation is imported or copied.
   154	
   155	Let `P_n=sqrt(n)*exp(2*pi*i*n*alpha)`, `rho=sqrt(n)` and
   156	
   157	`Y_n=exp(-2*pi*i*n*alpha)*(X_alpha-P_n)`.
   158	
   159	The rotation in this definition is an **analysis coordinate frame**, not an allowed transformation of the spiral in the theorem.
   160	
   161	**Forward bound.** Let `v=(q/(2*rho),2*pi*rho*(q*alpha-p)) in L_n` have norm at most `R`, and suppose `rho>=4*R`. Then `n+q>=n/2>0`, so the corresponding index is allowed. With `theta=2*pi*(q*alpha-p)` and `s=sqrt(n+q)`,
   162	
   163	`Ypoint=s*exp(i*theta)-rho`,  `v=q/(2*rho)+i*rho*theta`.
   164	
   165	Here `|q|<=2*R*rho`, `|theta|<=R/rho`, `s<=3*rho/2`, and
   166	
   167	`|s-rho|<=2*R`,
   168	
   169	`|s-rho-q/(2*rho)|=q^2/(2*rho*(s+rho)^2)<=2*R^2/rho`.
   170	
   171	Using `|exp(i*theta)-1-i*theta|<=theta^2/2`,
   172	
   173	`|Ypoint-v| <= 2*R^2/rho + 2*R^2/rho + 3*R^2/(4*rho)`
   174	`             <= 5*R^2/rho`.                                         (8)
   175	
   176	Also `|q*alpha-p|<=1/(8*pi)<1/2`, so `p` is the unique nearest integer to `q*alpha` in this local range.
   177	
   178	**Reverse bound.** If `Ypoint=s*exp(i*theta)-rho` has norm at most `R` and `rho>=12*R`, choose the principal argument `theta=arg(rho+Ypoint)` and the integer `p` with `theta=2*pi*(q*alpha-p)`. Since `|s-rho|<=R`,
   179	
   180	`|q/(2*rho)|<=R+R^2/(2*rho)<=25*R/24`.
   181	
   182	The real part of `rho+Ypoint` is positive, and
   183	
   184	`|theta|<=R/(rho-R)`, hence `|rho*theta|<=12*R/11`.
   185	
   186	Thus the corresponding `v` has norm less than `3*R`. Apply (8) at radius `3*R` to obtain
   187	
   188	`|Ypoint-v|<=45*R^2/rho`.                                             (9)
   189	
   190	These are neighborhood inclusions into the **full** opposing sets. They are not an incorrect same-radius truncated Hausdorff claim, which would be sensitive to points on the ball boundary. For each fixed `R`, the errors tend to zero. Equations (8)–(9) and the basis convergence in (7) therefore imply `Y_(n_k) -> B Z^2`.
   191	
   192	## 6. Physical orientation without rotating any translate
   193	
   194	For any positive integers `m,n`, the model lattices obey the exact relation
   195	
   196	`L_m = diag(sqrt(n/m),sqrt(m/n))*L_n`.                                 (10)
   197	
   198	Thus changing `n_k` by an integer `b_k` with `b_k=o(n_k)` does not alter the limit in (7).
   199	
   200	We need the following elementary uniform form of irrational-rotation density. For an irrational `alpha` and any `epsilon>0`, some finite set `{exp(2*pi*i*b*alpha):0<=b<=H}` is an epsilon-net of the unit circle. One proof uses pigeonhole to find `0<||q*alpha||<delta`, then uses the points at indices `0,q,2q,...,floor(1/||q*alpha||)*q`; they traverse a circle grid in one of the two directions with gaps at most `||q*alpha||`. Take `delta` arbitrarily small. Rotating this finite net by any starting phase preserves the net property.
   201	
   202	Let `H_k=floor(sqrt(n_k))`. Then `H_k -> infinity` and `H_k/n_k -> 0`. For any desired rotation `R_theta`, choose `0<=b_k<=H_k` so that
   203	
   204	`exp(2*pi*i*(n_k+b_k)*alpha) -> exp(i*theta)`.
   205	
   206	The finite-net argument gives this for the whole sequence, not only a subsequence; for example choose a distance-minimizing index in each finite window. Set `m_k=n_k+b_k`. Equation (10) preserves the model-lattice limit, and the local comparison applies equally at `m_k`. Therefore
   207	
   208	`X_alpha-P_(m_k) -> R_theta*B Z^2`.                                   (11)
   209	
   210	Take `R_theta=R` from §2 to obtain `Lambda`. Every other physical rotation is obtained by choosing another target angle, with the **same** `alpha`. Adding a fixed vector `b` to the translating maps gives `R_theta*B Z^2+b`, and
   211	
   212	`|-P_(m_k)+b| >= sqrt(m_k)-|b| -> infinity`.
   213	
   214	All transformations in (11) are translations. The coordinate rotation in §5 disappears through the selected phases; there is no post-hoc rotation or dilation. This completes the proof of Theorem U.

   216	## 7. What was and was not settled
   217	
   218	- **Settled by the written proof:** the full all-lattice existence statement with covolume exactly `pi`, a single fixed badly approximable angle for each shape, translation-only escape to infinity, and arbitrary orientation/affine offset.
   219	- **Not asserted:** one badly approximable angle works for every lattice shape; an effective uniform bound on partial quotients independent of the target; a finite algorithm selecting the winning-set point for arbitrary noncomputable real input; optimal packing or elastic-energy selection; the book's cone chirality conjecture.
   220	- **Why the core obligation is bounded:** two simultaneous badly-approximable real ratios are selected by a published winning-set theorem; an explicit bounded-alphabet block construction realizes them. No bounded-orbit realization or generic-geodesic assumption remains.
   221	- **Novelty boundary:** the exact question is explicitly open in the cited 2025 preprint. A bounded follow-up screen cannot establish worldwide priority, and a classical proof assembled here is labeled Certification rather than automatically Discovery.

   223	## 8. Frozen finite verification protocol
   224	
   225	Finite computations exercise the construction and distinguish modeling errors; they do not prove the all-target theorem. The fixed protocol is recorded before its first run:
   226	
   227	- Four exact quadratic examples: square (`x=0,h^2=1,beta=(1+sqrt(5))/2`); rectangular (`x=0,h^2=9,beta=9+(1+sqrt(5))/2`); hexagonal (`x=1/2,h^2=3/4,beta=sqrt(3)`); skew (`x=1/3,h^2=5/4,beta=(1+sqrt(15))/2`). For each, set `u=h^2/(beta-x)-x`, `t=(beta-x)/h`, `|V|=sqrt(pi/h)`, `A=|V|/sqrt(1+t^2)`, `C=t*A`. Verify `beta>1` and `0<u<1` before evaluating.
   228	- Exact periodic continued fractions for `r={beta}` and `u`; construct the infinite word's finite prefixes with parity padding. Inspect pivots `k=6,10,14`; determine an enclosing cylinder from the common prefix through stage 24. It is the same infinite constructed `alpha` at every pivot, not stage-dependent rational or quadratic replacements. Record the cylinder width and its propagated phase uncertainty.
   229	- Use high-precision arithmetic chosen from the actual convergent sizes, exact integer recurrences and an independently evaluated complex spiral displacement. Inspect integer basis combinations in `[-2,2]^2` and phase windows of lengths `128,512,2048`. All numerical observations remain Empirical evidence even when accompanied by conservative cylinder bounds.
   230	- Check unimodularity, covolume normalization, signed convergent-error formulas, the local Taylor bound, phase-window mesh bounds, and final relative basis error below `1e-4`. No finite sample is promoted to a proof of boundedness for an unspecified angle.
   231	- Negative controls deliberately break the factor of `pi`, reverse the wrong digit history, and replace a unimodular basis by an index-two sublattice. These must be detected. No optimization, random seeds, parameter adaptation or large point-cloud search.

Further Questions
The author conjectures that there is a spiral set that is a dense forest, this is
in contrast to a conjecture of Adiceam and Tsokanos in [AT22]. A possible
avenue to consider is noting that if all of the Chabauty limits of a set are dense
forests, the set is also a dense forest (by Proposition 6), and then constructing a
spiral set whose limits are similar to the construction given by Bishop in [Bis11]
using the lattice limits given by Fermat spirals. If there are indeed spiral sets
that are dense forests, one might ask what is the best visibility function, and in
particular whether there is a Danzer spiral set.
    We saw a classification of all of the lattices that can be achieved, however,
this classification is quite difficult to use. In particular, the question whether
every lattice with co-volume π is achieved as a Chabauty limit of some Fermat
spiral with badly-approximable angle difference remains.
    A third question is what are the Chabauty limits of Fermat spirals with
irrational angle difference that is not badly-approximable. As noted above,
the author conjectures that the limits are isomorphic to ∅, R, Z2 , and Z × R.
Computational evidence suggests they are all achieved, but a full classification
could be interesting, and in particular do the lattices also have co-volume π.

                                     Abstract
          This paper describes two new types of winning sets in Rn , defined
      using variants of Schmidt’s game. These strong and absolute winning
      sets include many Diophantine sets of measure zero and first category,
      and have good behavior under countable intersections. Most notably,
      they are invariant under quasiconformal maps, while classical winning

      1       Introduction
      Winning sets. Suppose two players, A and B, take turns choosing a
      nested sequence of closed Euclidean balls

                           B1 ⊃ A1 ⊃ B2 ⊃ A2 ⊃ B3 · · ·

      in Rn whose diameters satisfy, for fixed 0 < α, β < 1,

                        |Ai | = α|Bi | and |Bi+1 | = β|Ai |.              (1.1)

Following Schmidt [Sch], we say E ⊂ Rn is an    T (α, β)-winning set if
player A has a strategy which guarantees that Ai meets E. We say
E is an α-winning set if it is (α, β)-winning for all 0 < β < 1, and a
winning set if it is α-winning for some α > 0.
    Winning sets have many useful properties; for example:
  1. Any winning set in Rn has Hausdorff dimension n.
  2. A countable intersection of α-winning sets is α-winning.
  3. Winning sets are preserved by bi-Lipschitz homeomorphisms of
     Rn .
See [Sch] and [Dani3, Prop 5.3]. However, as we will see in §4:

Theorem 1.1 Winning sets are generally not preserved by quasisym-
metric maps.

   Here a map φ : Rn → Rn is k-quasisymmetric if for any ball
B(x, r) ⊂ Rn , there exists an s > 0 such that

               B(φ(x), s) ⊂ φ(B(x, r)) ⊂ B(φ(x), ks).             (1.2)

In dimensions n ≥ 2, quasisymmetric maps are the same as quasicon-
formal maps [Geh2, Thm 7.7].

game. In the first, (1.1) is replaced by the pair of inequalities:

                 |Ai | ≥ α|Bi | and |Bi+1 | ≥ β|Ai |.             (1.3)

If A has a winning strategy in the game defined by (1.3), we say E
is an (α, β)-strong winning set. Sets which are α-strong winning and
strong winning are defined analogously.
    It is straightforward to verify that:
  1. A strong winning set E ⊂ Rn is winning; in particular, H. dim(E) =
     n; and
  2. A countable intersection of α-strong winning sets is α-strong win-
     ning.
In addition, property (3) for winning sets can be strengthened to:

Theorem 1.2 Strong winning sets are preserved by quasisymmetric
homeomorphisms φ : Rn → Rn .
