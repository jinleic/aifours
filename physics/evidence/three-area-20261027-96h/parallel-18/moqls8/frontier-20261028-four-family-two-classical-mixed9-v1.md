## 1. Actual four-net and two line lemmas

The two classical mates define a four-net of rows, columns and their symbol classes. Q and R are ONBs on every net line: if two cells have the same classical symbol, orthogonality of the corresponding product vectors forces their Q and R rays separately orthogonal. Choose any actual Q net line as first row and independently normalize Q and R on it to basis rays e_j. Let H be Q's mixed cells. As in the cited ≥8 proof, every net line meeting H contains at least two points of H, and any two cells share at most one net line.

If a line has exactly two mixed Q rays, its six light singleton rays occupy six distinct first-row axes, so both mixed rays form an ONB of the remaining two-dimensional coordinate space. Their supports are the **same two axes**. If a line has exactly three mixed Q rays, they lie in the three-dimensional complement of its five light axes and at most one of the three has support size 2: distinct 2-subsets of three axes intersect in one coordinate and cannot be orthogonal, whereas two orthogonal rays with the same two support axes force the third ray to be the remaining singleton. Thus at least one of three mixed rays on such a line has support size 3.

## 2. Eight mixed cells force a single two-dimensional trade

Assume `|H|=8`. A line with k mixed cells has at least k+3 mixed cells in total, because each of the other three class-lines through any one of its mixed points needs a distinct mixed point outside that line. A line with k≥6 is impossible. If k=5, its three remaining mixed points must each connect to all five points on the line by one of only three other classes, impossible because two cells already on the first line cannot share a second line.

A line L with four mixed points X is also impossible. Its four outside mixed points form Y. Each x∈X needs a neighbor on each of the three other classes, hence at least 12 X–Y adjacencies in total. Each y∈Y can be adjacent to at most three points of X, one per other class, so there are exactly 12. The bipartite graph is 3-regular on 4+4 vertices, hence `K_(4,4)` minus a perfect matching and connected. Every crossing line has exactly two mixed points: each x already has exactly three outside neighbors on its three other class-lines. Their Q supports are equal two-sets by the line lemma; connectedness makes all eight supports the same two-set. Four Q rays on L cannot be orthonormal in two dimensions.

A line L with exactly three mixed points X is impossible as well. The five outside mixed points, in lines parallel to L, partition as 3+2 because singleton mixed lines and four-or-more mixed lines are impossible. On L choose a mixed ray x of support size 3. Every other class-line through x needs at least one outside mixed point, and cannot contain exactly two mixed points because that would force x to have support size 2. It cannot contain another X point or two points from either outside parallel line; so each of its three remaining lines must contain one point from each outside group. The outside group of size 2 cannot supply three distinct points to three distinct lines through x. Contradiction.

Therefore every net line meets H in 0 or 2 cells. Its mixed-cell graph is a simple 4-regular graph on eight vertices: every mixed point has exactly one mixed neighbor in each line class, and distinct classes cannot connect the same pair twice. Each connected component of a 4-regular graph has at least five vertices, so this graph is connected. Along every edge Q's two supports are the same two-set, so all eight Q rays lie in one common two-dimensional coordinate space `span{e_a,e_b}` with both coordinates nonzero.

Orthogonality of nonzero rays in C² alternates between a one-dimensional ray and its unique perpendicular ray. Thus the connected mixed-cell graph is bipartite; a 4-regular bipartite graph on eight vertices has four vertices on each side and is exactly `K_(4,4)`. All Q rays in one part are collinear, and the two parts carry perpendicular rays. This is an actual support/orthogonality conclusion, not a graph-only replacement for the physical vectors.

## 3. The fourth member's dimension contradiction

Consider R at the eight cells of H. If two H cells are in opposite parts of `K_(4,4)`, they share an actual net line, so R's two rays are orthogonal. If they are in the same part, the corresponding Q rays are collinear and hence have nonzero overlap; the actual Q⊗R pair-product ONB forces R's two rays orthogonal. Therefore the eight R rays on H are pairwise orthogonal nonzero vectors.

Every Q ray on H has nonzero overlaps with both first-row Q rays `e_a,e_b`. Comparing each Q⊗R product at H with the first-row products at columns a and b forces the corresponding R ray orthogonal to both first-row R rays `e_a,e_b`. Hence all eight orthogonal R rays lie in a subspace of dimension at most six, contradiction.

Thus eight mixed cells cannot occur in a four-family with two classical mates. The cited independent ≥8 bound then yields **≥9**. If both extra members Q,R are nonclassical, this gives at least 18 mixed-cell incidences and at least 18 units of total off-first-row support surplus across the two members; their mixed cells need not be disjoint. The upper per-cell surplus budget remains 3, so this is a strict structural improvement rather than a complete q2 exclusion.
