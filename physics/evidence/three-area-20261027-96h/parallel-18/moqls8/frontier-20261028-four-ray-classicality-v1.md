# Four-ray overlap rigidity for ordinary four-MOQLS(8)

**Status: WRITTEN candidate plus independently checked finite net witnesses; Hcat is an external, explicitly unproved catalogue premise. Independent mathematical review, source authentication and novelty review are pending.** The main result strictly extends the earlier all-real-flat subclass to complex non-real-flat QLS.

Define a QLS(8) to have the **four-ray overlap property** if, for every two of its 64 cell rays, their squared inner-product modulus lies in `{0, 1/4, 1}`. This is a ray property, independent of cell phases and global output unitaries.

1. **Unconditional written candidate:** in any actual ordinary four-MOQLS(8), at most one member can be both nonclassical and four-ray. The other members need not have this property.
2. **Conditional candidate, Hcat:** if an actual ordinary four-MOQLS(8) has three classical members and its fourth member is four-ray, then the fourth member is classical. Consequently, if all four members are four-ray, the whole family is classical.

Here **Hcat** asserts that every ordinary triple of order-8 MOLS is paratopic to one of the 39 actual triples in the two pinned local snapshots. Hcat and faithful correspondence of the snapshots to the external catalogue are not established by this work. Four-ray is a genuine restriction: these statements do not establish unrestricted four-family classicality, M(8), A10/A11, or existence/nonexistence outside the stated subclass.

## 1. Four-ray supports and two-member exclusion

Choose a common actual first grid row and independently identify the first-row ONB of each member with the coordinate basis e_0,...,e_7. For any member satisfying the four-ray property, expanding a cell ray in its first-row basis gives squared coordinate values only 0, 1/4 or 1. Since they sum to one, the cell is either **light**, equal to one basis ray, or **heavy**, supported on four coordinates with squared coefficient 1/4 on each. All first-row cells are light. This conclusion works for complex phases and requires no real-flat or affine-plane hypothesis.

At a nonfirst-row cell in column j, every member excludes coordinate j. If two members had nonzero coordinate k at that cell, their actual same-cell product would have nonzero overlap with their product at first-row cell (0,k), a contradiction. Thus all four members' supports there are pairwise disjoint subsets of the seven labels other than j. In particular, distinct four-ray members cannot both be heavy at one cell. On any ONB line of a four-ray member, a heavy ray uses four axes. No light ray can use an axis occupied by a heavy ray; each such axis receives 1/4 from each heavy occurrence, so any line containing a heavy ray has at least four heavy cells. Consequently, two distinct four-ray members cannot have heavy cells in the same column: each would need at least four disjoint heavy cells among its seven nonfirst-row cells.

A nonclassical four-ray member Q needs at least four heavy columns. If it has exactly four, call them C. Every heavy row has all four columns C heavy. In any ONB line containing exactly four heavy rays, their four supports coincide: each occupied axis must receive four 1/4 contributions and no light contribution. Each such row support avoids all four first-row column labels C, hence is exactly `S=C^c`, regardless of the heavy row. In each column C, all heavy rays are mutually orthogonal in the four-dimensional space S, so there are at most four heavy rows. The line lower bound gives at least four; therefore there are exactly four, and Q's heavy cells form a 4×4 board R×C.

At every cell of R×C, Q occupies S and all three mates exclude S and the column label in C. Their three nonzero disjoint supports must be the three remaining singleton axes of C. In each mate, the four entries of R×C along a row form an ONB of its C-space, so the other four entries in that row lie in its S-space. The same holds by columns, and completeness then forces its R^c×C^c entries into C. Thus any two mates have 32 actual pair-product vectors in their `C ⊗ C` 16-dimensional subspace, contradicting their pair-product ONB. Hence Q needs at least five heavy columns. Heavy-column sets of two four-ray members are disjoint, so two nonclassical members would need at least ten columns. This proves claim 1 with the two other members arbitrary.

## 2. The five-net heavy-forcing graph is connected for every net

Now suppose Q has three classical mates. After separate output unitaries these define ordinary 3-MOLS on the same board. Q is an ONB on every one of the five line classes: rows and columns by definition, and a fixed classical-symbol line because product orthogonality with that mate forces its Q rays pairwise orthogonal. Thus ANY two classes may become the row/column classes; the remaining three are new classical mates. The four-ray property is unchanged because it refers to the same ray set.

Choose any such orientation and normalize the three mate symbols by their first-row values, so all three first rows are `0,...,7`. Let `f_j=Q(0,j)`. For a nonfirst cell x=(i,j), the four distinct forbidden first-row labels are its column j and the three mate labels at x. Their complement `A_x` has four elements and contains every nonzero coordinate of Q(x). A heavy ray therefore has support exactly A_x. The distinctness follows from the five net classes being mutually orthogonal.

For a net line L and a first-row point f_t not on L, there are exactly four cells of L whose allowed sets contain t: the other four net lines through f_t meet L in four distinct points, leaving four. If L passes through f_t, none of its nonfirst cells allows t. If a cell x on L is heavy and t∈A_x, no light ray on L can use t. ONB completeness on t needs exactly four 1/4 contributions, so **all four** L-cells allowing t must be heavy.

Make a graph on the 56 nonfirst-row cells; join x,y when they lie on one net line and `A_x∩A_y` is nonempty. The preceding implication and symmetry make heavy/light status constant on each connected component. The graph is connected, without any catalogue assumption:

- Fix a column j. Its seven off-first-row allowed sets are four-subsets of the other seven labels. Every pair intersects, so all seven cells form a graph clique. Components therefore partition whole columns.
- If there were at least two components, pick columns j,k in different components and any nonfirst row i. Their allowed four-sets are disjoint, hence complementary, say S and S^c. Every column in j's component has allowed set S in row i; every column in k's component has S^c; no third component can avoid both. Since each allowed set excludes its own column label, the first component's columns are contained in S^c and the second's in S. Both have at most four columns and together have eight, so they are exactly S^c and S.
- Let C be the first component's four column labels. The same reasoning for each nonfirst row forces `A_(i,j)=C^c` for all j∈C. Thus each normalized mate value at every (i,j) with j∈C lies in C, including the first row. But a fixed mate's column j must contain all eight distinct symbols, impossible. Hence the graph is connected.

Therefore, if Q is nonclassical, at least one cell is heavy and **all 56 nonfirst-row cells are heavy** in any chosen orientation. If that orientation contains two cells on a common net line whose allowed sets meet in exactly one coordinate, their actual inner product is one nonzero term and cannot vanish. Such an orientation excludes nonclassical Q. This is a written, catalogue-free necessary criterion for any actual five-net; it does not rely on selected phases or real-flatness.

## 4. Four-ray strictly contains real-flat QLS

In a real-flat QLS, rephase each ray to its real sign vector. Its first row consists of mutually orthogonal sign vectors; any two differ in four positions and have the same parity. Every other cell is orthogonal to the first-row cell in its column, so it has that same parity as well. Thus any two cell sign vectors have even Hamming distance and their squared overlap is 0, 1/4 or 1. Hence every real-flat QLS is four-ray.

The converse fails already for a **standalone QLS**, not claimed to have any mates. Label rows by `(a,b)` and columns by `(c,d)` with a,c∈{0,1}, b,d∈{0,1,2,3}; split C^8 into two four-dimensional blocks. Cell `((a,b),(c,d))` lies in block `a XOR c`, and uses basis index `b XOR d`. Use the ordinary coordinate basis in the three quadrants except `(a,c)=(1,1)`, where use columns of the complex Fourier matrix `F_4/2`, `F_4[r,k]=i^(rk)`. Every row and column combines two orthonormal four-bases in orthogonal blocks. Squared overlaps are exactly 0, 1/4 or 1.

Choose standard rays e_0,e_1 and Fourier rays f_0,f_1 in block 0. The phase- and unitary-invariant cycle `⟨e_0,f_0⟩⟨f_0,e_1⟩⟨e_1,f_1⟩⟨f_1,e_0⟩` equals `i/16`. If all rays could be made real in one frame by independent phases, this cycle would be real, contradiction. Therefore this QLS cannot be real-flat in any frame.
