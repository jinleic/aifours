    46	\tag{5}
    47	\]
    48	If the determinant vanished, irrationality of \(\sqrt2\) would force both integer coefficients to vanish. But
    49	\[
    50	(ae-bd)^2+(ad+be)^2=(a^2+b^2)(d^2+e^2)>0,
    51	\tag{6}
    52	\]
    53	a contradiction. Therefore the two lattices have no common nonzero rational line direction. In particular, they have no common nonzero point. This includes vertical directions; no division by a slope denominator is used.
    54	
    55	Also \(Q^TQ=I\), so both copies have exactly the same integer squared-norm shells and covolume one. Each finite disk contains finitely many labels. Every label eventually appears, and only finitely many lie before any fixed norm, proving the enumeration assertions in Theorem 1.
    56	
    57	## 3. Established ingredient: a union of two grids is a dense forest
    58	
    59	Finite-grid forest constructions and their irrationality characterization are established in Adiceam–Solomon–Weiss (2022) and Shirandami (2023), Theorem 1.1. Here is a self-contained sufficient argument for (1), so no qualitative-to-uniform gap is delegated to a citation.
    60	
    61	### 3.1 Irrational directions give dense positive-time torus lines
    62	
    63	For \(v\in\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\) not parallel to an integer vector, the positive-time line \(a+tv\pmod{\mathbb Z^2}\), \(t\ge0\), is dense in \(\mathbb T^2\). Indeed, \(v_1\ne0\) and \(v_2/v_1\) is irrational. To hit a prescribed first coordinate modulo one, take \(t=(x-a_1+m)/v_1\) with integers \(m\) of the sign making \(t\to+\infty\). The second coordinates are an irrational circle rotation and are dense. Density of positive multiples of an irrational number follows from the pigeonhole principle: arbitrarily small nonzero fractional steps exist, and their forward multiples form arbitrarily fine nets of the circle. Hence every nonempty torus ball is hit at some finite nonnegative time, from every initial phase.
    64	
    65	By §2, for every unit direction \(v\), at least one of \(v\) and \(Q^{-1}v\) is irrational in this sense. We do **not** claim that the combined flow is dense in a four-dimensional torus; density in one of its two factors suffices.
    66	
    67	### 3.2 Compactness makes the hitting time uniform in position and direction
    68	
    69	Fix \(\delta>0\). On the compact parameter space
    70	\[
    71	K=S^1\times\mathbb T^2\times\mathbb T^2
    72	\]
    73	let, for each real \(t\ge0\),
    74	\[
    75	U_t=\{(v,a,b):\min(d_{\mathbb T^2}(a+tv,0),
    76	 d_{\mathbb T^2}(b+tQ^{-1}v,0))<\delta\}.
    77	\tag{7}
    78	\]
    79	Each \(U_t\) is open because the torus distance is continuous. Section 3.1 says \(\bigcup_{t\ge0}U_t=K\). A finite subcover exists. Thus some finite integer \(H_G(\delta)\ge1\) bounds all the selected times.
    80	
    81	For any physical starting point \(x\), substitute \(a=[x]\), \(b=[Q^{-1}x]\). A hit in either factor means that \(x+tv\) is within **strict distance \(\delta\)** of a point of \(G\), since \(Q\) is orthogonal. Hence every length-\(H_G(\delta)\) segment is \(\delta\)-close to \(G\), uniformly over all starts and directions. One may define \(H_G(\delta)\) as the least positive integer with this universal property; it is finite and nonincreasing in \(\delta\).
    82	
    83	The density of \(G\) is finite by lattice counting. Scaling preserves the visibility property, and
    84	\[
    85	H_{cG}(\delta)=cH_G(\delta/c)
    86	\tag{8}
    87	\]
    88	is a finite nonincreasing segment-visibility function for \(cG\). Deletion of the origin is harmless by the finite-core argument in §5, rather than by ignoring that point.
    89	
    90	## 4. Classical circle discrepancy gives a uniformly vanishing radial correction
    91	
    92	### 4.1 The required error estimate, including real radii
    93	
    94	Let \(N_0(R)=\#(\mathbb Z^2\cap\overline B_R)\). We use the classical bound
    95	\[
    96	N_0(R)=\pi R^2+O(R^{2/3})\qquad(R\ge2\text{ real}).
    97	\tag{9}
    98	\]
    99	It is the planar positive-curvature estimate stated in Guo, *Rev. Mat. Iberoam.* 31 (2015), p.411, DOI 10.4171/rmi/839; **not** Guo's stronger almost-every-rotation theorem. To state all assumptions explicitly, a short standard proof follows.
   100	
   101	Choose a nonnegative smooth function \(\phi\), supported in the open unit disk, with integral one. Put \(\phi_h(x)=h^{-2}\phi(x/h)\). For \(0<h\le1\), the smooth inner/outer functions
   102	\[
   103	f_-=1_{B_{R-h}}*\phi_h,\qquad f_+=1_{B_{R+h}}*\phi_h
   104	\]
   105	satisfy \(f_-\le1_{\overline B_R}\le f_+\), also at the disk boundary. Their Fourier transforms, using \(e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\), satisfy for nonzero integer \(\ell\)
   106	\[
   107	\widehat{1_{B_R}}(\ell)=\frac{R}{\|\ell\|}J_1(2\pi R\|\ell\|),\qquad
   108	|\widehat{1_{B_R}}(\ell)|\ll R^{1/2}\|\ell\|^{-3/2}.
   109	\tag{10}
   110	\]
   111	The formula is the disk-slicing/Bessel integral and the bound is the fixed-order estimate \(J_1(x)=O(x^{-1/2})\) for \(x\ge1\); see DLMF [10.9.4](https://dlmf.nist.gov/10.9.E4) and [10.17.3](https://dlmf.nist.gov/10.17.E3).
   112	
   113	Poisson summation applies to the smooth compactly supported \(f_\pm\), not directly to the discontinuous disk indicator. Since \(\widehat\phi(h\ell)\ll_M(1+h\|\ell\|)^{-M}\) for any fixed \(M>2\), and the number of lattice vectors in a unit-width annulus of radius \(j\) is \(O(j)\), the nonzero Fourier contribution is
   114	\[
   115	\ll R^{1/2}\sum_{j\ge1}j^{-1/2}(1+hj)^{-M}
   116	\ll R^{1/2}h^{-1/2}.
   117	\]
   118	The zero-frequency volume difference is \(O(Rh)\), so the sandwich gives
   119	\[
   120	|N_0(R)-\pi R^2|\ll Rh+R^{1/2}h^{-1/2}.
   121	\]
   122	Taking \(h=R^{-1/3}\) proves (9), uniformly for all real \(R\ge2\). The lattice-annulus estimate follows by comparing the disjoint unit squares centered at lattice points with an annulus enlarged by a fixed width. No thin discrete shell is replaced by its area.
   123	
   124	### 4.2 Strict counts, ties, and the normalization constant
   125	
   126	Since the only overlap of the two lattices is the origin,
   127	\[
   128	N_F(R)=2(N_0(R)-1)=2\pi R^2+O(R^{2/3}).
   129	\tag{11}
   130	\]
   131	For \(r>2\), taking real \(R\uparrow r\) gives the same estimate for the strict count \(N_{F,<}(r)\). Local finiteness ensures that this left limit is the strict count. The finitely many radii \(1\le r\le2\) can be absorbed by increasing the absolute constant. Thus both count estimates hold for every shell radius \(r\ge1\).
   132	
   133	For every possible rank in a shell,
   134	\[
   135	N_{F,<}(r)+1\le k\le N_F(r),
   136	\qquad |k-2\pi r^2|\le C r^{2/3}
   137	\tag{12}
   138	\]
   139	for a single constant \(C\). This controls the **entire tie**, not just its last element. Now
   140	\[
   141	\|s_k-cf_k\|=|\sqrt{k}-\sqrt{2\pi}\,r_k|
   142	=\frac{|k-2\pi r_k^2|}{\sqrt{k}+\sqrt{2\pi}\,r_k}
   143	\le\frac{C}{\sqrt{2\pi}}r_k^{-1/3}.
   144	\tag{13}
   145	\]
   146	This proves (4). The scale is \(\sqrt{2\pi}\), not \(\sqrt\pi\): the base set has two covolume-one grids. Merely knowing \(N_F(R)\sim2\pi R^2\), or an \(O(R)\) error, would not imply a vanishing displacement.
   147	
   148	## 5. Vanishing-displacement stability, with the finite core handled
   149	
   150	We prove exactly the stability needed, without requiring uniform separation.
   151	
   152	**Lemma.** Let \(A\subset\mathbb R^2\) have a finite segment-visibility function \(H_A(\delta)\), nonincreasing in \(\delta\). Suppose a map \(p\mapsto\psi(p)\in B\) is defined for all \(p\in A\) outside a fixed ball, and
   153	\[
   154	\sup_{p\in A,\ \|p\|>R}\|\psi(p)-p\|\longrightarrow0
   155	\quad(R\to\infty).
   156	\tag{14}
   157	\]
   158	Then \(B\) has uniform segment visibility. If \(B\) is locally finite and of finite density, it is a dense forest. Additional points of \(B\) and arbitrary changes inside the finite core cannot harm this conclusion.
   159	
   160	**Proof.** Given \(\eta>0\), take \(\delta=\min(\eta/4,1/4)\). Choose an integer \(R(\delta)\ge1\) so that the map is defined and its error is strictly less than \(\delta\) for all \(\|p\|>R(\delta)\). These radii may be chosen nonincreasing in \(\delta\), by choosing the least admissible integer above the fixed domain threshold. Set
   161	\[
   162	L_B(\eta)=2H_A(\delta)+2R(\delta)+4.
   163	\tag{15}
   164	\]
   165	A segment of this length spends at most \(2(R(\delta)+1)\) of its length inside the closed ball \(\overline B_{R(\delta)+1}\). Its outside portion has at most two components and total length at least \(2H_A(\delta)+2\). Therefore one outside component contains a **closed** subsegment of length \(H_A(\delta)\), entirely and strictly outside that ball; the extra unit of length supplies room to avoid boundary endpoints.
   166	
   167	Visibility of \(A\) supplies a point \(p\in A\) within strict distance \(\delta\) of this subsegment. Its norm is greater than \(R(\delta)+1-\delta>R(\delta)\), so it is not in the exceptional core and the displacement estimate applies. Its image is within distance \(<2\delta\le\eta/2<\eta\) of the original segment. The length (15) is finite, independent of start and direction, and nonincreasing in \(\eta\). This proves the lemma. It also proves stability under deletion of finitely many points by using the identity map outside a ball containing them. ∎
   168	
   169	Apply the lemma with \(A=cG\), \(B=S\), and \(\psi(cf_k)=s_k\) on all nonzero points. The omitted origin lies inside the core. Equation (13) proves (14) uniformly outside a radius, not just along a selected subsequence. Section 3 supplies \(H_A\). Exact radii in (3) give local finiteness and finite density of \(S\), so it is a dense forest.
   170	
   171	## 6. Match Tevet's open-rectangle and decreasing-function definition
   172	
   173	Let \(L_S\) be (15) for this construction. For \(\varepsilon>0\), put
   174	\[
   175	W(\varepsilon)=L_S(\min(\varepsilon,1)/4)+1.
   176	\tag{16}
   177	\]
   178	This is finite and nonincreasing on the whole positive half-line. In any open rectangle of width \(\varepsilon\) and length \(W(\varepsilon)\), take the centerline segment obtained by removing length \(1/2\) at each longitudinal end. It has length \(L_S(\min(\varepsilon,1)/4)\). The preceding lemma supplies an \(S\)-point at distance strictly less than \(\min(\varepsilon,1)/4\) from this inner segment. Its transverse distance is strictly less than \(\varepsilon/2\), and its longitudinal overshoot is less than \(1/4\), smaller than the end padding. The point is therefore **strictly inside** the rectangle. This addresses endpoints and open boundaries, rather than identifying a segment's tubular neighborhood with a rectangle without padding.
   179	
   180	Finally, distinct exact radii make all \(s_k\) distinct, and
   181	\[
   182	\#(S\cap\overline B_R)=\lfloor R^2\rfloor,
   183	\qquad\lim_{R\to\infty}\#(S\cap\overline B_R)/R^2=1.
   184	\tag{17}
   185	\]
   186	For open balls only the possible boundary point changes. Thus the finite-density condition in Tevet's Eq. (1) is satisfied. Equations (3), (16), and (17) prove every clause of the stated theorem. **The proof is universal; it does not depend on the finite smoke computations.**

    62	   The goal of this paper is to study distribution properties of spiral sets from a comple-
    63	mentary standpoint; namely, from that of so–called visibility problems in discrete geometry
    64	which quantify in suitable senses the density of a point set. More precisely, the goal is to
    65	establish necessary and sufficient conditions for a spiral set to become arbitrarily close to
    66	line segments, provided they are long enough. This can be formalised in several distinct

    69	   A first possible formalisation is motivated by Pólya’s classical orchard’s problem stated
    70	in [12, Problem 239] : “how thick must the trunks of the trees in a regularly spaced cir-
    71	cular orchard grow if they are to block completely the view from the center?”. Assume
    72	that the observer stands at the origin, that the centres of the trees are located at the
    73	nonzero points of the lattice Z2 and that they have Euclidean norms at most Q > 0. Pólya
    74	(ibid.), based on a method due to Speiser, and then Honsberger [10], based on Mikowski’s
    75	Convex Body Theorem, showed that it is enough for the trees to have radius 1/Q to block
    76	any ray emerging from the origin. Slightly sharper estimates are also obtained by Allen [7].

    53	close to the bound given by (1.1). We mention four papers which are important

    58	considered in [6] is not uniformly discrete, and the first proof of the existence

    77	   The explicit sets we will consider involve periodic and almost periodic sets,
    78	and their finite unions. Note that a lattice is clearly uniformly discrete but
    79	is not a dense forest, and the same holds for a periodic set (a finite union
    80	of translates of one lattice). In fact a periodic set misses a neighborhood of
    81	some affine subspace of codimension one. On the other hand, as Peres showed,
    82	a union of finitely many periodic sets could be a dense forest. Such a finite
    83	union is clearly of finite density, and it is sometimes uniformly discrete (see
    84	Proposition 2.1). Another source of explicit constructions are cut-and-project
    85	sets or model sets, which are intensively studied in the literature on aperiodic

    22	Let B ⊂ Rd (d ⩾ 2) be a compact convex domain, which contains the origin in its
